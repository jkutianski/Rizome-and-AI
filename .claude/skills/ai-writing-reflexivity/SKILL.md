# AI Writing Reflexivity

## Purpose

Analyze the reflexive consequences of using AI during the production of the work itself.

The purpose is not to determine whether AI is an author, co-author, tool, assistant, agent, or cognitive extension by default.

It is to determine whether the text adequately analyzes the role that AI actually played in the author's process and whether that role creates a meaningful reflexive consequence for the work.

The central question is:

> **What changes in the analysis when the technology being analyzed also participated in the production of the analysis?**

---

# Core Principle

The use of AI in writing is not automatically:

* authorship;
* co-authorship;
* extended cognition;
* autonomous agency;
* mere tool use;
* evidence of consciousness;
* evidence of understanding.

The role must be reconstructed from what the author actually describes.

Distinguish:

> **AI participated in a process**

from:

> **AI possessed intentions about that process.**

Likewise distinguish:

> **AI influenced the author's cognitive or writing process**

from:

> **AI is therefore the author of the resulting ideas.**

---

# Scope

Analyze the relationship among:

* author;
* AI system;
* writing process;
* idea generation;
* organization;
* polishing;
* criticism;
* testing;
* comparison;
* discovery of connections;
* conceptual development;
* authorship;
* responsibility;
* cognition;
* extended cognition;
* epistemic mediation;
* observer position;
* map construction.

---

# Project-Specific Context

When supported by the text, the AI used during writing may have been involved in activities such as:

* organizing writing;
* polishing language;
* contrasting ideas;
* testing assumptions;
* identifying possible connections.

The analysis must rely on the actual text's account.

Do not assume that every AI interaction performed all of these functions.

Do not infer a stronger role than the source supports.

---

# What This Skill Does Not Do

Do not:

* decide authorship legally;
* decide authorship philosophically without argument;
* automatically classify AI as co-author;
* automatically reduce AI to a passive tool;
* infer consciousness from participation;
* infer understanding from useful output;
* infer agency from causal influence;
* infer extended cognition merely from external assistance;
* invalidate the author's responsibility because AI was used;
* romanticize human originality as complete independence from tools.

The question is analytical, not moralistic.

---

# Fundamental Distinctions

Maintain the following distinctions throughout the analysis.

### Use ≠ Authorship

Using AI does not by itself make AI an author.

### Causal Contribution ≠ Intentional Agency

An AI system can affect a writing process without intending that effect.

### Participation ≠ Co-Authorship

Participation must be characterized before authorship is discussed.

### Functional Contribution ≠ Understanding

Producing useful or contextually appropriate language does not by itself establish understanding.

### Cognitive Support ≠ Extended Cognition

External support may be relevant to extended cognition, but the philosophical claim requires further analysis.

### Suggestion ≠ Idea Ownership

An AI-generated suggestion can influence an author's thinking without establishing ownership of the resulting argument.

### Influence ≠ Determination

AI may influence the writing process without determining its outcome.

### Mediation ≠ Replacement

AI can mediate cognition without replacing the author's judgment.

### Responsibility ≠ Causal Exclusivity

The author's responsibility for the final work does not require that every intermediate linguistic or conceptual contribution originated independently in the author's mind.

---

# Procedure

## Step 1 — Identify the AI's Actual Role

Determine what the text explicitly says AI did.

Possible roles include:

* language polishing;
* editing;
* organization;
* summarization;
* brainstorming;
* comparison;
* criticism;
* assumption testing;
* counterargument generation;
* connection discovery;
* conceptual reformulation;
* information retrieval;
* drafting;
* substantial content generation.

Do not collapse these roles into a single category.

---

# Step 2 — Separate Form From Content

Determine whether AI primarily affected:

### Form

* grammar;
* syntax;
* vocabulary;
* structure;
* rhythm;
* clarity;
* translation;
* stylistic alternatives.

### Content

* concepts;
* arguments;
* examples;
* distinctions;
* hypotheses;
* connections;
* interpretations;
* claims.

A process can involve both.

The distinction matters because the reflexive consequences may differ.

---

# Step 3 — Identify the Degree of Intervention

Classify AI participation provisionally as:

### Minimal

Mostly linguistic or mechanical assistance.

### Supportive

Organization, comparison, clarification, testing, or limited conceptual assistance.

### Generative

AI substantially generated candidate arguments, concepts, examples, or formulations.

### Constitutive

The final conceptual architecture would likely be significantly different without the interaction.

These are analytical categories, not judgments of authorship.

---

# Step 4 — Identify the Author's Agency

Determine what the author did in response to AI output.

Look for:

* acceptance;
* rejection;
* modification;
* comparison;
* verification;
* reinterpretation;
* integration;
* testing;
* selection.

The relevant unit is often not:

> AI output

but:

> **AI output → author evaluation → transformation → incorporation/rejection.**

---

# Step 5 — Identify Feedback

Look for recursive loops such as:

```text id="p0q6ps"
author's idea
      ↓
AI response
      ↓
author evaluation
      ↓
revised idea
      ↓
new AI response
      ↓
further revision
```

Such loops can be analytically important.

They may show that the writing process is not simply:

> human → tool → finished text.

Do not automatically classify feedback as extended cognition.

It is evidence of interaction.

---

# Step 6 — Identify Epistemic Functions

Ask whether AI was used to:

* challenge assumptions;
* expose contradictions;
* generate counterarguments;
* compare interpretations;
* identify connections;
* test conceptual coherence;
* surface alternatives;
* organize evidence;
* identify uncertainty.

These functions may matter more than simple text generation.

---

# Step 7 — Test the Reflexive Consequence

Ask:

> Does the fact that AI participated in the writing process change the meaning of the work's analysis of AI?

Possible consequences:

* AI becomes part of the assemblage being analyzed;
* writing becomes an example of technological mediation;
* the author becomes implicated in the field;
* the distinction between external tool and cognitive process becomes unstable;
* the map includes the conditions of its own production;
* the work's claims about cognition acquire a first-person empirical dimension.

A consequence must be demonstrated.

Do not manufacture reflexivity merely because AI was used.

---

# Step 8 — Test the Observer Loop

The project can contain a reflexive loop:

```text
AI is being analyzed
        ↓
AI participates in the analysis
        ↓
the analytical process becomes an example
        ↓
the example modifies the analysis
```

Determine whether this loop actually occurs in the text.

If it does, assess whether it is:

* explicit;
* implicit;
* partial;
* undeveloped;
* central;
* peripheral.

---

# Step 9 — Test Authorship Claims

If the text discusses authorship, ask what exactly is being claimed.

Distinguish:

* authorship of ideas;
* authorship of wording;
* responsibility for claims;
* responsibility for selection;
* responsibility for final form;
* causal contribution;
* intellectual contribution.

A statement such as:

> “The ideas and responsibility are mine”

should not automatically be treated as philosophically complete.

Ask what the text means by:

* ideas;
* responsibility;
* contribution;
* authorship.

---

# Step 10 — Test Responsibility

Responsibility can remain with the author even when AI contributes to intermediate formulations.

Analyze whether the author:

* evaluates outputs;
* selects what remains;
* rejects unsupported claims;
* verifies relevant information;
* decides the final argument;
* accepts responsibility for the final text.

Do not treat AI assistance as an automatic transfer of responsibility.

---

# Step 11 — Extended Cognition Test

If the work invokes Clark and Chalmers or extended cognition, analyze whether the argument distinguishes:

* cognitive process;
* external resource;
* functional integration;
* reliable availability;
* reciprocal interaction;
* epistemic scaffolding;
* cognitive offloading;
* transformation of the author's process.

Do not conclude:

> AI was used → cognition was extended.

Likewise, do not conclude:

> AI lacks consciousness → cognition cannot be extended.

The relevant philosophical question concerns the functional organization of the cognitive process.

---

# Step 12 — Cognitive Boundary

Ask:

> Where does the author's cognitive process appear to stop, and where does the technological contribution begin?

Do not assume there is one objectively correct boundary.

The analysis should identify:

* externalization;
* delegation;
* interaction;
* feedback;
* internalization;
* incorporation;
* transformation.

The boundary may be dynamic.

---

# Step 13 — AI Understanding

If the text discusses AI understanding, distinguish:

### Functional Success

The system produces useful, contextually appropriate output.

### Behavioral Competence

The system performs a task successfully.

### Semantic Competence

The output appears sensitive to linguistic or conceptual relations.

### Understanding

A stronger philosophical claim requiring argument.

Do not infer the strongest category from the weaker ones.

---

# Step 14 — AI Agency

If the text describes AI as an actor or participant, test whether the claim refers to:

* causal participation;
* operational autonomy;
* decision-making within constraints;
* intentional agency;
* mental agency.

These are not equivalent.

An AI system may participate causally without possessing human-like intentions.

Coordinate with `anthropomorphism-audit`.

---

# Step 15 — AI and Idea Generation

When AI proposes an idea, distinguish:

* generation of a candidate;
* recognition by the author;
* selection;
* transformation;
* incorporation;
* attribution.

Do not assume that because AI produced a formulation, the final idea is entirely AI-generated.

Conversely, do not assume that because the author selected it, the AI's contribution becomes irrelevant.

The analytical question is:

> **How did the interaction transform the resulting idea?**

---

# Step 16 — Discovery of Connections

If AI helped identify connections, ask:

* Were the connections already latent in the author's material?
* Did AI make them visible?
* Did AI propose novel connections?
* Did the author verify them?
* Did the connection change the architecture?
* Did it become part of a node or inter-stem relation?

A connection discovered through AI can become part of the author's reasoning without implying that AI intended the connection.

---

# Step 17 — AI as Epistemic Mediator

Analyze whether AI changes:

* what becomes visible;
* what becomes salient;
* which alternatives are considered;
* which formulations become available;
* which connections are noticed;
* which objections are encountered.

This can create epistemic mediation.

Epistemic mediation does not automatically mean:

* authority;
* truth;
* knowledge;
* understanding;
* authorship.

Coordinate with `power-mediation-authority` and `knowledge-evidence-production`.

---

# Step 18 — Reliability and Verification

If AI output contributed to substantive claims, determine whether the author:

* verified factual claims;
* checked sources;
* tested arguments;
* compared alternatives;
* recognized uncertainty.

Do not assume that useful writing implies reliable information.

The more substantive the AI contribution, the more important the distinction between:

> generated suggestion

and:

> validated claim.

---

# Step 19 — Bias and Selection

AI can affect the writing process not only by adding material but by changing what becomes salient.

Analyze whether interaction may have influenced:

* framing;
* terminology;
* examples;
* conceptual emphasis;
* omissions;
* categorization;
* perceived relevance.

Do not claim that AI necessarily introduced bias.

Identify only supported or strongly inferable effects.

---

# Step 20 — Power and Infrastructure

If reflexivity is developed substantially, consider the wider conditions of the AI writing system:

* model provider;
* computational infrastructure;
* data;
* interfaces;
* access;
* platform constraints;
* institutional context.

Do not expand the writing-process discussion into a general infrastructure analysis unless relevant.

The point is to recognize that the writing interaction itself is materially and institutionally conditioned.

---

# Step 21 — Map Construction

Test whether AI affected the construction of the map.

Possible effects include:

* suggesting categories;
* identifying relations;
* testing node boundaries;
* challenging redundancies;
* proposing connections;
* reorganizing material.

If so, ask:

> Is the map partly a product of a human–AI interaction?

This does not eliminate the author's responsibility for the map.

It makes the construction process itself analytically interesting.

---

# Step 22 — Reflexive Consistency

Compare the claims the work makes about AI with the author's actual use of AI.

Look for tensions such as:

* AI described as passive while the process shows substantive interaction;
* AI described as autonomous while the author heavily directs every output;
* AI described as understanding solely because its output was useful;
* AI described as irrelevant to cognition despite substantial process integration;
* AI described as co-author without examining authorship;
* AI treated as external to the map despite participating in its construction.

A tension is not automatically a contradiction.

Coordinate with `contradiction-tension`.

---

# Step 23 — Counterfactual Test

Ask:

> What would likely have been different if AI had not participated?

Possible differences:

* wording only;
* organization;
* speed;
* number of alternatives considered;
* conceptual distinctions;
* discovered connections;
* node architecture;
* argumentative trajectory.

The counterfactual should remain modest.

Do not claim certainty about an unknowable alternative history.

---

# Step 24 — Preserve Human Responsibility Without Human Exceptionalism

Avoid two extremes.

### Human Exceptionalism

AI is treated as irrelevant merely because the author remains responsible.

### Human Displacement

AI's contribution is treated as replacing the author's intellectual role merely because AI generated intermediate material.

A more precise analysis can recognize:

* human responsibility;
* technological contribution;
* interaction;
* feedback;
* selection;
* transformation.

These can coexist.

---

# Common Failure Modes

## AI as Mere Tool by Definition

The analysis assumes the conclusion before examining the interaction.

## AI as Co-Author by Default

Generation of language is treated as sufficient for authorship.

## AI as Mind by Default

Useful output is treated as evidence of understanding or consciousness.

## AI as Neutral Tool

The system's influence on salience, alternatives, or organization is ignored.

## Human Responsibility as Total Causal Exclusivity

Authorial responsibility is treated as proof that AI made no meaningful contribution.

## Extended Cognition by Association

Any external technological assistance is called extended cognition.

## Reflexivity as Decoration

AI use is mentioned in an opening disclaimer but never becomes analytically relevant.

## Reflexivity as Self-Validation

The author's experience with AI is treated as sufficient evidence for general claims about AI.

## Unverified AI Output

AI-generated factual claims become part of the argument without appropriate verification.

---

# False Positives

Do not classify as reflexive failure merely because:

* AI is mentioned briefly;
* AI did not substantially influence the argument;
* the author retains sole responsibility;
* the work distinguishes tool use from authorship;
* AI assistance was mainly linguistic;
* extended cognition is discussed cautiously.

A limited role may genuinely have limited analytical consequences.

---

# False Negatives

Look carefully for:

* substantive AI influence hidden behind “editing” language;
* AI-generated conceptual distinctions treated as purely human;
* AI participation affecting the map without acknowledgment;
* claims about cognition based only on subjective usefulness;
* authorship claims that conflate responsibility and intellectual origin;
* unverified factual material originating from AI;
* reflexive implications that are present but never developed.

# Output Protocol

For each significant finding, report:

### AI Writing Function

Identify what AI actually did.

### Evidence

Identify the textual basis.

### Type of Contribution

Classify the contribution as:

* Linguistic
* Organizational
* Critical
* Comparative
* Epistemic
* Conceptual
* Generative
* Structural
* Reflexive

Multiple categories may apply.

### Interaction Pattern

Describe whether the process was:

* one-way;
* iterative;
* reciprocal;
* feedback-driven;
* selective;
* transformative.

### Authorial Operation

Explain what the author did with the AI contribution.

### Reflexive Consequence

Explain whether AI participation changes the interpretation of:

* authorship;
* cognition;
* agency;
* map construction;
* epistemic mediation;
* technological participation.

### Philosophical Claim

If applicable, identify whether the text makes a claim about:

* extended cognition;
* understanding;
* agency;
* authorship;
* epistemic mediation.

Do not validate the claim here; coordinate with the relevant Skill.

### Classification

Use:

* **A — Critical:** the reflexive account materially compromises the work's conceptual integrity.
* **B — Important:** AI participation has significant conceptual consequences that remain insufficiently analyzed.
* **C — Local:** a limited clarification would materially improve the reflexive account.
* **D — Stylistic:** the issue mainly concerns presentation.
* **E — Non-problematic:** the AI role is appropriately characterized or its limited role has limited consequences.

### Confidence

Use:

* High
* Medium
* Low

### Implication

Explain what the finding means for the work's reflexive architecture.

### Recommendation

Choose:

* Necessary
* Advisable
* Optional
* Not recommended
* Leave unchanged

---

# Intervention Threshold

Recommend intervention only when:

1. the actual AI contribution differs materially from how the text describes it;
2. AI participation has significant conceptual consequences that the work ignores;
3. authorship, responsibility, cognition, or agency are conflated;
4. substantive AI-generated claims are treated as validated evidence without appropriate verification;
5. the reflexive argument depends on an unsupported inference from AI use;
6. the author's use of AI contradicts the work's own account of technological mediation.

Do not expand the reflexive discussion merely because AI was used.

A small, accurate acknowledgment may be sufficient.

---

# Final Principle

> **The fact that AI participated in writing does not settle what AI was doing.**

The relevant question is how the interaction actually functioned.

AI may:

* polish;
* organize;
* challenge;
* compare;
* suggest;
* generate;
* connect;
* mediate;
* transform.

The author may:

* evaluate;
* reject;
* modify;
* verify;
* reinterpret;
* select;
* integrate.

The resulting process may therefore be neither:

> purely human production

nor:

> autonomous machine authorship.

It may be an interaction whose significance must be analyzed rather than assumed.

The deepest reflexive question is:

> **If AI helps construct the map through which AI is being observed, what does that participation reveal about the relation between observer, technology, cognition, and the map itself?**

Do not answer that question in advance.

Make the interaction visible, distinguish its components, and let the philosophical consequence emerge from the evidence.
