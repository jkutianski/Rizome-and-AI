# Power, Mediation, Relevance, and Authority

## Purpose

Analyze how AI participates in processes through which information is:

* mediated;
* selected;
* filtered;
* organized;
* ranked;
* made visible or invisible;
* treated as relevant;
* attributed authority;
* incorporated into decisions;
* connected to relations of power.

This Skill is designed primarily for the conceptual territory represented by **Node D: AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority**.

Its central transformation is:

**information mediation → relevance and authority**

The analysis must preserve the distinctions among:

* mediation;
* selection;
* visibility;
* relevance;
* authority;
* legitimacy;
* truth;
* auditability;
* power.

Do not collapse these into a single category such as "truth production."

---

# 1. Governing Principle

AI systems can participate in processes that shape what becomes:

* available;
* visible;
* salient;
* relevant;
* credible;
* actionable;
* authoritative.

But participation in these processes does not automatically mean that AI:

* determines truth;
* possesses authority;
* exercises political power autonomously;
* replaces institutions;
* produces knowledge by itself;
* intentionally manipulates users.

The analytical task is to reconstruct the relations through which mediation becomes consequential.

---

# 2. Central Chain

A useful analytical sequence is:

**mediation → selection → visibility → relevance → authority → power**

This is a heuristic for identifying relations, not a universal linear causal chain.

The stages may:

* overlap;
* feed back into one another;
* operate in parallel;
* be institutionally mediated;
* be contested;
* be interrupted;
* vary across contexts.

Do not assume that every case contains every stage.

---

# 3. Mediation

Mediation concerns how an intermediary configuration affects the relation between:

* information;
* users;
* institutions;
* practices;
* decisions;
* other technologies.

Ask:

* What does the system mediate?
* Between whom or what?
* Through what mechanism?
* What becomes easier?
* What becomes harder?
* What is transformed?
* What is filtered?
* What is reordered?

Mediation is not necessarily neutral, but neither is it automatically manipulative.

---

# 4. Selection

Selection concerns which information, possibilities, signals, outputs, or interpretations receive differential treatment.

Selection may occur through:

* ranking;
* filtering;
* recommendation;
* retrieval;
* summarization;
* classification;
* prioritization;
* interface design;
* training data;
* system constraints;
* institutional rules.

The key question is:

> **What becomes more or less available for attention or action because of the selection process?**

---

# 5. Visibility

Visibility concerns what becomes perceptible, discoverable, prominent, or accessible within a particular informational environment.

AI may affect visibility through:

* search;
* recommendation;
* generation;
* summarization;
* classification;
* ranking;
* automated moderation;
* personalization.

Do not assume:

**visibility = truth**

or:

**invisibility = falsehood.**

Visibility is a condition of attention, not a guarantee of epistemic validity.

---

# 6. Relevance

Relevance concerns what is treated as worth:

* noticing;
* retrieving;
* considering;
* comparing;
* prioritizing;
* discussing;
* acting upon.

A system can influence relevance without determining truth.

For example:

> an AI system may rank one piece of information as more salient without establishing that it is objectively more true.

Therefore preserve:

**visibility ≠ relevance ≠ truth**

---

# 7. Authority

Authority concerns the capacity of a source, output, institution, procedure, or actor to be treated as deserving:

* trust;
* deference;
* consideration;
* acceptance;
* institutional uptake;
* decision-making weight.

Ask:

* Who attributes authority?
* Through what procedure?
* On what grounds?
* Is authority institutional, epistemic, technical, legal, social, or practical?
* Is the authority delegated to the system?
* Is the system merely perceived as authoritative?
* Does institutional embedding amplify its authority?

Do not say that AI "has authority" without identifying the relation through which authority is attributed.

---

# 8. Legitimacy

Legitimacy is distinct from authority.

A system may have practical authority without being legitimate.

Conversely, an institution may be legitimate while possessing limited practical influence.

Analyze:

* source of legitimacy;
* procedure;
* institutional recognition;
* accountability;
* consent;
* authorization;
* transparency;
* contestability.

Preserve:

**authority ≠ legitimacy**

---

# 9. Truth

Truth must remain analytically distinct.

Do not collapse:

* visibility into truth;
* relevance into truth;
* authority into truth;
* legitimacy into truth;
* institutional acceptance into truth.

A claim may be:

* highly visible but false;
* relevant but uncertain;
* authoritative but contested;
* legitimate but empirically mistaken;
* obscure but true.

If the text makes a claim about truth, analyze it separately.

---

# 10. Auditability

Auditability concerns whether decisions, outputs, classifications, rankings, or processes can be:

* inspected;
* reconstructed;
* challenged;
* traced;
* evaluated.

Ask:

* What can be audited?
* By whom?
* With what information?
* At what level?
* Can the process be reconstructed?
* Can errors be identified?
* Can decisions be contested?

Do not assume:

**explainability = auditability**

or:

**transparency = accountability.**

These relations require separate analysis.

---

# 11. Power

Power concerns differential capacity to:

* shape conditions;
* control resources;
* establish standards;
* determine access;
* influence decisions;
* impose constraints;
* define categories;
* distribute visibility;
* attribute authority.

Power is relational.

Ask:

> **Who can change the conditions under which others act, and through what relations?**

Do not equate power exclusively with intentional domination.

Power can operate structurally through:

* infrastructure;
* standards;
* dependencies;
* defaults;
* resource concentration;
* institutional arrangements;
* technical architectures.

---

# 12. AI as Mediator

Analyze AI as a participant in mediation without automatically treating it as an intentional agent.

Possible functions include:

* filtering;
* ranking;
* generating;
* summarizing;
* classifying;
* recommending;
* retrieving;
* translating;
* prioritizing;
* formatting;
* organizing.

The fact that AI performs one of these functions does not by itself establish:

* political agency;
* intentional manipulation;
* autonomous authority;
* consciousness.

Coordinate with:

`anthropomorphism-audit`.

---

# 13. Institutional Embedding

AI-mediated effects often depend on institutional embedding.

Analyze:

* organizations;
* governments;
* companies;
* schools;
* laboratories;
* courts;
* media systems;
* professional communities;
* regulatory structures.

Ask:

> **What institution incorporates, authorizes, constrains, interprets, or rejects the AI-mediated output?**

An AI output can become consequential because an institution gives it procedural weight.

Therefore distinguish:

**AI capability → institutional uptake → practical authority**

from:

**AI capability → automatic authority**

---

# 14. Delegated Authority

Authority may be delegated to AI systems.

Examples can include systems used for:

* screening;
* recommendation;
* ranking;
* risk assessment;
* decision support;
* content moderation;
* resource allocation.

Analyze:

* who delegates;
* what is delegated;
* what remains under human/institutional control;
* whether delegation is reversible;
* whether users understand the delegation;
* whether outputs can be contested.

Delegated authority is not identical to autonomous authority.

---

# 15. Perceived Authority

A system can acquire authority because people perceive it as:

* objective;
* neutral;
* technically sophisticated;
* knowledgeable;
* efficient;
* impartial.

Analyze the social mechanism producing that perception.

Do not infer actual epistemic authority merely from perceived authority.

Preserve:

**perceived authority ≠ justified authority**

---

# 16. Automation and Authority

Automation can redistribute decision weight.

But:

**automation ≠ authority**

and:

**automation ≠ power**

Analyze the institutional arrangement through which automation changes:

* discretion;
* responsibility;
* accountability;
* workflow;
* decision speed;
* visibility;
* contestability.

---

# 17. Ranking and Relevance

Ranking deserves special attention.

Ranking can alter:

* visibility;
* attention;
* perceived importance;
* retrieval probability;
* decision context.

But ranking does not automatically establish truth.

The analytical sequence may be:

**ranking → visibility → attention → relevance → potential authority**

rather than:

**ranking → truth**

The final step depends on social and institutional uptake.

---

# 18. Generation and Authority

Generative systems introduce a specific tension.

Generated output may appear:

* coherent;
* fluent;
* comprehensive;
* confident;
* technically sophisticated.

These characteristics can affect perceived authority.

But:

**fluency ≠ truth**

**coherence ≠ evidence**

**confidence ≠ certainty**

**functional performance ≠ understanding**

The relevant question is how these properties interact with human and institutional evaluation.

---

# 19. Relevance and Attention

Relevance is partly relational.

What becomes relevant depends on:

* goals;
* context;
* available alternatives;
* institutional criteria;
* user expectations;
* temporal constraints;
* ranking systems;
* cultural or professional norms.

AI can participate in this process without determining relevance universally.

Analyze the conditions under which relevance is assigned.

---

# 20. Authority and Evidence

Authority should not substitute for evidence.

Ask:

* What evidence supports the output?
* What evidence supports the attribution of authority?
* Is authority based on institutional status?
* Technical performance?
* Reputation?
* Procedural validation?
* User perception?

Preserve:

**authority ≠ evidence**

and:

**evidence ≠ authority.**

Coordinate with:

`knowledge-evidence-production`.

---

# 21. Authority and Attribution

Attribution concerns who or what receives credit or responsibility for:

* claims;
* decisions;
* classifications;
* recommendations;
* generated outputs.

AI-mediated authority raises questions such as:

* Who is responsible?
* Who is credited?
* Who is accountable?
* Who can challenge the output?
* Who controls the system?

Do not infer responsibility from causal participation alone.

---

# 22. Relevance and Power

The ability to shape relevance can become a form of power.

Analyze whether control over:

* ranking;
* retrieval;
* recommendation;
* visibility;
* categorization;

changes who or what receives attention.

Do not automatically label every act of selection as power.

Power requires a meaningful asymmetry in capacity or conditions.

---

# 23. Authority and Power

Authority can function as a mechanism of power.

But:

**authority ≠ power**

A source may be authoritative within one context without possessing broad power.

Conversely, an actor may possess significant power without epistemic authority.

Analyze the specific relation.

---

# 24. Legitimacy and Power

Power can influence what becomes legitimate.

But legitimacy should not be reduced to power.

Ask:

* What procedures authorize a decision?
* Who recognizes it?
* Who contests it?
* What institutional mechanisms sustain it?

Maintain analytical distinctions.

---

# 25. Mediation and Bias

Do not treat all mediation as bias.

A mediated system necessarily selects and transforms information.

The relevant question is:

> **Which patterns of selection produce systematic and consequential asymmetries?**

Analyze:

* systematicity;
* direction;
* affected groups or domains;
* institutional consequences;
* feedback;
* correction mechanisms.

---

# 26. Feedback Loops

Power and relevance can form feedback circuits.

For example:

**visibility → attention → uptake → more visibility**

or:

**institutional adoption → perceived authority → increased adoption**

Such loops can amplify existing concentrations.

Do not infer feedback merely from recurrence.

A feedback loop requires a return effect that changes subsequent conditions.

---

# 27. Concentration of Power

Analyze whether AI-mediated systems concentrate power through:

* infrastructure;
* data;
* compute;
* standards;
* platforms;
* expertise;
* capital;
* institutional access.

A distributed interface can conceal concentrated infrastructure.

Therefore distinguish:

**surface decentralization**

from:

**underlying concentration.**

---

# 28. Dependency

Dependency is especially important.

Ask:

* Who depends on whose infrastructure?
* Who depends on whose models?
* Who depends on whose standards?
* Who depends on whose data?
* Who can switch systems?
* Who bears the cost of switching?

Dependency can create power asymmetry without direct coercion.

---

# 29. Material Infrastructure

Power and authority should be connected to material conditions when relevant.

Consider:

* data centers;
* energy;
* compute;
* networks;
* chips;
* storage;
* logistics;
* labor;
* capital.

Material infrastructure can determine who can:

* operate;
* scale;
* access;
* audit;
* compete;
* govern.

Do not treat materiality as an ornamental addition.

---

# 30. Contingency and Contestability

Power relations are not necessarily permanent.

Analyze:

* regulation;
* resistance;
* institutional redesign;
* competing systems;
* open alternatives;
* technical changes;
* market changes;
* public contestation.

Ask:

> **What would have to change for this concentration of authority or power to weaken?**

This prevents deterministic interpretation.

---

# 31. Rhizomatic Perspective

A rhizomatic analysis does not imply:

* equal power;
* universal access;
* decentralized authority;
* absence of hierarchy.

Instead, it asks how power is distributed through relations.

The map should make visible:

* concentrations;
* dependencies;
* bottlenecks;
* crossings;
* feedback;
* asymmetries;
* alternative pathways.

Coordinate with:

`rhizomatic-cartography`.

---

# 32. Inter-Stem Relevance

Node D may qualify as an inter-stem node when mediation and authority relations cross assemblages and modify their conditions of functioning.

For example:

**AI-mediated information → altered relevance → institutional uptake → redistributed authority**

The decisive question is whether these relations:

1. cross assemblages;
2. form a concentration;
3. modify conditions;
4. produce a transformation.

Do not classify D as inter-stem simply because power appears in several domains.

Coordinate with:

`inter-stem-validation`.

---

# 33. Distinguishing Node D from Node E

Node D concerns:

**mediation → relevance → authority → power**

Node E concerns:

**production → evidence → attribution**

They can interact, but they should not be collapsed.

For example:

* E may ask whether an AI-generated claim has evidential support.
* D may ask why that claim becomes visible, relevant, authoritative, or consequential.

The same output can therefore participate in both nodes through different relations.

---

# 34. Distinguishing Node D from Node A

Node A concerns:

**observable capacities → attribution of mental capacities**

Node D concerns:

**mediation → relevance → authority → power**

Do not interpret institutional attribution of authority as evidence of AI consciousness or understanding.

Social attribution of authority and philosophical attribution of mentality are different operations.

---

# 35. Distinguishing Node D from Node C

Node C concerns:

**technical capacity → strategic capacity**

Node D concerns:

**mediation → relevance → authority → power**

They may intersect where infrastructure or technical capacity becomes a source of strategic power, but they remain analytically distinct.

---

# 36. Common Conceptual Collapses

Flag these when they occur:

**visibility = truth**

**relevance = truth**

**authority = truth**

**legitimacy = truth**

**evidence = authority**

**fluency = knowledge**

**automation = authority**

**AI output = institutional decision**

**technical capability = political power**

**selection = manipulation**

**mediation = bias**

**perceived authority = justified authority**

**causal influence = intentional manipulation**

Each collapse requires separate analysis.

---

# 37. Anthropomorphic Risk

Be especially careful with verbs such as:

* decides;
* knows;
* judges;
* manipulates;
* wants;
* prefers;
* controls;
* believes;
* understands.

These may be legitimate shorthand for system behavior, but they require contextual analysis.

Ask:

> Is the text describing functional behavior, causal influence, institutional attribution, or mental agency?

Do not treat ordinary active language as automatically anthropomorphic.

---

# 38. Evidence Requirements

For claims about mediation, identify:

* system function;
* process;
* relation;
* effect.

For claims about authority, identify:

* attribution;
* procedure;
* institutional uptake;
* evidence.

For claims about power, identify:

* asymmetry;
* capacity;
* dependency;
* consequence.

For claims about truth, identify:

* epistemic criteria;
* evidence;
* verification.

For claims about legitimacy, identify:

* authorization;
* recognition;
* procedure;
* contestation.

---

# 39. Counterfactual Tests

### Remove AI

Would the same authority relation exist?

If yes, AI may be a mediator rather than the source of authority.

### Remove Institutional Uptake

Does the AI output still possess practical authority?

If no, authority depends on institutional embedding.

### Remove Visibility

Does relevance remain unchanged?

If no, visibility is part of the mechanism.

### Remove Ranking

Does the distribution of attention change?

If yes, ranking participates in relevance formation.

### Remove Infrastructure

Does the power relation remain possible?

If no, material infrastructure is constitutive.

### Remove Perceived Objectivity

Does authority weaken?

If yes, perceived neutrality may be part of the mechanism.

# 41. Final Validation Test

Before treating a passage as a significant power/mediation/authority finding, ask:

1. What is being mediated?
2. What is being selected?
3. What becomes visible?
4. What becomes relevant?
5. Who or what receives authority?
6. On what basis?
7. Is legitimacy involved?
8. Is a truth claim being made?
9. Is the process auditable?
10. Where is the power relation?
11. What asymmetry sustains it?
12. What material infrastructure supports it?
13. What institutions embed it?
14. Is there feedback?
15. What makes it contingent?
16. Does the relation transform conditions of another assemblage?
17. How does it differ from Node E?
18. How does it differ from Node A?
19. How does it differ from Node C?

If these distinctions cannot be maintained, the analysis risks collapsing epistemology, politics, authority, and truth into a single category.

---

# 42. Final Principle

> **AI does not need to produce truth in order to participate in the conditions under which something becomes visible, relevant, authoritative, and consequential.**

> **Mediation is not truth, relevance is not truth, authority is not legitimacy, and authority is not power.**

> **The central analytical task is to reconstruct the relations through which information acquires differential visibility, relevance, authority, and practical consequence.**

> **Power should be mapped not only where someone commands, but where relations, infrastructures, dependencies, standards, and institutional arrangements alter the conditions under which others can act.**

> **The strongest analysis does not ask simply whether AI has power. It asks through which relations AI-mediated processes participate in the distribution of visibility, relevance, authority, capacity, and dependency.**
