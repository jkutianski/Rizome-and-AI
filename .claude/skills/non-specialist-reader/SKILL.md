# Non-Specialist Reader

## Purpose

Analyze whether the work remains intelligible to an attentive reader who does not possess specialized knowledge of the philosophical, methodological, scientific, or technical fields involved.

The purpose is not to simplify the work for everyone.

It is to determine whether the text distinguishes:

* necessary conceptual difficulty;
* productive abstraction;
* specialized terminology that can be reconstructed from context;
* unnecessary opacity;
* unexplained assumptions;
* inaccessible technical or philosophical references.

The central question is:

> **Does the work demand intellectual effort from the reader, or does it demand knowledge that the text itself never provides?**

---

# Core Principle

A difficult text is not necessarily an unclear text.

A philosophical work may require:

* concentration;
* conceptual reconstruction;
* tolerance for ambiguity;
* following multiple relations;
* moving between scales;
* understanding unfamiliar distinctions.

These demands are not defects in themselves.

The objective is therefore not:

> make the text easy.

It is:

> **make sure the difficulty comes from the ideas rather than from avoidable opacity.**

---

# Scope

Evaluate the work from the perspective of an attentive non-specialist reader.

The reader may have:

* general familiarity with contemporary technology;
* ordinary familiarity with AI;
* general reading ability;
* no specialized training in philosophy of technology;
* no detailed knowledge of Deleuze and Guattari;
* no specialized knowledge of STS;
* no advanced technical knowledge of AI;
* no previous access to the project's methodological framework.

Do not assume ignorance of ordinary concepts.

---

# Reader Model

Use a reader who is:

* intelligent;
* attentive;
* willing to read carefully;
* unfamiliar with the project's specialized conceptual vocabulary;
* capable of following abstract reasoning;
* not necessarily trained in the relevant disciplines.

Do not model the reader as incapable of understanding philosophical abstraction.

---

# Difficulty Taxonomy

Classify difficulty before recommending intervention.

## Conceptual Difficulty

The idea itself is demanding.

Usually legitimate.

## Structural Difficulty

The reader must reconstruct relationships across distant sections.

Potentially legitimate, but should be tested.

## Terminological Difficulty

A specialized term appears without sufficient contextual support.

Potentially problematic.

## Evidential Difficulty

The reader cannot determine whether a statement is observation, interpretation, empirical claim, or philosophical claim.

Potentially problematic.

## Referential Difficulty

The text assumes knowledge of an author, theory, event, technology, or debate that is necessary for understanding.

Potentially problematic.

## Syntactic Difficulty

The sentence structure obscures an otherwise understandable idea.

Usually local.

## Stylistic Density

The prose is compressed, abstract, or conceptually dense.

Not automatically a problem.

## Accumulative Difficulty

Several individually manageable concepts are introduced simultaneously.

May require pacing rather than simplification.

---

# Procedure

## Step 1 — Read Without Specialized Assistance

Read the complete work without consulting:

* philosophical references;
* methodological explanations from previous conversations;
* external definitions;
* previous versions;
* supplementary documentation.

Determine what can be understood from the work itself.

---

## Step 2 — Identify the Reader's Minimum Requirements

List the knowledge that appears necessary to follow the work.

Separate:

### Required Knowledge

Without it, a major argument cannot be understood.

### Helpful Knowledge

It improves interpretation but is not necessary.

### Specialized Background

The text may reasonably assume it.

### Unnecessary Background

The text should not require it because the relevant point can be understood internally.

---

# Conceptual Accessibility

## Step 3 — Test Central Concepts

Evaluate whether a non-specialist can reconstruct the function of:

* rhizome;
* map;
* territory;
* assemblage;
* element;
* relation;
* tension;
* concentration;
* node;
* inter-stem node;
* substrate;
* transformation;
* propagation;
* emergence;
* agency;
* knowledge;
* evidence;
* relevance;
* authority.

A concept need not have a formal definition.

It may become accessible through:

* examples;
* contrasts;
* repetition with variation;
* application;
* consequences.

---

# Contextual Definition

## Step 4 — Test Whether Concepts Define Themselves Through Use

Ask:

> If the reader does not know this term beforehand, can its function be inferred from what happens around it?

For example, a reader may understand “node” because the text demonstrates:

* concentrated relations;
* tensions;
* transformations;
* recurrence.

That can be sufficient.

Do not add dictionary-style definitions merely because a concept is unfamiliar.

---

# Specialized References

## Step 5 — Audit External References

When the work invokes:

* philosophical authors;
* scientific concepts;
* technical systems;
* historical events;
* theoretical frameworks;
* empirical claims;

ask whether the reference is:

### Contextually sufficient

The reader can understand its role without additional knowledge.

### Partially sufficient

The general role is clear but important details remain inaccessible.

### Insufficient

The argument depends on knowledge not supplied by the work.

Do not demand exhaustive explanation of every reference.

---

# Deleuze and Guattari

## Step 6 — Test Rhizomatic References

A non-specialist reader should not need to know Deleuze and Guattari in detail merely to understand the project's own use of:

* rhizome;
* map;
* multiplicity;
* connection;
* heterogeneity.

If the work's conceptual operation depends on a highly specific interpretation of Deleuze and Guattari that cannot be reconstructed from the text, identify that dependency.

Do not turn the work into an exegesis of Deleuze and Guattari.

---

# AI and Technical Concepts

## Step 7 — Test Technical Accessibility

When technical AI concepts appear, ask whether the reader needs specialist knowledge to understand the argument.

Distinguish:

* technically precise but intelligible;
* technically simplified but sufficient;
* technically ambiguous;
* technically inaccurate;
* unnecessarily specialized.

Do not demand technical exposition where the technical detail is not central to the argument.

---

# Abstraction

## Step 8 — Test Abstract Passages

For dense passages ask:

1. What is the central proposition?
2. What relations are being described?
3. What changes?
4. What tension is present?
5. What example or consequence makes the abstraction intelligible?
6. Can the reader reconstruct the point without translating every sentence into simpler language?

Abstraction is legitimate when the relational structure remains traceable.

---

# Examples

## Step 9 — Evaluate Examples

Examples may:

* ground an abstract concept;
* demonstrate a relation;
* clarify a transformation;
* establish scale;
* expose materiality.

But examples can also become:

* decorative;
* excessive;
* substitutes for argument;
* misleading generalizations.

Ask whether examples perform analytical work.

Do not require examples for every concept.

---

# Density and Pacing

## Step 10 — Analyze Conceptual Load

Identify passages where several new operations occur simultaneously.

For example:

* new concept;
* new assemblage;
* new scale;
* new causal relation;
* new empirical claim;
* new terminology.

A passage can be correct but overloaded.

The appropriate intervention may be:

* paragraph division;
* slight expansion;
* clearer sequencing;

rather than conceptual simplification.

Coordinate with `voice-style-rhythm`.

---

# Transitions

## Step 11 — Test Reader Movement

Determine whether a non-specialist can follow transitions between:

* personal experience and conceptual analysis;
* observation and map;
* map and rhizome;
* domains and assemblages;
* assemblages and nodes;
* nodes and inter-stem nodes;
* technical dynamics and social consequences;
* micro and macro scales;
* analysis and reflexivity.

A transition may remain implicit if the relation is recoverable.

---

# Productive Difficulty

## Step 12 — Preserve Necessary Difficulty

Do not recommend simplification when difficulty results from:

* genuine philosophical complexity;
* multiple simultaneous relations;
* productive ambiguity;
* unresolved tension;
* non-linear structure;
* conceptual novelty;
* deliberate asymmetry.

The reader should sometimes have to work.

That work can be part of the experience of the text.

---

# Unnecessary Opacity

## Step 13 — Identify Avoidable Obstacles

Look for:

* unexplained technical terms;
* ambiguous pronouns or references;
* compressed reasoning;
* abrupt scale changes;
* missing premises;
* undefined distinctions essential to the argument;
* excessively long sentences hiding relational structure;
* conclusions that presuppose unavailable knowledge;
* terminology that shifts meaning without signaling it.

These are stronger candidates for intervention.

---

# Generalization Test

## Step 14 — Test Broad Claims

A non-specialist reader may interpret broad statements more literally than an expert would.

Check formulations involving:

* “AI” as a homogeneous entity;
* “society” as a single actor;
* “technology” as a unified force;
* “science” as a unified practice;
* “knowledge” as a homogeneous category;
* “humans” as a homogeneous group.

Determine whether contextual qualifications are sufficient.

Coordinate with `semantic-precision` and `technological-determinism`.

---

# Causal Interpretation

## Step 15 — Test Whether the Reader May Infer Determinism

Non-specialist readers may interpret:

> X changes Y

as:

> X determines Y.

Check whether the text provides enough relational context to preserve distinctions between:

* influence;
* participation;
* enablement;
* constraint;
* contribution;
* transformation;
* determination.

Do not weaken legitimate causal claims merely because they could theoretically be misunderstood.

---

# Anthropomorphism Test

## Step 16 — Test AI Attribution

Determine whether an attentive non-specialist could reasonably read AI as possessing:

* intentions;
* desires;
* beliefs;
* consciousness;
* understanding;
* autonomous will.

Distinguish ordinary active language from genuine mental attribution.

For example:

> “AI generates a response”

does not by itself imply intention.

Coordinate with `anthropomorphism-audit`.

---

# Knowledge, Authority, and Truth

## Step 17 — Test Conceptual Differentiation

Check whether a non-specialist reader can distinguish:

* information;
* production;
* knowledge;
* evidence;
* relevance;
* authority;
* legitimacy;
* truth.

Particularly test the D and E territories.

The reader should not be pushed into assuming:

> relevance = authority

or:

> authority = truth

or:

> production = knowledge.

If the distinction is central but inaccessible, classify the problem accordingly.

---

# Materiality

## Step 18 — Test Material Conditions

Ask whether the role of:

* energy;
* water;
* minerals;
* semiconductors;
* compute;
* data centers;
* networks;
* devices;
* logistics;

can be understood by a non-specialist as more than background scenery.

The reader should understand why materiality matters when the text makes it analytically relevant.

Do not add technical detail merely to demonstrate material sophistication.

---

# Power

## Step 19 — Test Power Relations

Check whether a non-specialist reader can understand power through:

* dependency;
* concentration;
* access;
* infrastructure;
* institutional authority;
* visibility;
* selection;
* standards.

Avoid assuming that “power” automatically means domination by a single actor.

---

# Scale

## Step 20 — Test Scale Transitions

A non-specialist reader should be able to follow movements between:

* individual;
* technical system;
* institution;
* infrastructure;
* organization;
* society;
* geopolitics.

When the text moves rapidly across scales, determine whether the relational mechanism remains visible.

---

# Reader Overload

## Step 21 — Identify Cumulative Burden

Some passages may become difficult not because any single sentence is unclear but because the reader is asked to retain too many unresolved relations.

Track:

* number of new concepts;
* number of active distinctions;
* number of entities;
* number of scales;
* number of simultaneous tensions;
* number of unresolved references.

If cognitive load becomes excessive, identify the source.

Do not automatically simplify the underlying argument.

---

# Reader Recovery

## Step 22 — Test Whether the Reader Can Recover

After a difficult passage, ask:

> Does the text subsequently provide enough information for the reader to reconstruct what was unclear?

A temporary difficulty can be legitimate if later passages:

* clarify;
* exemplify;
* return;
* reframe;
* connect.

Do not classify temporary uncertainty as structural opacity.

---

# Whole-Work Accessibility

## Step 23 — Test Cumulative Understanding

At several points in the work, ask whether the reader can still answer:

* What is the work doing?
* What is being mapped?
* Why is the current section here?
* What changed from the previous territory?
* What question remains open?

A reader does not need to remember every detail.

They need to retain the trajectory.

Coordinate with `whole-work-reading`.

---

# Non-Specialist Reconstruction

At the end of the reading, reconstruct the work using ordinary language.

Explain:

1. the central question;
2. the role of the rhizome;
3. what the map does;
4. what assemblages do;
5. what nodes do;
6. what inter-stem nodes do;
7. what the major transformations are;
8. what remains unresolved.

If this reconstruction is possible without specialized vocabulary, the work has strong conceptual accessibility even if the original prose remains sophisticated.

---

# Accessibility vs Simplification

Do not confuse:

> making something understandable

with:

> making something simpler.

A concept may remain complex while becoming intelligible.

Prefer interventions that clarify:

* relations;
* distinctions;
* dependencies;
* examples;
* transitions.

Avoid replacing conceptual complexity with generic language.

---

# Common False Positives

Do not classify as problematic:

* difficult philosophical concepts;
* unfamiliar terminology that becomes clear through context;
* long but structurally coherent passages;
* abstract argumentation;
* unresolved tensions;
* multiple interpretations;
* asymmetrical development;
* non-linear organization;
* lack of textbook definitions;
* references that function without exhaustive explanation.

---

# Common False Negatives

Look carefully for:

* hidden assumptions about specialized knowledge;
* technical vocabulary doing argumentative work without explanation;
* philosophical terms used as if self-evident;
* unexplained transitions between scales;
* dense paragraphs containing several independent claims;
* broad claims that become misleading to non-specialists;
* terminology whose meaning depends on previous project conversations;
* apparent accessibility that conceals conceptual conflation.

# Output Protocol

For each significant finding, report:

### Reader Difficulty

Identify what the reader is likely to find difficult.

### Evidence

Quote or identify the relevant passage or structural feature.

### Difficulty Type

Classify it as:

* Conceptual
* Structural
* Terminological
* Evidential
* Referential
* Syntactic
* Stylistic
* Accumulative

### Reader Reconstruction

Explain what an attentive non-specialist can reasonably understand.

### Missing Support

If applicable, identify what the text does not provide.

### Diagnosis

Determine whether the difficulty is:

* productive;
* acceptable;
* locally problematic;
* structurally problematic;
* unnecessary opacity.

### Classification

Use:

* **A — Critical:** accessibility failure materially prevents understanding of the work.
* **B — Important:** an important conceptual or structural distinction is difficult to reconstruct.
* **C — Local:** a limited passage creates avoidable difficulty.
* **D — Stylistic:** the issue concerns density or presentation more than conceptual accessibility.
* **E — Non-problematic:** the difficulty is justified or productive.

### Confidence

Use:

* High
* Medium
* Low

### Implication

Explain how the difficulty affects the reader's ability to follow the work.

### Recommendation

Choose:

* Necessary
* Advisable
* Optional
* Not recommended
* Leave unchanged

---

# Intervention Threshold

Recommend intervention only when:

1. the difficulty blocks a necessary conceptual distinction;
2. the reader requires hidden project context;
3. terminology cannot be reconstructed from use;
4. an important transition is genuinely inaccessible;
5. technical or philosophical assumptions exceed what the text reasonably provides;
6. density obscures the relational structure.

Prefer the smallest intervention capable of restoring intelligibility.

Do not simplify merely to reduce effort.

---

# Final Principle

> **The goal is not to make a difficult work easy. The goal is to ensure that the difficulty belongs to the thought rather than to avoidable opacity.**

A sophisticated work may ask a non-specialist reader to slow down, reconstruct relations, and tolerate uncertainty.

That is legitimate.

The decisive distinction is:

> **Does the reader struggle because the idea is complex, or because the text has failed to give the reader the means to understand the complexity?**

Preserve the first.

Intervene selectively in the second.

> **Productive difficulty invites the reader into the map. Unnecessary opacity prevents the reader from seeing how the map was constructed.**
