# Propagation Analysis

## Purpose

Analyze whether a transformation **propagates through a relational field** and, if so, how that propagation occurs, what conditions enable or constrain it, what changes along the way, and what new dynamics emerge elsewhere.

The purpose is not to identify every instance of influence, diffusion, repetition, or consequence as propagation.

Propagation is a stronger analytical claim.

The central question is:

> **“What transformation moves, extends, or generates effects through the relational field, under what conditions, and what changes as it reaches other parts of the map?”**

---

# Scope

Analyze possible propagation:

* within an assemblage;
* between assemblages;
* across domains;
* between nodes;
* across material and institutional infrastructures;
* through practices;
* through feedback circuits;
* through changes in conditions of functioning;
* through transformations that generate further transformations.

Propagation may be:

* direct or mediated;
* local or transversal;
* rapid or delayed;
* stable or contingent;
* asymmetric;
* partial;
* interrupted;
* amplified;
* attenuated;
* redirected;
* transformed during transmission.

Propagation does not have to reach the entire map.

A transformation may propagate along one line while remaining contained elsewhere.

---

# Core Principles

## 1. Propagation Requires More Than Connection

A connection establishes a relation.

Propagation requires a transformation that has effects beyond its initial location.

Therefore:

> A is connected to B

does not establish:

> a transformation propagates from A to B.

First identify the transformation.

Then identify how it reaches another part of the relational field.

---

## 2. Propagation Is Not Mere Diffusion

Something appearing in several places does not automatically mean that it propagated.

Distinguish:

* repetition;
* imitation;
* diffusion;
* circulation;
* transmission;
* influence;
* causal consequence;
* propagation.

Propagation becomes analytically significant when a transformation alters conditions or generates a new dynamic elsewhere.

---

## 3. Identify What Propagates

Do not use “propagation” without specifying what is moving or extending.

Possible objects of propagation include:

* a transformed practice;
* a changed condition;
* a technical capability;
* a dependency;
* a constraint;
* a tension;
* an institutional arrangement;
* a standard;
* a mode of evaluation;
* a new relation;
* a change in visibility;
* a redistribution of authority;
* a new capacity;
* a feedback effect.

The propagated phenomenon may change form as it moves.

Do not assume that what arrives elsewhere is identical to what originated at the starting point.

---

## 4. Propagation Is Relational

Propagation should be analyzed as a process through relations.

A useful abstract structure is:

**initial transformation → altered condition → relational transmission → modification elsewhere → further dynamic**

This is an analytical heuristic, not a mandatory causal chain.

The actual process may contain:

* loops;
* interruptions;
* substitutions;
* competing forces;
* delays;
* reversals;
* amplification;
* attenuation;
* multiple pathways.

Do not impose linearity where the source describes a more complex dynamic.

---

## 5. Propagation Does Not Imply Linear Causality

Propagation can involve causal relations, but propagation itself is not synonymous with linear causation.

Possible structures include:

> A → B → C

but also:

> A ↔ B → C

or:

> A → B ↔ C

or:

> A → B → C ↺ A

or multiple simultaneous pathways.

Use causal language when justified.

Do not replace relational complexity with an artificial chain merely because it is easier to describe.

---

## 6. Propagation Does Not Imply Inevitability

A transformation may propagate without being historically inevitable.

Ask:

* What conditions enabled propagation?
* What conditions could have blocked it?
* Were institutional decisions involved?
* Were resources required?
* Were technical standards involved?
* Did investment matter?
* Were there contingencies?
* Were there alternative pathways?

A successful propagation does not demonstrate that it had to occur.

---

## 7. Propagation Can Be Partial

A transformation may propagate:

* to some assemblages but not others;
* through some infrastructures but not others;
* at one scale but not another;
* temporarily;
* unevenly;
* with different effects in different locations.

Do not assume propagation must be complete or system-wide.

Partial propagation may be analytically more revealing than universal propagation.

---

## 8. Propagation Can Be Asymmetrical

Propagation may produce different effects depending on where it arrives.

The same transformation can:

* enable one assemblage;
* constrain another;
* destabilize a third;
* be absorbed by a fourth.

Therefore:

> propagation ≠ uniformity.

Analyze the receiving conditions.

---

## 9. Propagation Can Transform What It Propagates

Propagation is not necessarily transmission of an unchanged object.

When a transformation enters another assemblage, it may be:

* adapted;
* translated;
* resisted;
* constrained;
* amplified;
* redirected;
* combined with existing relations;
* transformed into a new dynamic.

The propagated process may therefore be better represented as:

**transformation → transmission → adaptation → new transformation**

rather than:

**origin → identical copy → destination**

---

# Procedure

## 1. Identify the Initial Transformation

Before claiming propagation, identify what changed at the point of origin.

Ask:

* What was different before and after?
* Which condition changed?
* Which relation changed?
* Which practice changed?
* Which capacity changed?
* Which dependency or tension changed?

If no transformation can be identified, do not classify the phenomenon as propagation.

---

## 2. Identify the Starting Point

Determine where the transformation initially becomes relevant.

This may be:

* an element;
* a practice;
* an assemblage;
* a node;
* an infrastructure;
* an institutional arrangement;
* a technical system;
* a change in discourse or evaluation.

Do not automatically treat the most visible element as the true origin.

The origin may be distributed across several relations.

---

## 3. Identify the Pathway

Determine how the transformation reaches another location.

Possible pathways include:

* technical infrastructure;
* institutional procedures;
* standards;
* markets;
* data flows;
* organizational practices;
* professional practices;
* policy;
* imitation;
* adaptation;
* feedback;
* material dependencies;
* social learning.

If the pathway is unknown, say so.

Do not invent a mechanism simply because propagation seems plausible.

---

## 4. Identify the Receiving Site

Determine where the transformation arrives.

Ask:

* Which assemblage is affected?
* Which element changes?
* Which relation changes?
* Which condition of functioning changes?
* Does the receiving assemblage respond differently from the source?

Propagation requires a destination or subsequent relational effect.

---

## 5. Analyze the Change at the Receiving Site

Determine whether the receiving site merely experiences the same phenomenon or whether its functioning is altered.

Relevant changes include:

* new dependencies;
* new constraints;
* new capacities;
* altered practices;
* changed standards;
* redistributed authority;
* new tensions;
* altered resource requirements;
* changed organizational structures;
* new forms of mediation.

This is where propagation becomes analytically stronger than simple diffusion.

---

## 6. Identify Intermediate Relations

When propagation passes through several locations, identify intermediate relations.

For example:

**technical capacity → institutional adoption → changed practice → new data → further technical development**

Do not collapse intermediate stages when they materially affect the process.

Likewise, do not multiply intermediate stages merely to make a model look sophisticated.

---

## 7. Analyze Modification Along the Path

Ask whether the propagated transformation:

* remains substantially similar;
* changes form;
* becomes stronger;
* becomes weaker;
* is redirected;
* is fragmented;
* encounters resistance;
* produces an unexpected effect.

Propagation may therefore be nonlinear in both structure and outcome.

---

## 8. Analyze Conditions of Propagation

Identify the conditions that enable, constrain, accelerate, delay, or interrupt propagation.

These may include:

* infrastructure;
* energy;
* compute;
* networks;
* data;
* standards;
* regulation;
* institutions;
* investment;
* labor;
* expertise;
* material resources;
* organizational capacity;
* political decisions;
* market conditions.

The substrate can therefore function as part of the propagation mechanism rather than merely as background context.

---

## 9. Analyze Obstacles and Interruptions

Look for:

* resistance;
* regulation;
* technical incompatibility;
* lack of resources;
* institutional barriers;
* competing infrastructures;
* conflicting standards;
* social rejection;
* economic constraints;
* material scarcity.

A blocked pathway may be as analytically important as a successful one.

Propagation analysis should therefore include **non-propagation** when it reveals relevant constraints.

---

## 10. Analyze Feedback

Determine whether propagation produces a return effect.

For example:

**A transforms B → B changes C → C modifies A**

This may create:

* reinforcement;
* stabilization;
* escalation;
* correction;
* adaptation;
* recursive transformation.

Do not call a process feedback merely because effects occur in both directions.

A feedback circuit requires a return relation that modifies subsequent functioning.

---

## 11. Analyze Scale

Propagation can operate across:

* micro-level practices;
* meso-level organizations or assemblages;
* macro-level institutions or systems.

Do not assume that a micro-level change automatically becomes macro-level transformation.

Identify the mechanism that connects scales.

Likewise, a macro-level change may reshape local conditions without producing identical local effects.

---

## 12. Analyze Directionality Without Forcing It

Propagation can have:

* one principal direction;
* multiple directions;
* reciprocal movement;
* circularity;
* branching;
* convergence;
* temporary reversal.

Do not impose a single source-to-destination model when the source supports a distributed process.

---

## 13. Analyze Concentration and Branching

A transformation may propagate along multiple paths.

This may produce:

* branching;
* convergence;
* concentration;
* divergence;
* recombination.

A branching process does not necessarily indicate greater complexity in the abstract.

Ask what the branching changes relationally.

---

## 14. Distinguish Propagation From Emergence

Propagation and emergence are different analytical claims.

Propagation asks:

> How does a transformation extend through relations?

Emergence asks:

> What relational configuration produces a property or dynamic that cannot be adequately reduced to one element alone?

A propagated transformation may contribute to emergence.

It does not automatically constitute emergence.

---

## 15. Distinguish Propagation From an Inter-Stem Node

Propagation can provide evidence for an inter-stem node, but propagation alone does not establish one.

For an inter-stem node, verify the stronger sequence:

**assemblages → relations → tensions → concentration → modification of conditions → transformation → possible propagation**

Propagation is the final possible stage of that sequence, not the definition of the category.

---

# Propagation Patterns

Use descriptive patterns when they clarify the dynamics.

## Linear Propagation

A transformation moves primarily from one site to another.

## Branching Propagation

One transformation generates effects across multiple pathways.

## Convergent Propagation

Different pathways converge on a common assemblage or condition.

## Recursive Propagation

Effects return and modify the process that initiated them.

## Cascading Propagation

A transformation alters conditions that enable further transformations elsewhere.

## Mediated Propagation

Propagation depends on an intermediate assemblage, infrastructure, institution, or practice.

## Attenuated Propagation

The effect becomes weaker as it moves through the relational field.

## Amplified Propagation

The effect becomes stronger through interaction with subsequent relations.

## Transformed Propagation

The propagated dynamic changes substantially as it enters different assemblages.

## Interrupted Propagation

The transformation encounters a condition that prevents or redirects further propagation.

These are analytical descriptions, not mandatory categories.

---

# Propagation and the Rhizomatic Map

Propagation is particularly important for making the dynamic character of the rhizome visible.

A static classification can show:

> A — B — C

Propagation asks what happens when a transformation moves through that relation:

> A changes B → B's changed condition affects C → C modifies another relation → the new condition feeds back into A or elsewhere.

The purpose is not to create a complete network.

It is to identify **lines of transformation**.

These lines may:

* branch;
* disappear;
* reconnect;
* reverse;
* encounter resistance;
* generate new tensions;
* change scale;
* alter their form.

The map should make such movement recognizable without pretending to capture the entire rhizome.

---

# Propagation and Materiality

Propagation should be tested against material conditions whenever relevant.

A transformation may depend on:

* energy availability;
* computing capacity;
* semiconductor supply;
* data-center infrastructure;
* networks;
* storage;
* devices;
* logistics;
* water;
* minerals;
* physical access;
* maintenance.

A transformation that appears informational or conceptual may therefore propagate through material infrastructures.

Conversely, material constraints may interrupt propagation.

Do not treat materiality as a decorative background layer.

---

# Propagation and Power

Propagation may be accelerated or constrained by concentrations of power.

Analyze:

* who controls infrastructure;
* who controls access;
* who determines standards;
* who can scale a technology;
* who can block adoption;
* who controls resources;
* who benefits from propagation;
* who absorbs its costs;
* who becomes dependent upon it.

Do not assume that greater propagation means greater decentralization.

A transformation can propagate precisely because power is concentrated.

---

# Propagation and Contingency

Ask what made propagation possible in the particular historical configuration.

Potential conditions include:

* investment;
* policy;
* regulation;
* institutional adoption;
* technical standards;
* infrastructure;
* accidents;
* reuse;
* competition;
* scarcity;
* geopolitical decisions.

A propagated transformation should not automatically be described as inevitable.

The fact that a line propagated does not prove that no alternative line was possible.

---

# Non-Propagation as Evidence

Failure to propagate may reveal:

* structural barriers;
* incompatible assemblages;
* insufficient infrastructure;
* competing dynamics;
* institutional resistance;
* material limits;
* political intervention;
* economic constraints.

Therefore, ask not only:

> “Where did the transformation go?”

but also:

> **“Where did it stop, and what stopped it?”**

Non-propagation can expose the conditions that make propagation possible.

---

# Common Analytical Errors

Do not:

* call every consequence propagation;
* equate propagation with diffusion;
* equate propagation with influence;
* equate propagation with causality;
* infer propagation merely because two nodes are connected;
* infer propagation from temporal succession alone;
* assume propagation is linear;
* assume propagation is uniform;
* assume propagation is inevitable;
* assume the propagated phenomenon remains unchanged;
* ignore intermediate relations;
* ignore material infrastructure;
* ignore resistance or interruption;
* ignore asymmetry;
* infer emergence from propagation;
* infer an inter-stem node from propagation alone;
* treat every repeated pattern as propagation;
* invent a pathway that the evidence does not support;
* treat successful propagation as proof of necessity;
* assume propagation must reach the whole map.

---

# Evidence Standard

For each propagation claim, distinguish:

### Directly Demonstrated

The source explicitly establishes the transformation, pathway, and subsequent effect.

### Strongly Supported

The source provides sufficient relational evidence to reconstruct the propagation with limited inference.

### Plausible

The propagation is compatible with the source but important intermediate mechanisms remain uncertain.

### Speculative

The propagation requires substantial assumptions not established by the source.

### Unsupported

The source does not provide enough evidence to justify the claim.

When the pathway is uncertain, preserve the uncertainty rather than completing the chain through speculation.

---

# Output Protocol

For each significant propagation, report:

### Initial Transformation

What changed and where.

### Propagated Dynamic

What is extending, moving, or generating further effects.

### Pathway

How the transformation reaches another site.

### Intermediate Relations

Relevant stages or mediating relations.

### Receiving Site

Where the propagated transformation arrives.

### Subsequent Transformation

What changes there.

### Conditions

Material, infrastructural, institutional, social, technical, economic, or political conditions enabling or constraining propagation.

### Modulation

Whether propagation is amplified, attenuated, redirected, adapted, transformed, or interrupted.

### Directionality

One-way, reciprocal, circular, branching, convergent, or unclear.

### Scale

Micro, meso, macro, cross-scale, or transversal.

### Feedback

Whether the propagation creates a return effect.

### Evidence

Direct, strongly supported, plausible, speculative, or unsupported.

### Inter-Stem Relevance

Whether the propagation contributes evidence to a possible inter-stem dynamic.

Do not classify it as an inter-stem node without satisfying the stricter criteria.

### Classification

Use:

* **A — Critical**
* **B — Important**
* **C — Local**
* **D — Stylistic**
* **E — Non-Problematic**

### Confidence

Use:

* High
* Medium
* Low

# Diagnostic Questions

Use these questions when needed:

1. What exactly changes initially?
2. Where does the transformation begin?
3. What exactly propagates?
4. Through which relation does it propagate?
5. What is the pathway?
6. What intermediate assemblages or conditions are involved?
7. Where does the transformation arrive?
8. What changes there?
9. Is the effect genuinely transformative or merely consequential?
10. Does the propagated dynamic remain the same or change form?
11. What enables propagation?
12. What constrains it?
13. Where does propagation stop?
14. What interrupts or redirects it?
15. Is the process linear, branching, recursive, convergent, or distributed?
16. Is there feedback?
17. Is the propagation asymmetrical?
18. Does it cross assemblage boundaries?
19. Does it cross domain boundaries?
20. Does it modify conditions of functioning?
21. Does it generate a new tension?
22. Does it provide evidence for an inter-stem dynamic?
23. Is emergence actually demonstrated, or merely inferred?
24. Is causality being asserted beyond the evidence?
25. Is the propagation historically contingent?
26. What would have to be different for the propagation not to occur?
27. What would be lost from the map if this propagation were removed?

# Final Principle

> **Propagation is not the mere spread of something through a network. It is the extension of a transformation through relations, under specific conditions, such that what happens elsewhere is altered by what happened before.**

A strong propagation analysis therefore identifies:

**what changed → what propagated → through what relation → under what conditions → where it arrived → what changed there → what happened next.**

The goal is not to draw longer causal chains.

It is to make **lines of transformation visible while preserving interruption, contingency, asymmetry, feedback, and change along the way.**
