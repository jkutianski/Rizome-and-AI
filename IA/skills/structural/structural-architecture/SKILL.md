# Structural Architecture Audit

## Purpose

Analyze the structural architecture of a text as a whole.

The purpose is to determine whether the different sections, domains, assemblages, nodes, inter-stem nodes, transitions, connections, and conceptual levels perform distinct and necessary functions, and whether their arrangement makes the intended cartographic operation visible.

This Skill does **not** ask whether the text is conventionally organized.

It asks whether the architecture:

* supports the conceptual operation of the work;
* preserves meaningful distinctions;
* makes relations visible;
* prevents unnecessary duplication;
* maintains appropriate analytical levels;
* avoids hidden hierarchies;
* preserves the distinction between map and territory;
* allows asymmetry where the material requires it;
* prevents categories from becoming more rigid than the phenomena they describe;
* gives each structural component a recognizable function.

The central question is:

> **Does the architecture clarify the relational field being mapped, or does the architecture begin to substitute a rigid classification for that field?**

---

# Core Principle

Structural coherence does not require conventional hierarchy.

A text may have:

* sections;
* domains;
* assemblages;
* nodes;
* inter-stem nodes;
* sequences;
* categories;
* boundaries;

while still functioning as a cartographic representation of a dynamic rhizomatic field.

Therefore:

* structure ≠ hierarchy
* order ≠ determinism
* classification ≠ anti-rhizomaticity
* stability of the map ≠ stability of the territory
* distinction ≠ separation
* boundary ≠ isolation
* category ≠ ontology
* asymmetry ≠ defect
* repetition ≠ redundancy
* connection ≠ hierarchy
* sequence ≠ causality
* architecture ≠ territory

The map may stabilize relations temporarily without claiming that the underlying field is stable.

---

# Scope

Apply this Skill to the architecture of:

* the complete work;
* major sections;
* domains;
* assemblages;
* nodes;
* inter-stem nodes;
* transitions;
* conceptual layers;
* connections;
* propagations;
* openings and endings;
* methodological sections;
* reflective sections;
* examples;
* conclusions.

It is particularly important when the text contains a deliberately constructed analytical architecture such as:

> Rhizome → Domains → Assemblages → Nodes → Inter-stem Nodes → Propagations

This sequence must be treated as a **cartographic architecture**, not automatically as an ontological hierarchy.

---

# Architectural Model

When relevant, reconstruct the architecture as:

```text
Rhizome
    ↓
General Domains
    ↓
Assemblages
    ↓
Concentrations of Relations and Tensions
    ↓
Nodes
    ↓
Inter-stem Nodes
    ↓
Transformations / Propagations
```

This representation is analytical.

It must not be interpreted as claiming that:

* the rhizome causes the domains;
* domains cause assemblages;
* assemblages cause nodes;
* nodes cause inter-stem nodes;
* the map's levels exist as natural layers in reality.

The architecture describes how the observer has chosen to make a dynamic field provisionally legible.

---

# Structural Units

## 1. Work

The complete text as a single conceptual trajectory.

Ask:

* Does the work have an identifiable problem?
* Does the trajectory develop?
* Do sections contribute to the same larger operation?
* Does the ending close the trajectory without pretending to close the rhizome?

---

## 2. Domains

In this project, general domains such as:

* Science
* Technology
* Society

should function as **cartographic orientation zones**.

They should not automatically become:

* ontological containers;
* rigid disciplines;
* isolated territories;
* equivalent categories;
* hierarchical levels.

The same element or relation may cross domains.

---

## 3. Assemblages

An assemblage is a relatively stable configuration of relations and tensions.

It is not merely:

* a topic;
* a list of elements;
* a discipline;
* a chapter heading;
* a collection of examples.

Check whether each assemblage has:

* identifiable elements;
* meaningful relations;
* internal tensions;
* relative stability;
* recognizable dynamics;
* concentrated relations;
* porous boundaries.

---

## 4. Nodes

A node is a **concentration dynamic of relations and tensions**.

It is not automatically:

* an important element;
* a broad topic;
* a keyword;
* an interdisciplinary subject;
* a major section.

The structural audit must verify that nodes have distinct functions.

---

## 5. Inter-Stem Nodes

An inter-stem node is not simply:

> AI + X

It requires a transformation across assemblages or domains.

The structural test is:

```text
Assemblages
→ relations
→ tensions
→ concentration
→ modification of conditions
→ transformation
→ possible propagation
```

The title cannot establish the category.

Structural architecture must therefore be tested against the actual relational dynamics.

---

# Structural Procedure

## Step 1 — Reconstruct the Architecture

Create an internal structural map of:

* opening;
* methodological framing;
* domains;
* assemblages;
* nodes;
* inter-stem nodes;
* connections;
* propagations;
* closing.

Do not modify the text yet.

The first task is descriptive reconstruction.

---

## Step 2 — Identify the Function of Each Section

For every major section ask:

> **What does this section do that another section does not?**

Possible functions include:

* framing;
* definition;
* methodological explanation;
* observation;
* conceptual distinction;
* analysis;
* synthesis;
* transition;
* complication;
* transformation;
* propagation;
* reflexive observation;
* closure.

A section can have more than one function, but its principal function should be identifiable.

---

## Step 3 — Test Structural Necessity

Ask:

> If this section disappeared, what conceptual function would be lost?

Possible results:

* indispensable;
* important but partially duplicated;
* replaceable;
* redundant;
* unclear;
* structurally useful despite local repetition.

Do not remove a section merely because another section discusses a related idea.

The question is whether the **function** is duplicated.

Coordinate with `necessity-redundancy`.

---

## Step 4 — Test Level Distinctions

Check whether the architecture distinguishes:

* element;
* relation;
* tension;
* concentration;
* node;
* assemblage;
* domain;
* inter-stem node;
* transformation;
* propagation.

Look for level collapse such as:

> element treated as node;

> theme treated as assemblage;

> connection treated as propagation;

> interdisciplinary topic treated as inter-stem node;

> transformation treated as node;

> domain treated as ontological territory.

Such collapses can create structural confusion.

---

## Step 5 — Test Domain Function

Ask whether domains remain:

* orienting;
* provisional;
* porous;
* cartographic.

Look for hidden claims that:

* Science is separate from Technology;
* Technology is separate from Society;
* each phenomenon belongs to one domain;
* domains have fixed boundaries;
* domain membership is intrinsic.

A phenomenon can occupy more than one domain.

---

## Step 6 — Test Assemblage Integrity

For each assemblage ask:

1. What elements participate?
2. What relations connect them?
3. What tensions organize them?
4. What gives the configuration relative stability?
5. What changes within it?
6. Which relations cross its boundary?
7. Which nodes concentrate relations?
8. What emergent dynamics appear?
9. What conditions enable or constrain it?

If the assemblage is merely a thematic grouping, flag the structural weakness.

---

## Step 7 — Test Node Differentiation

Compare nodes across the entire architecture.

For each node ask:

* What relational concentration does it capture?
* What tension distinguishes it?
* What transformation does it organize?
* What function does it have that another node does not?
* Does it overlap with another node?
* Is the overlap productive or redundant?

Do not demand equal size or equal complexity.

---

## Step 8 — Test Inter-Stem Structure

For every inter-stem node reconstruct:

1. assemblages involved;
2. relations between them;
3. tensions;
4. concentration;
5. modification of conditions;
6. transformation;
7. possible propagation.

If one or more steps are absent, classify the structural status carefully.

Possible results:

* genuine inter-stem node;
* partially developed inter-stem node;
* thematic crossing;
* ordinary connection;
* unresolved;
* insufficient evidence.

Do not force the category.

Coordinate with `inter-stem-validation`.

---

# Transversal Architecture

## Step 9 — Compare the Whole Map

Perform a transversal pass across all sections.

Ask:

* Which ideas recur?
* Which functions recur?
* Which tensions recur?
* Which transformations recur?
* Which examples recur?
* Which distinctions recur?
* Which nodes overlap?
* Which relations connect apparently separate sections?

The purpose is not to eliminate recurrence.

The purpose is to determine whether recurrence represents:

* reinforcement;
* development;
* transformation;
* return;
* feedback;
* reframing;

or simple duplication.

---

## Step 10 — Detect Hidden Hierarchy

A hierarchy may emerge unintentionally even when the text explicitly rejects hierarchy.

Look for:

* one domain treated as foundational;
* one node treated as the center;
* one concept treated as the explanation of all others;
* one section functioning as a hidden conclusion;
* one category absorbing the rest;
* a linear progression presented as necessary;
* causal ordering disguised as structural ordering.

Do not treat every sequence as hierarchy.

A sequence may simply describe the order in which the observer constructs the map.

---

# Connections and Crossings

## Step 11 — Analyze Structural Connections

Determine how major units connect.

Possible relations include:

* dependency;
* feedback;
* mediation;
* transformation;
* constraint;
* enablement;
* transfer;
* conflict;
* adaptation;
* amplification;
* filtering;
* substitution;
* reinforcement.

Do not assume that every section must connect to every other section.

A meaningful map can be sparse.

---

## Step 12 — Analyze Boundary Crossings

Look for elements and relations that cross:

* domains;
* assemblages;
* nodes;
* scales;
* conceptual levels.

Ask whether the crossing:

* merely connects;
* creates tension;
* modifies conditions;
* generates transformation;
* propagates elsewhere.

Only the stronger transformation pattern supports an inter-stem classification.

---

# Redundancy

## Step 13 — Distinguish Recurrence from Redundancy

Repeated material is not automatically redundant.

A concept may recur because it:

* develops;
* changes function;
* appears at another scale;
* returns after transformation;
* connects different assemblages;
* gains a new implication.

Redundancy exists when substantially the same:

* claim;
* function;
* tension;
* example;
* conclusion;

is repeated without adding a meaningful structural role.

Coordinate with `necessity-redundancy`.

---

# Asymmetry

## Step 14 — Preserve Meaningful Asymmetry

Do not impose symmetry merely for visual or structural neatness.

Nodes may differ in:

* length;
* density;
* number of elements;
* number of relations;
* number of tensions;
* evidential support;
* conceptual importance;
* degree of development.

Assemblages may also differ.

A structurally valid map does not require equal-sized boxes.

Asymmetry may itself reveal:

* concentration;
* uneven development;
* power;
* evidential density;
* different analytical functions.

Coordinate with `asymmetry-analysis`.

---

# Architecture and the Observer

## Step 15 — Identify the Observer's Construction

Ask:

* Who created these categories?
* Why these boundaries?
* Why these nodes?
* Why these crossings?
* What has been excluded?
* What has been grouped?
* What has been stabilized?
* What remains outside the map?

The architecture is an operation of observation.

It should not silently present its categories as properties of the territory itself.

The observer does not become omnipotent merely because the map is constructed.

---

# Architecture and Rhizomaticity

## Step 16 — Test Whether the Architecture Makes the Rhizome Visible

Ask whether the structure makes visible:

* multiplicity;
* relationality;
* crossings;
* tensions;
* transformations;
* feedback;
* contingency;
* asymmetry;
* propagation;
* partiality;
* changing conditions.

A highly organized architecture can still be rhizomatic in analytical function.

Conversely, a chaotic or fragmented architecture is not automatically rhizomatic.

The relevant question is:

> **Does the organization preserve the relational dynamism of what it maps?**

---

# Structural Capture

## Step 17 — Test for Conventional Classification

A major risk is that the map gradually becomes a conventional taxonomy.

Indicators include:

* every element assigned to one category;
* rigid boundaries;
* exhaustive classification;
* fixed membership;
* stable hierarchy;
* categories treated as natural;
* no meaningful crossings;
* nodes reduced to labels;
* relations becoming secondary to boxes.

If this occurs, determine whether the architecture is losing its cartographic character.

Do not assume that categorization itself is the problem.

The problem is when classification erases the dynamics it was meant to make visible.

---

# Closure

## Step 18 — Analyze the Ending Structurally

Ask:

* Does the ending close the trajectory?
* Does it synthesize without totalizing?
* Does it preserve openness?
* Does it avoid claiming that the rhizome has been completely mapped?
* Does it return to the initial problem?
* Does it leave relevant questions open?

A map may have a boundary without claiming that the territory ends there.

Coordinate with `ending-openness`.

---

# Structural Stress Test

Perform the following counterfactuals:

### Remove one node

What changes?

### Merge two nodes

What conceptual distinction disappears?

### Remove one assemblage

What relations become invisible?

### Remove one inter-stem node

What transformation disappears?

### Remove one connection

Does the architecture lose a necessary relation or merely become less explicit?

### Remove the methodological framing

Can the reader still understand the map?

### Remove the closing

Does the work lose its trajectory or merely its summary?

These tests are diagnostic, not automatic editing instructions.

---

# Common Structural Errors

Look for:

### Topic architecture

Sections organized merely by subjects rather than relational dynamics.

### Element architecture

Important elements treated as nodes.

### Taxonomic architecture

Everything forced into fixed categories.

### Hidden hierarchy

One level becomes implicitly superior or foundational.

### Linear architecture

Structural order is presented as necessary causal progression.

### Symmetry pressure

Unequal phenomena are artificially equalized.

### Duplicate architecture

Multiple sections perform the same structural function.

### Missing architecture

Important transformations or connections remain invisible.

### Overloaded architecture

One node absorbs too many distinct tensions.

### Fragmented architecture

Sections lack sufficient relations to the larger work.

### Premature closure

The architecture presents a provisional map as complete territory.

---

# Common False Positives

Do **not** flag architecture merely because:

* it contains categories;
* it has headings;
* it uses domains;
* sections have different lengths;
* some nodes are more developed;
* some relations are stronger than others;
* the text follows a sequence;
* the map is relatively stable;
* not every node connects to every other;
* some concepts recur;
* one section is more important than another;
* the architecture is asymmetrical.

These can all be legitimate properties of a cartographic construction.

---

# Common False Negatives

Do not overlook structural problems when:

* a title is doing the work of an argument;
* an element is treated as a node without relational concentration;
* an interdisciplinary topic is treated as an inter-stem node;
* domains become rigid containers;
* nodes duplicate one another;
* one node silently becomes the center of the architecture;
* a sequence hides causal determinism;
* the ending repeats earlier material without structural function;
* the map claims openness while its categories function as closed taxonomy;
* distinctions disappear because several concepts are repeatedly merged;
* a section exists only because the architecture expects symmetry.

# Output Protocol

For each relevant finding, report:

### Structural Unit

Identify the section, domain, assemblage, node, inter-stem node, connection, transition, or ending involved.

### Structural Function

State what the unit currently does.

### Evidence

Identify the relevant textual evidence.

### Structural Diagnosis

Explain whether the unit:

* performs a unique function;
* overlaps another unit;
* collapses analytical levels;
* creates hidden hierarchy;
* lacks necessary support;
* creates a useful crossing;
* becomes too taxonomic;
* remains structurally justified.

### Classification

Use:

* **A — Critical:** the structural problem compromises the architecture of the work.
* **B — Important:** the problem significantly affects differentiation, relationality, or cartographic coherence.
* **C — Local:** a specific structural adjustment would improve clarity or function.
* **D — Stylistic:** the issue concerns presentation more than architecture.
* **E — Non-problematic:** the structure is unconventional or asymmetrical but justified.

### Confidence

Use:

* High
* Medium
* Low

### Architectural Implication

Explain what the finding changes in the larger map.

### Recommendation

Choose:

* Necessary
* Advisable
* Optional
* Not recommended
* Leave unchanged

# Decision Test

Before recommending a structural intervention, answer:

1. What is the structural unit?
2. What function does it perform?
3. Is that function unique?
4. What other unit performs a similar function?
5. Is the overlap productive or redundant?
6. What analytical level is involved?
7. Is the level appropriate?
8. Are domains functioning as cartographic zones?
9. Are assemblages relational configurations?
10. Are nodes concentrations rather than themes?
11. Are inter-stem nodes supported by transformations?
12. Are connections genuinely relational?
13. Are propagations supported by transformed conditions?
14. Is there hidden hierarchy?
15. Is sequence being confused with causality?
16. Is classification becoming more rigid than the mapped phenomenon?
17. Is asymmetry meaningful?
18. Are boundaries porous?
19. Is the observer's construction visible?
20. Does the architecture preserve the distinction between map and territory?
21. Does the architecture make relational dynamics visible?
22. Does the structure preserve contingency?
23. Does the ending close the work without closing the rhizome?
24. Would the proposed intervention increase cartographic precision?
25. Or would it merely make the architecture more orderly?

Only intervene when the structural gain is meaningful.

---

# Final Principle

> **A more orderly text is not necessarily a better map.**

Structural architecture should not aim to make the work perfectly symmetrical, exhaustive, hierarchical, or taxonomic.

Its task is to stabilize enough of the observer's gaze to make relationships recognizable while preserving the fact that the territory exceeds the map.

The correct structural question is therefore not:

> “Can this architecture be made cleaner?”

but:

> **“Does this architecture make the relevant relations, tensions, transformations, crossings, asymmetries, and contingencies more visible without turning the map into a substitute for the rhizome?”**

The map requires structure.

The rhizome exceeds that structure.

A successful architecture keeps both facts visible.
