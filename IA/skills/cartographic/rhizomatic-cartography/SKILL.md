# Rhizomatic Cartography

## Purpose

Analyze whether a work genuinely operates through a **rhizomatic cartographic perspective** rather than merely using the vocabulary of the rhizome.

This Skill evaluates the relationship between:

* rhizome;
* map;
* territory;
* observation;
* relations;
* multiplicity;
* assemblages;
* nodes;
* inter-stem nodes;
* transformations;
* propagation;
* boundaries;
* materiality;
* power;
* contingency;
* observer position.

The central question is not:

> **Does the text mention the rhizome?**

It is:

> **Does the cartographic operation allow a dynamic field of relations, tensions, transformations, and connections to become visible without reducing that field to a fixed classification?**

---

# 1. Governing Principle

The project does **not** claim that AI is itself a rhizome.

The rhizome is used as a way of observing how AI:

* emerges;
* develops;
* connects;
* transforms;
* becomes materially embedded;
* encounters constraints;
* participates in relations;
* produces and receives effects;
* propagates through different configurations.

Therefore:

**rhizome = mode of observation / conceptual perspective**

not:

**rhizome = empirical identity of AI**

Do not collapse this distinction.

---

# 2. Rhizome and Map

The rhizome and the map have different functions.

### Rhizome

A dynamic field of:

* relations;
* tensions;
* transformations;
* connections;
* feedback;
* multiplicities;
* contingencies;
* displacements;
* emergent configurations.

### Map

A relatively stable construction produced by an observer in order to make selected relations and tensions recognizable.

The map:

* selects;
* cuts;
* groups;
* separates;
* classifies;
* names;
* stabilizes;
* emphasizes;
* temporarily fixes.

Therefore:

> **The map is not the territory.**

And:

> **The stability of the map does not imply the stability of the territory.**

---

# 3. Map–Territory Difference

A successful rhizomatic cartography does not attempt to reproduce the territory completely.

A map is necessarily:

* partial;
* selective;
* situated;
* provisional;
* incomplete.

This is not automatically a defect.

The relevant question is:

> **Does the map make its own selectivity sufficiently visible while preserving the dynamism of what it maps?**

Do not demand exhaustive representation.

---

# 4. Cartography Is an Operation

Treat mapping as an active analytical operation.

Identify what the map:

* includes;
* excludes;
* groups;
* separates;
* names;
* stabilizes;
* foregrounds;
* leaves peripheral;
* renders invisible.

Classification is therefore not automatically a discovery of natural categories.

A classification may be:

> **a decision made by the map in order to make a dynamic field temporarily legible.**

This does not mean the classification is arbitrary.

The map can be evidence-based and analytically useful while remaining constructed.

---

# 5. Classification Belongs to the Map

The project distinguishes:

**classification as cartographic operation**

from:

**classification as ontological property of the territory.**

Therefore:

> **The classification belongs to the map, not necessarily to the territory.**

This principle does not imply that the territory lacks structure.

It means that the particular divisions introduced by the observer should not automatically be treated as intrinsic divisions of the field itself.

---

# 6. Dynamic Rhizome, Relatively Stable Map

The apparent tension between a dynamic rhizome and a stable map is not a contradiction.

The rhizome may change while the map temporarily stabilizes an observation.

The map can therefore be:

* stable enough to analyze;
* provisional enough to remain revisable.

The relevant distinction is:

**territorial change ≠ cartographic instability**

and:

**cartographic stability ≠ territorial permanence.**

---

# 7. Domains as Cartographic Orientation

The general domains:

* Science;
* Technology;
* Society;

should be treated as **cartographic orientation tools**, not necessarily as three ontologically separate worlds.

They help orient the observer.

They should not automatically become:

* rigid containers;
* mutually exclusive territories;
* hierarchical levels;
* fixed causal stages.

A domain boundary may be useful precisely because relations cross it.

---

# 8. Assemblages

Assemblages are relatively stable configurations of relations and tensions.

They are not merely collections of elements.

A valid assemblage should contain some combination of:

* elements;
* relations;
* tensions;
* practices;
* institutions;
* infrastructures;
* norms;
* material conditions;
* capacities;
* dependencies.

Assemblages are **porous**.

Their boundaries are analytical rather than necessarily absolute.

---

# 9. Nodes as Cartographic Concentrations

A node is not simply an important topic.

It is:

> **a concentration of relations and tensions that forms a recognizable dynamic.**

The cartographic task is therefore not:

> "What topics does this text contain?"

but:

> **"Where do relations and tensions become sufficiently concentrated to form a recognizable dynamic?"**

Do not create nodes simply to make the architecture look organized.

---

# 10. Inter-Stem Nodes

An inter-stem node is a stronger cartographic claim.

It must:

1. involve distinct assemblages;
2. cross their boundaries;
3. contain a concentration of relations and tensions;
4. modify conditions of functioning;
5. produce a transformation;
6. potentially propagate.

The decisive sequence is:

**assemblages → relations → tensions → concentration → modified conditions → transformation → possible propagation**

Interdisciplinarity alone is insufficient.

---

# 11. Rhizomaticity Is Not "Everything Connects"

A common failure is to reduce the rhizome to universal connectivity.

Do not assume:

* every element connects to every other;
* every relation is equally important;
* every boundary is false;
* every node must connect to every node;
* every connection must propagate;
* every difference must be erased.

A rhizomatic field may contain:

* concentrations;
* gaps;
* asymmetries;
* bottlenecks;
* exclusions;
* dependencies;
* discontinuities;
* interruptions;
* dead ends.

Multiplicity does not imply uniform connectivity.

---

# 12. Multiplicity Is Not Horizontality

A rhizomatic architecture can contain:

* power concentrations;
* monopolies;
* dependencies;
* institutional asymmetries;
* infrastructural bottlenecks;
* unequal access.

Do not infer:

**multiplicity → horizontality**

or:

**non-hierarchy → equality.**

The presence of many connections can coexist with strong concentrations of power.

---

# 13. Connectivity Is Not Equality

Analyze whether the map accidentally represents the field as more egalitarian than the evidence supports.

Ask:

* Who controls infrastructure?
* Who controls access?
* Where are dependencies concentrated?
* Which relations are asymmetric?
* Which actors can modify conditions for others?
* Which actors are constrained?

Rhizomatic cartography should reveal concentrations of power rather than conceal them behind network language.

---

# 14. Connection, Propagation, Transformation

Keep these distinct.

### Connection

A relation exists.

### Transformation

A condition or configuration changes.

### Propagation

A transformation extends through relations and changes conditions elsewhere.

Therefore:

**connection ≠ transformation ≠ propagation**

A map that contains many connections has not necessarily demonstrated a rhizomatic dynamic.

---

# 15. Feedback

Look for feedback circuits.

Examples include:

**technology → changed practice → new data → new technology → changed practice**

or:

**AI deployment → changed evaluation practices → new data/criteria → modified AI systems → further evaluation changes**

Feedback can produce:

* recursive modification;
* amplification;
* attenuation;
* adaptation;
* path dependence.

Do not call every reciprocal relation feedback.

A feedback circuit requires a return effect that modifies subsequent states of the system.

---

# 16. Nonlinearity

Rhizomatic cartography should remain open to:

* multiple causes;
* multiple effects;
* branching;
* reciprocal influence;
* delayed effects;
* threshold effects;
* interruptions;
* contingent pathways.

But do not equate:

**rhizomatic → nonlinear**

automatically.

A valid linear causal relation can exist inside a larger rhizomatic field.

The question is whether the linear relation is mistakenly presented as the whole field.

---

# 17. Causality Within a Rhizomatic Field

Rhizomatic analysis does not require abandoning causality.

It requires avoiding causal reductionism.

Preserve causal relations when supported.

Distinguish:

* causation;
* contribution;
* enablement;
* constraint;
* mediation;
* feedback;
* transformation;
* propagation.

A text can therefore say:

> X contributed to Y

without becoming technologically deterministic.

Coordinate with:

`causality-propagation`

and:

`technological-determinism`.

---

# 18. Contingency

A rhizomatic map should not transform possibility into inevitability.

Check whether the text recognizes:

* choices;
* investments;
* regulation;
* standards;
* institutional decisions;
* accidents;
* resistance;
* material constraints;
* technical limitations;
* adoption;
* non-adoption;
* alternative trajectories.

Distinguish:

**technical possibility ≠ historical inevitability**

and:

**capacity ≠ destiny.**

---

# 19. Materiality

A rhizomatic cartography should not become purely conceptual when material conditions are analytically relevant.

Possible material substrates include:

* energy;
* water;
* minerals;
* semiconductors;
* compute;
* data centers;
* networks;
* devices;
* storage;
* logistics;
* labor;
* supply chains.

The substrate is not merely background.

It can:

* enable;
* constrain;
* accelerate;
* slow;
* generate dependency;
* create strategic tension;
* redistribute power.

Materiality should be included when it actually participates in the dynamics.

---

# 20. Power as Cartographic Structure

Power should be mapped relationally.

Look for:

* control;
* dependency;
* concentration;
* exclusion;
* access;
* authority;
* infrastructure;
* standards;
* resource distribution;
* institutional capacity.

Do not assume that decentralized appearance means decentralized power.

A rhizomatic field may contain highly concentrated control points.

---

# 21. Observer Position

The observer is part of the conditions under which the map exists.

The observer:

* selects;
* categorizes;
* names;
* stabilizes;
* excludes;
* emphasizes;
* determines scale;
* decides what counts as a node.

But this does not imply that the map is merely a projection of the observer.

Maintain the distinction:

**observer construction ≠ arbitrary invention**

and:

**situated observation ≠ epistemic relativism.**

Coordinate with:

`map-territory`

and:

`observer-implication`.

---

# 22. Reflexivity

A strong rhizomatic cartography can become reflexive when the act of mapping becomes part of what is mapped.

For example:

**AI is analyzed → AI participates in constructing the analysis → that participation becomes part of the assemblage → the map changes how AI participation is understood.**

This creates a recursive observer–object relation.

Do not force reflexivity into every text.

It is relevant when the observer's operation materially affects the analytical object or its representation.

---

# 23. AI Writing and the Map

If AI participated in producing the work, analyze whether that participation affects:

* selection;
* organization;
* conceptual connections;
* wording;
* testing;
* criticism;
* discovery;
* map construction;
* observer position.

Do not automatically conclude that AI is:

* co-author;
* autonomous subject;
* intentional agent;
* conscious participant.

The relevant cartographic question is:

> **Did AI participation alter the conditions under which the map was constructed?**

Coordinate with:

`ai-writing-reflexivity`.

---

# 24. Boundaries

A rhizomatic map requires boundaries to be analytically useful.

Do not assume:

> boundaries = anti-rhizomatic.

Boundaries can:

* make differences visible;
* identify crossings;
* reveal tensions;
* enable comparison;
* show transformations.

The problem occurs when boundaries are treated as:

* absolute;
* naturalized;
* impermeable;
* permanently fixed.

A productive boundary can make a crossing visible.

---

# 25. Scale

Rhizomatic cartography operates across scales.

Possible scales include:

* micro;
* meso;
* macro;
* local;
* institutional;
* infrastructural;
* geopolitical;
* historical.

Do not assume that movement across scale is automatic.

A macro claim requires an account of how the transformation reaches that scale.

Coordinate with:

`scale-analysis`.

---

# 26. Temporal Dynamics

A rhizomatic map is not necessarily timeless.

Analyze:

* emergence;
* persistence;
* acceleration;
* stabilization;
* transformation;
* decay;
* interruption;
* recurrence;
* path dependence.

The map may represent a moment of a moving field.

Therefore:

> **a map can stabilize a temporal slice without claiming that the slice is permanent.**

---

# 27. Map Revision

Because the territory changes, the map may become inadequate.

Ask:

* What would require the map to be redrawn?
* Which relations are provisional?
* Which nodes may split?
* Which nodes may merge?
* Which boundaries may move?
* Which transformations could create new concentrations?

This does not mean the map must be continuously rewritten.

It means its stability should remain understood as provisional.

---

# 28. Cartographic Reduction

Every map reduces.

Reduction is not automatically failure.

The relevant questions are:

* What does the reduction make visible?
* What does it obscure?
* What does it exclude?
* Does it erase a relation necessary to understand the dynamic?
* Does it turn a fluid relation into a fixed category?
* Does it transform a provisional boundary into an ontological claim?

A useful map is selective without becoming misleading.

---

# 29. Cartographic Excess

The opposite problem is excessive inclusion.

A map may become so comprehensive that:

* everything becomes connected;
* distinctions disappear;
* no node has analytical function;
* no relation has priority;
* every phenomenon becomes part of every other phenomenon.

This produces apparent multiplicity without analytical precision.

Do not confuse:

**more elements → better cartography**

or:

**more connections → more rhizomatic analysis.**

---

# 30. Hidden Tree Structures

Check whether a supposedly rhizomatic architecture secretly becomes a tree.

Warning signs:

* rigid parent-child hierarchy;
* fixed levels of importance;
* one central trunk;
* predetermined causal progression;
* A–E treated as stages;
* each node assigned a single exclusive territory;
* inter-stem nodes treated as branches from a central root.

A structured architecture is not automatically a tree.

The question is whether the structure permits:

* crossing;
* recursion;
* multiple relations;
* asymmetry;
* feedback;
* provisional boundaries.

---

# 31. Hidden Taxonomy

A classification becomes problematic when categories are treated as exhaustive natural kinds without justification.

Test whether:

* domains are treated as mutually exclusive;
* assemblages are treated as closed;
* nodes are treated as permanent;
* inter-stem nodes are treated as fixed categories;
* every phenomenon is forced into one location.

The map should remain capable of recognizing overlap and displacement.

---

# 32. Rhizomaticity and Order

Do not equate:

**order = hierarchy**

or:

**chaos = rhizome.**

A map can be highly organized while remaining rhizomatic in its conceptual operation.

Likewise, fragmented writing is not automatically rhizomatic.

The criterion is relational architecture, not visual disorder.

---

# 33. Rhizomaticity and Symmetry

Do not force equal size, length, evidence, or conceptual weight across nodes.

A rhizomatic map may contain:

* dense regions;
* sparse regions;
* major concentrations;
* minor concentrations;
* strong relations;
* weak relations;
* central-looking but structurally dependent elements.

Asymmetry can be analytically meaningful.

Coordinate with:

`asymmetry-analysis`.

---

# 34. Rhizomaticity and Emergence

Do not call every new phenomenon emergent.

Emergence requires a relational configuration whose properties cannot be adequately attributed to a single element alone.

Coordinate with:

`emergence-analysis`.

---

# 35. Rhizomaticity and Anthropomorphism

Do not use the rhizome as justification for attributing agency or mentality to AI.

The following remain distinct:

* causal participation;
* functional behavior;
* operational autonomy;
* actor status;
* intentional agency;
* understanding;
* consciousness.

Coordinate with:

`anthropomorphism-audit`.

---

# 36. Rhizomaticity and Technological Determinism

A rhizomatic perspective should resist narratives in which technology becomes:

* sufficient cause;
* exclusive cause;
* autonomous historical actor;
* inevitable driver.

But do not overcorrect by denying genuine technological effects.

The relevant question is:

> **Through which relations, institutions, infrastructures, practices, material conditions, and contingencies does technological change become consequential?**

Coordinate with:

`technological-determinism`.

---

# 37. Negative Space

Analyze what the map does **not** connect.

Absences can matter when they reveal:

* excluded relations;
* blind spots;
* missing material conditions;
* unexamined power;
* unexplored scale transitions;
* assumptions treated as external.

However:

> absence is not automatically evidence of omission.

Only identify a cartographic blind spot when the text provides sufficient reason to expect the relation to matter.

---

# 38. Cartographic Precision

The goal is not maximal complexity.

The goal is:

> **making the relational field more visible without pretending to exhaust it.**

A good map should allow the reader to see:

* where relations concentrate;
* where tensions intensify;
* where boundaries are crossed;
* where conditions change;
* where transformations occur;
* where power concentrates;
* where material constraints matter;
* where uncertainty remains.

---

# 39. Counterfactual Cartographic Tests

### Remove the Rhizome

Would the work become a conventional linear account of AI?

### Remove the Map

Would the architecture lose its account of selection and provisional stabilization?

### Replace Nodes with Topics

Would the architecture lose relational dynamics?

### Remove Inter-Stem Nodes

Would meaningful cross-assemblage transformations disappear?

### Flatten Asymmetries

Would analytical information be lost?

### Make Every Connection Explicit

Would the map become less precise rather than more?

### Treat All Categories as Natural

Would the map falsely stabilize the territory?

---

# 40. Cartographic Failure Modes

Identify if present:

### Decorative Rhizome

The term "rhizome" appears, but the analysis remains linear.

### Universal Connectivity

Everything is connected to everything.

### False Horizontality

Multiplicity is presented as equality.

### Static Territory

The map is treated as permanent reality.

### Ontological Classification

Map categories are treated as natural divisions.

### Taxonomic Capture

The architecture becomes a rigid classification.

### Deterministic Rhizome

Rhizomatic language hides an inevitable causal narrative.

### Romantic Rhizome

The rhizome is assumed to be inherently liberating, decentralized, or positive.

### Network Reduction

Rhizome is reduced to a generic network metaphor.

### Complexity Inflation

More entities and connections are mistaken for more rhizomaticity.

### Chaos Equivalence

Fragmentation or disorder is mistaken for rhizomatic structure.

---

# 41. Evidence Standard

For each cartographic diagnosis distinguish:

* what the text explicitly maps;
* what relations are strongly inferable;
* what the analyst reconstructs;
* what remains speculative.

Prefer direct textual evidence for claims about:

* architecture;
* classification;
* nodes;
* observer position;
* boundaries;
* transformations.

Do not infer hidden rhizomatic structure merely because the topic is complex.

# 44. Final Cartographic Test

Before concluding that the work is genuinely rhizomatic, ask:

1. Does the text distinguish rhizome from map?
2. Does it distinguish map from territory?
3. Is the map understood as constructed and provisional?
4. Are classifications treated as cartographic operations rather than automatically natural divisions?
5. Are domains used as orientation rather than rigid ontology?
6. Are assemblages understood as porous configurations?
7. Are nodes relational concentrations rather than topics?
8. Are inter-stem nodes validated through transformation rather than interdisciplinarity?
9. Are connections distinguished from transformations and propagations?
10. Are asymmetries preserved?
11. Are power concentrations visible?
12. Are material conditions included where relevant?
13. Is contingency preserved?
14. Is causality retained where valid without becoming deterministic?
15. Is emergence not inferred merely from complexity?
16. Is AI not anthropomorphized?
17. Is the observer's position acknowledged without collapsing the map into subjectivity?
18. Can the map be revised without destroying the architecture?
19. Does the map make dynamics visible rather than merely classify topics?
20. Does the architecture preserve multiplicity without becoming analytically indiscriminate?

---

# 45. Final Principle

> **A rhizomatic cartography does not reproduce the rhizome. It constructs a provisional map through which some of the rhizome's relations, tensions, transformations, and concentrations become visible.**

> **The map must be stable enough to be read and provisional enough not to mistake its own categories for the territory.**

> **Rhizomatic analysis is not the claim that everything connects to everything. It is the attempt to make visible how relations connect, where they concentrate, how they transform conditions, where they cross boundaries, and what remains outside any particular map.**

> **The goal is neither chaos nor taxonomy. It is cartographic precision adequate to a changing field.**

> **The rhizome remains in motion. The map stabilizes the gaze. Good cartography keeps that difference visible.**
