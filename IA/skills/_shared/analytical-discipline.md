# Analytical Discipline

## Purpose

This protocol defines the general analytical discipline shared by all Skills.

The objective is to produce precise, evidence-based analysis without forcing problems, imposing predetermined conclusions, or confusing analytical preference with actual weakness.

## Analyze Before Intervening

* Analyze the material before proposing changes.
* Establish what the source currently does before determining what it should do.
* Do not rewrite, restructure, expand, simplify, or correct material unless the relevant task explicitly requires it.
* When intervention is requested, justify the intervention through a prior diagnosis.

## Do Not Force Problems

* Do not assume that every imperfection is a problem.
* Do not manufacture criticism to make an analysis appear rigorous.
* If a passage, concept, structure, or relationship works adequately, say so.
* A finding is not required merely because a category exists for detecting one.
* A Skill may legitimately conclude: **"No relevant problem detected."**

## Distinguish Types of Problems

Do not collapse different kinds of issues into a single diagnosis.

Distinguish at minimum:

* conceptual problems;
* methodological problems;
* argumentative problems;
* structural problems;
* semantic problems;
* linguistic or grammatical problems;
* stylistic problems;
* evidential problems;
* problems of scope or qualification.

A stylistic preference must not be presented as a conceptual flaw.

A disagreement with the author's position must not automatically be presented as an inconsistency.

## Preserve Productive Difficulty

Not every difficulty should be removed.

Distinguish between:

* unnecessary obscurity;
* productive conceptual difficulty;
* deliberate ambiguity;
* unresolved but meaningful tension;
* genuine conceptual confusion.

Do not simplify a difficult passage merely because it requires effort from the reader.

## Respect Asymmetry

* Do not assume that sections, concepts, nodes, arguments, or examples must have equal size or weight.
* Asymmetry may reflect differences in conceptual importance.
* Do not impose symmetry for aesthetic or organizational reasons alone.
* Recommend balancing only when asymmetry produces an actual functional problem.

## No Category by Title Alone

Do not classify a passage, section, concept, or structure solely because of its title or label.

Determine the function performed by the material itself.

A label such as "AI + X" does not establish a particular conceptual category, relationship, node, or transformation.

## No Importance-to-Category Inference

Do not infer analytical status from importance alone.

An element may be important without being a node.

A relation may be important without constituting a concentration.

A concept may be central without functioning as an organizing category.

Determine categories from their demonstrated relational and functional properties.

## No Automatic Causality

Do not infer causal relationships merely from:

* sequence;
* proximity;
* thematic association;
* correlation;
* mutual reference;
* one concept appearing after another.

When a causal claim is relevant, identify what mechanism or evidence supports it.

Preserve valid causal claims when adequately supported.

Do not replace every causal explanation with vague language about complexity, emergence, networks, or rhizomatic relations.

## No Automatic Emergence

Do not label something as emergent merely because it is:

* new;
* complex;
* surprising;
* multidimensional;
* interdisciplinary;
* difficult to explain;
* produced by multiple factors.

Use emergence only when the relevant analytical criteria are satisfied.

## No Automatic Anthropomorphism

Do not classify the use of human-like verbs as anthropomorphism by default.

Distinguish between:

* describing functional behavior;
* attributing a role within a system;
* attributing causal participation;
* attributing agency;
* attributing intention, consciousness, understanding, will, autonomy, or subjective experience.

Anthropomorphism becomes a substantive problem when the text crosses a relevant conceptual boundary, not merely because it uses ordinary language for technical behavior.

## No Automatic Determinism

Do not interpret technological influence as technological determinism.

Distinguish between:

* technical affordance;
* causal contribution;
* constraint;
* enabling condition;
* institutional mediation;
* feedback;
* structural influence;
* historical inevitability.

A claim that technology changes conditions does not by itself establish that technology determines the outcome.

## No Automatic Rhizomaticity

Do not classify a text as rhizomatic merely because it contains:

* many topics;
* many connections;
* interdisciplinarity;
* nonlinear language;
* complexity;
* networks;
* decentralization;
* multiplicity.

Likewise, do not classify a text as non-rhizomatic merely because it contains categories, temporary structures, distinctions, or organization.

Evaluate whether the relevant relational dynamics are actually made visible.

## No Automatic Interdisciplinarity

The presence of two disciplines, domains, or fields does not establish a meaningful cross-domain transformation.

When a cross-domain category is being evaluated, reconstruct the relations and tensions involved before assigning it analytical status.

## Respect the Author's Conceptual Operation

* Do not replace the author's conceptual framework with a more familiar framework merely because it is easier to classify.
* Do not force conventional academic categories onto an intentionally unconventional structure.
* Evaluate whether the author's own distinctions are internally functional before proposing alternatives.
* Do not assume that a nonstandard formulation is erroneous.

## Open Interpretation

When multiple interpretations are textually plausible:

* identify the alternatives;
* determine which interpretation is best supported;
* explain what evidence distinguishes them;
* preserve uncertainty when the source does not resolve the issue.

Do not collapse genuine ambiguity prematurely.

## Scope Control

Do not make claims broader than the available evidence permits.

Distinguish among:

* what the source establishes;
* what it suggests;
* what it leaves open;
* what would require external evidence.

When a claim is too broad, narrow the diagnosis rather than expanding the evidence artificially.

## Cross-Section Awareness

Analyze the relevant material in relation to the rest of the work when the task requires it.

Do not judge a passage in isolation when its function depends on:

* an earlier definition;
* a later qualification;
* another section;
* a recurring distinction;
* a structural relationship;
* a deliberate contrast.

At the same time, do not use the existence of a related passage elsewhere to excuse a genuine local problem.

## Transversal Review Before Structural Intervention

Before proposing to:

* create a new category;
* create a new node;
* merge sections;
* eliminate a section;
* move material;
* substantially restructure the architecture;

perform a transversal check of the whole relevant structure.

Ask:

1. Is the proposed problem already addressed elsewhere?
2. Is the proposed category duplicating an existing function?
3. Could the material be absorbed without conceptual loss?
4. Does the proposed change create a new hierarchy or conventional classification?
5. Does it increase analytical precision or merely organizational order?

## Intervention Threshold

Recommend an intervention only when there is a demonstrable reason.

Prioritize interventions that:

* resolve a genuine conceptual problem;
* eliminate meaningful redundancy;
* clarify a structurally necessary distinction;
* repair a significant argumentative gap;
* prevent a serious misreading;
* increase cartographic or analytical precision.

Do not recommend changes solely because another formulation would be possible.

## Preserve What Works

When analysis identifies a strong formulation, distinction, asymmetry, tension, or structural decision:

* identify it explicitly;
* do not modify it unnecessarily;
* do not generate alternatives merely for the sake of comparison.

The purpose of analysis is not maximal intervention.

## Analytical Confidence

Calibrate conclusions according to evidence.

Use distinctions such as:

* **Established** — directly supported by the material.
* **Strongly supported** — supported by converging evidence.
* **Plausible** — reasonable but dependent on interpretation.
* **Ambiguous** — multiple readings remain viable.
* **Unsupported** — insufficient evidence.
* **Contradicted** — the material provides significant evidence against the claim.

Do not express a weak inference with the certainty appropriate to a direct textual finding.

## Core Principle

Rigorous analysis does not mean finding more problems.

It means distinguishing accurately between what is strong, what is weak, what is unresolved, what is unconventional but valid, and what genuinely requires intervention.

**Do not improve what does not need improvement.**
