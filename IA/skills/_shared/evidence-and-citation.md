# Evidence and Citation

## Purpose

This protocol defines how analytical Skills distinguish source evidence, analytical inference, external evidence, and unsupported claims.

The purpose is to ensure that conclusions are traceable to the material being analyzed and that the analyst does not attribute to the source what is actually an interpretation.

## Source Evidence

Treat as source evidence:

* explicit statements made in the source;
* relevant passages, formulations, examples, distinctions, definitions, and transitions;
* structural features that can be directly observed in the source;
* explicit relationships between concepts, sections, actors, elements, or arguments;
* repetitions, omissions, contrasts, qualifications, and changes that are demonstrable from the text.

When a finding depends on wording or structure, identify the relevant passage or location.

Do not present an interpretation as though it were an explicit statement by the author.

## Analytical Inference

Distinguish clearly between:

* **What the source says**
* **What follows reasonably from the source**
* **What the analyst interprets**
* **What remains uncertain**

An inference may be legitimate without being explicitly stated in the source, but it must be presented as an inference.

Use calibrated language when appropriate:

* "The text states..."
* "The passage suggests..."
* "This can be read as..."
* "This appears to imply..."
* "A possible consequence is..."
* "The evidence is insufficient to establish..."

Avoid presenting speculative interpretations as established facts.

## Evidence Strength

Assess evidence according to its relation to the claim.

### Direct evidence

The source explicitly states or clearly demonstrates the relevant point.

### Strong inferential evidence

The point is not explicitly stated, but multiple textual elements converge on the interpretation.

### Weak inferential evidence

The interpretation is plausible but depends substantially on assumptions not established by the source.

### Insufficient evidence

The available material does not support the claim adequately.

Do not strengthen a claim merely because it is philosophically plausible.

## External Evidence

Use external evidence only when the task requires factual verification, contextualization, empirical assessment, or evaluation of a claim that cannot be established from the source alone.

When external sources are used:

* distinguish them from the source being analyzed;
* prefer authoritative and relevant sources;
* use current sources when the claim is time-sensitive;
* do not introduce external information as though it were contained in the source;
* distinguish empirical evidence from philosophical interpretation;
* identify when an external source supports, qualifies, or challenges the source's claim.

External evidence should support the analysis, not replace close reading.

## Citation Discipline

Citations must support the specific claim for which they are provided.

Do not:

* cite a source merely because it concerns the same topic;
* use a citation to give an appearance of authority to an unsupported interpretation;
* cite secondary commentary when a primary or authoritative source is reasonably available;
* attach one citation to a paragraph containing multiple unrelated claims that it does not support;
* present a source's interpretation as an established fact without qualification.

When the evidence supports only part of a statement, narrow the statement rather than overstating what the citation establishes.

## Source vs. Analyst

Maintain a clear distinction between:

**Source**

* what the document explicitly contains;
* what its structure demonstrably does;
* what its wording establishes.

**Analyst**

* interpretation;
* comparison;
* conceptual reconstruction;
* criticism;
* categorization;
* hypotheses about implications.

The analyst may reconstruct relationships that the author does not explicitly name, but the reconstruction must remain identifiable as analytical work.

## Absence and Omission

Do not treat the absence of a statement as evidence that the author rejects or denies that position.

A missing element may be:

* intentionally omitted;
* implicitly assumed;
* outside the scope of the text;
* undeveloped;
* genuinely absent.

Only make stronger claims about omission when the structure or context provides evidence for them.

## Negative Findings

Negative findings require particular caution.

Prefer:

* "The text does not establish..."
* "No explicit evidence was found for..."
* "The argument does not currently demonstrate..."
* "The connection remains underdeveloped..."

over categorical claims such as:

* "The author has no argument..."
* "The text completely ignores..."
* "There is no relationship..."

unless the available material genuinely justifies such certainty.

## Evidence Before Correction

Before recommending a correction, establish:

1. What the text currently says.
2. What evidence supports the interpretation.
3. What specific problem has been identified.
4. Why the problem matters.
5. Whether the problem is conceptual, methodological, argumentative, structural, semantic, or stylistic.
6. Whether the proposed intervention is actually necessary.

Do not recommend changes merely because an alternative formulation is possible.

## No Invented Evidence

Never:

* invent passages;
* attribute claims to the source that are not present;
* fabricate citations;
* assume evidence that has not been provided;
* fill gaps in the source with imagined arguments;
* use previous versions of a text as evidence for the current version unless explicitly authorized.

If evidence is unavailable, state the limitation.

## Core Principle

Every substantive analytical claim should be traceable to one of three things:

1. **Evidence in the source**
2. **Explicitly identified analytical inference**
3. **Reliable external evidence**

If none of these supports the claim adequately, do not present it as established.
