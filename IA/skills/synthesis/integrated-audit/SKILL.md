# Integrated Audit

## Purpose

Synthesize the findings produced by multiple analytical Skills into a coherent diagnosis of the work as a whole.

This Skill does not perform all individual audits again.

Its purpose is to determine:

* which findings matter;
* which findings overlap;
* which findings are independent;
* which findings affect the architecture;
* which findings are local;
* which tensions are productive;
* which problems require intervention;
* which apparent problems should be preserved;
* whether the work is sufficiently consolidated for final editing.

The central question is:

> **What does the complete set of findings reveal about the integrity, precision, and functioning of the work as a whole?**

---

# Core Principle

Integration is not accumulation.

Twenty local findings do not necessarily constitute twenty problems.

Several findings may arise from one underlying issue.

Conversely, a single local issue may have architectural consequences.

Therefore:

> **The integrated audit must reorganize findings according to their structural significance, not simply reproduce them in sequence.**

---

# Scope

Synthesize findings concerning:

* conceptual coherence;
* methodological coherence;
* cartographic architecture;
* assemblages;
* nodes;
* inter-stem nodes;
* connections;
* propagations;
* causality;
* emergence;
* scale;
* materiality;
* power;
* contingency;
* map/territory;
* observer implication;
* AI-writing reflexivity;
* philosophical claims;
* knowledge/evidence/authority;
* voice;
* rhythm;
* reader experience;
* originality;
* ending;
* redundancy;
* asymmetry;
* contradiction;
* vulnerability.

The integrated audit operates across the entire work.

---

# What This Skill Does Not Do

Do not:

* simply concatenate Skill outputs;
* count findings;
* average severity scores;
* create artificial numerical precision;
* reopen every settled question without evidence;
* introduce new nodes merely because a gap exists;
* redesign the architecture automatically;
* rewrite the work;
* normalize asymmetry;
* eliminate productive tensions;
* treat every recommendation as mandatory.

The integrated audit is a **diagnostic synthesis**, not an automatic revision engine.

---

# Inputs

The Skill may receive findings from:

* atomic Skills;
* composite Skills;
* individual rounds of the 39-round audit;
* previous analytical passes explicitly supplied by the user.

Treat findings as analytical evidence.

Do not assume that every finding is equally reliable.

---

# Evidence Hierarchy

Prioritize:

1. direct textual evidence;
2. repeated structural evidence;
3. convergent findings from independent Skills;
4. strong inferential findings;
5. plausible interpretations;
6. speculative possibilities.

A finding supported only by stylistic impression should not override a directly supported conceptual distinction.

---

# Step 1 — Inventory the Findings

Collect findings by domain:

* conceptual;
* methodological;
* structural;
* argumentative;
* semantic;
* evidential;
* causal;
* cartographic;
* philosophical;
* stylistic;
* reader-oriented;
* authorship/reflexivity.

Do not assume that categories are mutually exclusive.

---

# Step 2 — Identify Convergent Findings

Determine when multiple Skills identify the same underlying issue.

For example:

* `semantic-precision` detects excessive certainty;
* `falsifiability-vulnerability` detects insufficient support;
* `technological-determinism` detects inevitability;
* `causality-propagation` detects excessive causal strength.

These may constitute one underlying problem:

> **The formulation attributes more necessity to the technological process than the argument establishes.**

Do not report four independent architectural problems unless they actually are four.

---

# Step 3 — Identify Independent Findings

Determine which findings remain conceptually distinct.

For example:

* an anthropomorphic formulation;
* a redundant paragraph;
* a missing transition;
* an underdeveloped inter-stem transformation.

They may coexist without sharing one cause.

Preserve independent findings.

---

# Step 4 — Separate Local from Architectural Problems

Ask:

> If this issue were corrected locally, would the architecture remain essentially unchanged?

If yes:

> **Local issue.**

If correction would require changing:

* node boundaries;
* inter-stem status;
* domain organization;
* conceptual definitions;
* major relations;
* trajectory;
* ending;
* map logic;

then it may be architectural.

Do not inflate local problems into architectural ones.

---

# Step 5 — Test Severity

Use:

* **A — Critical:** compromises a fundamental conceptual, methodological, or architectural operation.
* **B — Important:** materially weakens an important part of the work.
* **C — Local:** limited correction improves precision or functioning.
* **D — Stylistic:** primarily affects expression or reading experience.
* **E — Non-problematic:** unusual, asymmetrical, difficult, or unconventional but valid.

Severity concerns consequences, not quantity.

---

# Step 6 — Identify Root Problems

Search for underlying causes.

Potential root problems include:

* conceptual drift;
* hidden hierarchy;
* category confusion;
* insufficient distinction between relation and causality;
* insufficient evidence;
* excessive generalization;
* map/territory collapse;
* node-as-theme;
* inter-stem-as-topic;
* propagation-as-diffusion;
* emergence-as-novelty;
* technical possibility as inevitability;
* AI behavior as intention;
* production as knowledge;
* relevance as authority;
* authority as truth;
* observer implication remaining merely declarative.

A root problem may explain several local findings.

---

# Step 7 — Identify Productive Tensions

Do not eliminate every tension.

Test whether apparent contradictions correspond to productive oppositions such as:

* map ↔ territory;
* stability ↔ transformation;
* classification ↔ multiplicity;
* order ↔ rhizomaticity;
* causality ↔ distributed relations;
* agency ↔ non-anthropomorphism;
* material constraint ↔ social construction;
* openness ↔ structure;
* power ↔ multiplicity;
* technical capacity ↔ contingency;
* emergence ↔ explanation;
* observer participation ↔ analytical distance.

If the tension generates analytical work, preserve it.

---

# Step 8 — Test Architectural Integrity

Reconstruct the actual architecture:

> **Rhizome → General Domains → Assemblages → Nodes → Inter-Stem Nodes → Transformations / Propagations**

Treat this as a cartographic architecture, not a hierarchy.

Verify:

* domains function as orientation zones;
* assemblages are configurations rather than topic containers;
* nodes are concentrations of relations/tensions;
* inter-stem nodes cross assemblages through transformations;
* connections are relational rather than merely thematic;
* propagation identifies transformed conditions;
* A–E do not become a causal chain.

---

# Step 9 — Validate A–E as a Set

Evaluate A–E collectively.

Current architecture:

### A — AI + Philosophy of Mind

**Transformation:** observable capacities → attribution of mental capacities

### B — AI + Biology

**Transformation:** representation → intervention

### C — AI + Geopolitics, Sovereignty, and Materiality

**Transformation:** technical capacity → strategic capacity

### D — AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority

**Transformation:** information mediation → relevance and authority

### E — AI + Knowledge Evaluation

**Transformation:** production → evidence → attribution

Do not assume that the sequence A–E expresses:

* chronology;
* causality;
* increasing importance;
* hierarchy;
* progressive development.

Test whether each node has a distinct analytical function.

---

# Step 10 — Test Node Differentiation

For each node ask:

1. What relations concentrate here?
2. What tensions concentrate here?
3. What transformation does it make visible?
4. What does it contribute that another node does not?
5. What would be lost if it were removed?
6. Could it be absorbed elsewhere without conceptual loss?

A node that survives only because its topic is important is insufficiently justified.

---

# Step 11 — Test Inter-Stem Integrity

For every inter-stem node verify:

1. identifiable assemblages;
2. relations between them;
3. tensions;
4. concentration;
5. modification of conditions;
6. transformation;
7. possible propagation.

Reject:

> “X is related to AI”

as sufficient evidence.

The decisive question is:

> **What changes in the functioning of the assemblages because of the relation?**

---

# Step 12 — Test Connections

Construct a qualitative relational picture.

Current important connections may include:

* A ↔ E;
* B → C;
* C ↔ D;
* D ↔ E;
* A → D, more weakly.

These are not required to form a complete network.

Do not add missing connections simply to make the architecture look more interconnected.

---

# Step 13 — Test Propagations

For every claimed propagation ask:

* what transforms;
* where it starts;
* through what relation it moves;
* what condition changes;
* where it arrives;
* what changes there;
* whether the transformation itself changes;
* whether feedback occurs.

Connection alone is insufficient.

Potential propagation is not established propagation.

---

# Step 14 — Test Causality

Determine whether the integrated architecture contains hidden linear chains.

Especially test whether:

> A → B → C → D → E

is implied.

Distinguish:

* causal influence;
* contribution;
* enablement;
* constraint;
* mediation;
* feedback;
* distributed causality;
* propagation.

A valid causal relation should be preserved.

The objective is not to eliminate causality.

---

# Step 15 — Test Emergence

Identify whether the work invokes emergence.

For each significant emergence claim ask:

* What emerges?
* From what configuration?
* Through which relations?
* Why is it not adequately reducible to one element?
* What transformation occurs?
* What role do feedback, scale, thresholds, or nonlinearity play?

Do not retain “emergence” merely as a synonym for novelty.

---

# Step 16 — Test Materiality

Determine whether material conditions are:

* constitutive;
* enabling;
* constraining;
* infrastructural;
* energetic;
* geopolitical;
* logistical.

Relevant conditions may include:

* energy;
* water;
* minerals;
* semiconductors;
* compute;
* data centers;
* networks;
* devices;
* logistics;
* storage.

Ask whether materiality changes the analysis or merely appears as background vocabulary.

---

# Step 17 — Test Power

Analyze whether the architecture makes visible:

* concentration;
* dependency;
* asymmetry;
* control;
* institutional power;
* infrastructural power;
* epistemic authority;
* visibility;
* selection.

Do not assume that a rhizomatic field is necessarily horizontal.

Do not assume that multiplicity eliminates concentration.

---

# Step 18 — Test Contingency

Check whether the work preserves:

* historical contingency;
* institutional decisions;
* investment;
* regulation;
* standards;
* accidents;
* reuse;
* constraints;
* path dependence.

Technical capacity should not automatically become historical inevitability.

---

# Step 19 — Test Map and Territory

Determine whether the work consistently distinguishes:

> **dynamic territory**

from:

> **provisionally stabilized map.**

Check whether:

* classification is recognized as cartographic;
* boundaries remain provisional;
* categories do not become naturalized;
* map stability is not mistaken for territorial stability;
* the rhizome exceeds the map.

---

# Step 20 — Test Observer Implication

Ask whether the observer is genuinely implicated.

Do not accept a methodological disclaimer alone.

Look for actual consequences in:

* selection;
* classification;
* exclusions;
* node construction;
* map stabilization;
* interpretation;
* AI-assisted writing.

The observer should be situated without becoming the sole source of reality.

---

# Step 21 — Test AI-Writing Reflexivity

If AI participated in the writing process, determine whether that participation has analytical consequence.

Possible loop:

> AI participates in constructing the map → AI is part of the object being mapped → the construction process becomes evidence concerning cognition, mediation, authorship, or observation.

Do not force this interpretation if the text does not support it.

---

# Step 22 — Test Philosophical Vulnerabilities

Integrate findings concerning:

* consciousness;
* understanding;
* agency;
* cognition;
* extended cognition;
* authorship;
* knowledge;
* evidence;
* authority;
* legitimacy;
* truth;
* emergence;
* autonomy.

Distinguish:

* functional behavior from understanding;
* causal participation from intention;
* operational autonomy from mental autonomy;
* production from knowledge;
* relevance from authority;
* authority from legitimacy;
* authority from truth.

---

# Step 23 — Test Evidential Proportionality

For significant claims ask:

> **Does the strength of the claim match the strength of the evidence?**

Classify:

* direct support;
* strong inference;
* plausible inference;
* weak inference;
* insufficient support.

Do not demand empirical evidence for purely interpretive claims.

Do demand stronger support when the text makes broad empirical, causal, historical, or universal claims.

---

# Step 24 — Test Semantic Precision

Integrate wording-based findings.

Pay particular attention to:

* agency;
* autonomy;
* understanding;
* consciousness;
* knowledge;
* evidence;
* authority;
* legitimacy;
* relevance;
* production;
* representation;
* intervention;
* transformation;
* emergence;
* sovereignty;
* conditions.

Check whether wording silently strengthens the conceptual architecture.

---

# Step 25 — Test Redundancy

Do not count repeated concepts.

Determine whether repetition performs different functions.

Ask:

* Does it define?
* develop?
* qualify?
* connect?
* transform?
* return?
* conclude?

A repeated concept may be necessary at multiple points.

Use:

> **function, not frequency**

as the primary test.

---

# Step 26 — Test Asymmetry

Analyze whether unequal development is:

* justified;
* informative;
* structurally meaningful;
* evidentially grounded;
* potentially problematic.

Do not equalize A–E automatically.

A map does not become more precise by making every region the same size.

---

# Step 27 — Test Contradictions

Integrate contradiction findings.

For each apparent contradiction determine whether it is:

* genuine;
* semantic;
* methodological;
* structural;
* temporal;
* scale-dependent;
* productive tension.

Do not resolve a productive tension merely to make the architecture appear cleaner.

---

# Step 28 — Test Reader Autonomy

Ask whether a reader without prior project context can reconstruct:

* the central question;
* rhizome as perspective;
* map/territory distinction;
* domains;
* assemblages;
* nodes;
* inter-stem nodes;
* A–E;
* connections;
* trajectory;
* ending.

If the work depends critically on explanations external to itself, identify that as an autonomy problem.

---

# Step 29 — Test Voice and Rhythm

Determine whether revisions recommended by other Skills would risk:

* academicization;
* excessive smoothing;
* artificial symmetry;
* loss of first-person situatedness;
* loss of essayistic character;
* generic AI-like prose.

Do not sacrifice voice for formal consistency.

---

# Step 30 — Test Originality

Synthesize whether the work's distinctive contribution lies in:

* its question;
* rhizome as perspective;
* map as provisional construction;
* node as concentration;
* inter-stem as transformation;
* observer implication;
* reflexive AI participation;
* distinctive relations among established concepts.

Do not claim absolute originality without external comparison.

---

# Step 31 — Test the Ending

Determine whether the ending:

* closes the work;
* integrates its trajectory;
* preserves productive openness;
* avoids totalization;
* avoids A–E hierarchy;
* preserves map/territory distinction;
* leaves the rhizome open.

The final node can close the work without becoming the final truth of the architecture.

---

# Step 32 — Determine What Must Be Preserved

Create an explicit preservation list.

At minimum evaluate whether to preserve:

* AI ≠ rhizome;
* map ≠ territory;
* dynamic rhizome / relatively stable map;
* classification as map operation;
* node as relational concentration;
* inter-stem as transformation across assemblages;
* propagation as transformed condition moving through relations;
* A–E differentiation;
* contingency;
* materiality;
* non-anthropomorphic treatment of AI;
* distinction between production, knowledge, evidence, attribution;
* distinction between relevance, authority, legitimacy, truth;
* observer implication;
* AI-writing reflexivity where genuinely supported;
* asymmetry where meaningful;
* open ending.

Preservation is an analytical result, not an optional courtesy.

---

# Step 33 — Determine What Requires Intervention

Classify recommendations into:

## Necessary

The issue materially compromises conceptual, methodological, evidential, or architectural integrity.

## Advisable

The work remains viable, but correction would significantly improve precision or functioning.

## Optional

Improvement is possible, but the current form is defensible.

## Not Recommended

The proposed intervention would likely damage voice, asymmetry, openness, or conceptual function.

## Leave Unchanged

No intervention is justified.

---

# Step 34 — Test Whether New Nodes Are Necessary

A gap does not justify a new node.

Before proposing one, ask:

1. Is there a distinct concentration of relations?
2. Is there a distinct tension?
3. Is there a distinct transformation?
4. Is the function not already performed elsewhere?
5. Does it survive transversal review?
6. Would omission create meaningful conceptual loss?

If not, do not add it.

---

# Step 35 — Test Absorption and Elimination

For each potentially weak component ask:

* Can it be absorbed?
* Can it be merged?
* Can it be relocated?
* Can it be compressed?
* Can it be eliminated?

Do not choose the most compact architecture automatically.

Choose the architecture with the greatest cartographic precision.

---

# Step 36 — Test Architectural Economy

Evaluate whether the architecture contains:

* unnecessary duplication;
* artificial distinctions;
* overloaded nodes;
* underdeveloped nodes;
* redundant inter-stem structures;
* excessive methodological scaffolding.

Functional economy is the goal.

Not maximal compression.

---

# Step 37 — Identify the Invisible Thread

Determine what holds the work together.

Possible threads:

* a question;
* a perspective;
* a mode of observation;
* a trajectory;
* a methodological operation;
* a changing relation between observer and observed.

A work does not require a single totalizing thesis.

---

# Step 38 — Determine Readiness

Evaluate whether the architecture is:

### Ready for Final Editing

Major conceptual and structural decisions are sufficiently consolidated.

### Nearly Ready

A limited set of important issues should be resolved first.

### Not Yet Ready

Fundamental architectural or methodological problems remain.

Do not recommend final editing merely because the text is long or polished.

---

# Step 39 — Produce the Integrated Diagnosis

The final diagnosis must not reproduce all individual findings.

It should answer:

> **What is the state of the work after all relevant audits are considered together?**

---

# Mandatory Final Report Structure

## 1. General Diagnosis

Provide a concise integrated assessment.

State:

* what is fundamentally solid;
* what is vulnerable;
* what remains unresolved;
* whether the architecture is consolidated.

---

## 2. Current Architecture

Describe the architecture actually supported by the text.

Use:

> Rhizome → Domains → Assemblages → Nodes → Inter-Stem Nodes → Transformations / Propagations

Clarify that this is cartographic organization, not ontological hierarchy.

---

## 3. Structural Strengths

Identify the strongest features.

Potential dimensions:

* conceptual distinctions;
* node differentiation;
* inter-stem methodology;
* map/territory relation;
* materiality;
* contingency;
* observer implication;
* reflexivity;
* differentiated A–E structure.

---

## 4. Real Problems

List only findings that survive integrated scrutiny.

For each:

* problem;
* evidence;
* underlying cause;
* severity;
* confidence;
* consequence.

---

## 5. Redundancies

Identify:

* genuine redundancy;
* partial overlap;
* productive recurrence.

Do not list repetition merely because the same concept appears more than once.

---

## 6. Nodes

For A–E provide:

* relational concentration;
* principal tension;
* transformation;
* distinct function;
* vulnerability;
* status.

---

## 7. Connections

Describe the important supported connections.

Do not manufacture a complete network.

---

## 8. Propagations

For each significant propagation state:

* transformation;
* pathway;
* changed condition;
* receiving site;
* resulting transformation;
* confidence.

---

## 9. Contradictions

Distinguish:

* genuine contradictions;
* productive tensions;
* semantic tensions;
* unresolved ambiguities.

---

## 10. Philosophical Risks

Evaluate risks concerning:

* anthropomorphism;
* determinism;
* consciousness;
* understanding;
* agency;
* knowledge;
* authority;
* truth;
* emergence;
* autonomy.

---

## 11. Semantic and Stylistic Risks

Include only issues that materially affect:

* conceptual precision;
* reader reconstruction;
* authorial voice;
* rhythm;
* argumentative force.

Do not turn stylistic preference into criticism.

---

## 12. Decisions to Preserve

Explicitly list the architectural and conceptual decisions that should not be disturbed without new evidence.

---

## 13. Recommended Interventions

Separate:

### Necessary

### Advisable

### Optional

### Not Recommended

### Leave Unchanged

Every recommendation should identify the problem it responds to.

---

## 14. Final Verdict

Answer:

> **Is the architecture sufficiently consolidated for final editing?**

Use one of:

* Yes
* Yes, after limited local corrections
* Not yet
* No, major restructuring required

Explain why.

# Cross-Skill Conflict Resolution

When Skills disagree:

1. return to source evidence;
2. identify whether they analyze different dimensions;
3. distinguish local from architectural disagreement;
4. compare confidence;
5. test whether the apparent conflict is productive;
6. preserve uncertainty when evidence does not resolve it.

Do not force artificial consensus among Skills.

---

# Double-Counting Prevention

When multiple Skills identify the same underlying problem:

* identify the root issue;
* retain the strongest evidence;
* record secondary confirmations;
* avoid counting each confirmation as a separate problem.

Convergence increases confidence.

It does not multiply severity.

---

# Negative Findings

The integrated audit must record meaningful absences of problems.

Examples:

* no relevant node confusion detected;
* no significant deterministic chain detected;
* no substantive anthropomorphism detected;
* no architectural redundancy detected;
* no necessary new node identified.

Negative findings protect the work from unnecessary revision.

---

# Uncertainty

When evidence is insufficient:

> **Do not resolve by invention.**

Use:

* Established;
* Strongly supported;
* Plausible;
* Ambiguous;
* Unsupported;
* Contradicted.

A strong integrated audit can end with unresolved questions.

---

# Final Intervention Test

Before recommending any change, ask:

1. What does the current form accomplish?
2. What exactly is wrong?
3. What evidence demonstrates the problem?
4. Is the problem local or architectural?
5. What would be lost by changing it?
6. Does the proposed intervention preserve the map/territory distinction?
7. Does it preserve A–E differentiation?
8. Does it preserve asymmetry where justified?
9. Does it preserve authorial voice?
10. Does it increase cartographic precision?

If the answer to the final question is no, do not recommend the intervention.

---

# Governing Principle

The integrated audit must follow:

> **Analyze before intervening.**

And:

> **Do not improve what does not need improvement.**

The purpose of integration is not to produce the cleanest architecture.

It is to determine whether the architecture accurately performs the conceptual operation of the work.

---

# Final Principle

> **The integrated audit is successful when it distinguishes what must change from what must be protected.**

The objective is not:

> maximum order;

nor:

> maximum complexity.

It is:

> **maximum cartographic precision compatible with the work's conceptual, philosophical, and authorial integrity.**

Therefore:

> **A more orderly text is not necessarily a better map.**

> **A larger architecture is not necessarily a more complex architecture.**

> **A more polished formulation is not necessarily a more precise formulation.**

The final question is always:

> **Does the proposed modification make the map more precise, or does it merely make the map look more orderly?**

And the governing distinction remains:

> **The map stabilizes the gaze. The rhizome remains in motion. Cartography attempts to keep that difference visible.**
