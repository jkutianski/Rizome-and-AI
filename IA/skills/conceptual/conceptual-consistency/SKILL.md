# Conceptual Consistency

## Purpose

Analyze whether the concepts used in a source document maintain a coherent meaning and function across the relevant passages and across the work as a whole.

This Skill evaluates conceptual consistency, not whether the analyst agrees with the author's theoretical position.

## Scope

Evaluate:

* definitions and implicit definitions;
* conceptual distinctions;
* changes in the meaning or scope of terms;
* relations between concepts;
* recurring concepts across sections;
* tensions between apparently incompatible formulations;
* conceptual dependencies;
* unexplained shifts in conceptual function.

Do not evaluate style, grammar, or preferred terminology unless they affect conceptual meaning.

## Analytical Procedure

### 1. Identify the Relevant Concepts

Identify the concepts that are:

* explicitly defined;
* repeatedly used;
* structurally important;
* necessary for a major argument;
* used to distinguish one category from another.

Do not treat every noun or technical term as a concept requiring analysis.

### 2. Establish Conceptual Function

For each relevant concept, determine how it functions in the source.

Ask:

* What does the concept refer to?
* What role does it perform?
* What distinctions does it establish?
* What other concepts does it depend on?
* What consequences does its use have for the argument?
* Does its meaning remain sufficiently stable?

Analyze the concept according to its function in the source rather than according to an external definition.

### 3. Track Conceptual Continuity

Compare significant uses of the same concept across the document.

Determine whether the concept:

* retains substantially the same meaning;
* changes meaning deliberately;
* changes scope without explanation;
* shifts between different analytical levels;
* becomes broader or narrower;
* acquires a different function;
* is used inconsistently.

A change in meaning is not automatically a problem. Determine whether the change is deliberate, justified, or structurally consequential.

### 4. Test Conceptual Distinctions

When the source distinguishes concepts, examine whether the distinction remains operative.

For example, determine whether the text actually maintains distinctions between concepts such as:

* possibility and inevitability;
* participation and intention;
* influence and determination;
* complexity and emergence;
* relation and causality;
* production and knowledge;
* relevance and authority;
* authority and legitimacy;
* representation and intervention.

These examples are methodological prompts, not mandatory categories.

Do not impose distinctions that the source does not require.

### 5. Detect Conceptual Drift

Identify conceptual drift when a term progressively changes its meaning or analytical role without sufficient indication.

Possible forms include:

* semantic expansion;
* semantic contraction;
* category shift;
* level shift;
* metaphor becoming literal;
* descriptive term becoming normative;
* functional claim becoming intentional claim;
* local concept becoming global explanation.

A change is significant only when it affects the reasoning, structure, or interpretation of the work.

### 6. Test Apparent Contradictions

When two passages appear conceptually incompatible:

1. identify the apparent contradiction;
2. determine whether the concepts are actually being used in the same sense;
3. check whether they operate at different analytical levels;
4. check whether one passage qualifies the other;
5. determine whether the tension is deliberate and productive;
6. determine whether a genuine inconsistency remains.

Do not classify productive tension as contradiction.

### 7. Check Cross-Section Function

When a concept is introduced in one section and reused elsewhere, determine whether later uses remain compatible with its earlier function.

Consider:

* definitions;
* examples;
* qualifications;
* structural categories;
* recurring distinctions;
* conclusions.

Do not judge a concept solely from an isolated passage when its function depends on the larger work.

### 8. Check Conceptual Hierarchy

Determine whether the source unintentionally treats concepts as though they belonged to the same analytical level when they do not.

Potential level distinctions may include:

* element vs. relation;
* relation vs. concentration;
* local phenomenon vs. structural condition;
* description vs. explanation;
* mechanism vs. consequence;
* observation vs. interpretation;
* empirical claim vs. philosophical claim.

Only flag a level confusion when it produces a genuine analytical problem.

### 9. Check Scope

Ask whether the scope assigned to a concept remains proportionate to what the source establishes.

Flag cases where a concept moves unjustifiably from:

* local → general;
* particular → universal;
* possible → necessary;
* contingent → inevitable;
* functional → intentional;
* descriptive → normative.

Do not treat broad claims as problematic merely because they are broad. The issue is whether their scope is justified.

## What Not to Do

Do not:

* impose an external philosophical definition without justification;
* replace the author's conceptual vocabulary with preferred terminology;
* assume every repeated word must have exactly the same meaning;
* treat contextual variation as inconsistency;
* confuse disagreement with incoherence;
* confuse ambiguity with contradiction;
* confuse metaphor with conceptual error automatically;
* infer inconsistency solely from different wording;
* manufacture conceptual problems because a concept could be formulated more precisely.

## Evidence Standard

A conceptual-consistency finding should identify:

1. the concept involved;
2. the relevant uses or passages;
3. the relationship between those uses;
4. the specific conceptual shift, conflict, or instability;
5. why it matters.

When the evidence is insufficient, classify the issue as ambiguous or unresolved rather than asserting inconsistency.

## Output

For each substantive finding, use the shared Output Protocol.

When useful, report:

* **Concept**
* **Relevant passages**
* **Observed function**
* **Consistency status**
* **Problem**
* **Why it matters**
* **Confidence**
* **Recommended intervention**

Possible conclusions include:

* Consistent
* Consistent with contextual variation
* Productively tensioned
* Potential conceptual drift
* Significant conceptual inconsistency
* Insufficient evidence

## Core Principle

Conceptual consistency does not require rigid repetition.

A concept may evolve, acquire new relations, or operate differently in different contexts.

The question is whether the evolution remains intelligible and justified within the work.

**Do not confuse conceptual variation with conceptual incoherence.**
