# Philosophical Claims

## Purpose

Analyze the philosophical claims made by a text and determine whether they are conceptually coherent, appropriately scoped, sufficiently supported, and consistent with the work's broader architecture.

This Skill is designed for philosophical, theoretical, essayistic, and interdisciplinary texts involving questions such as:

* consciousness;
* understanding;
* agency;
* cognition;
* extended cognition;
* knowledge;
* truth;
* authority;
* power;
* emergence;
* technology;
* artificial intelligence;
* representation;
* intervention;
* autonomy;
* materiality;
* subjectivity.

Its purpose is **not** to decide whether a philosophical position is correct simply because it conflicts with a particular philosophical tradition.

Its purpose is to determine:

> **What philosophical claim is the text actually making, what assumptions does it require, what follows from it, what would challenge it, and whether the formulation exceeds what the argument can support.**

---

# 1. Governing Principle

A philosophical claim must be evaluated according to the relationship between:

**claim → conceptual meaning → argumentative support → scope → implications**

Do not evaluate philosophical claims merely by asking whether they are familiar, controversial, unconventional, or compatible with one philosophical school.

An unconventional claim may be philosophically strong.

A conventional claim may be poorly supported.

---

# 2. Scope

Analyze claims concerning:

* ontology;
* epistemology;
* philosophy of mind;
* philosophy of technology;
* philosophy of science;
* agency;
* cognition;
* knowledge;
* truth;
* power;
* authority;
* emergence;
* subjectivity;
* objectivity;
* representation;
* intervention;
* materiality;
* technological mediation;
* AI.

Also analyze philosophical claims embedded inside apparently empirical or descriptive passages.

A sentence does not cease to be philosophical merely because it uses technical or empirical vocabulary.

---

# 3. Claim Reconstruction

For every significant philosophical claim, reconstruct:

1. **Subject** — what or whom the claim concerns.
2. **Predicate** — what is being attributed.
3. **Relation** — what relationship is asserted.
4. **Modality** — whether the claim is possible, probable, actual, necessary, sufficient, conditional, speculative, or open.
5. **Scope** — local, contextual, general, structural, historical, universal.
6. **Conditions** — under what circumstances the claim holds.
7. **Evidence** — textual, empirical, conceptual, philosophical, inferential.
8. **Mechanism** — if a mechanism is implied.
9. **Implication** — what follows if the claim is accepted.
10. **Alternatives** — plausible competing interpretations or explanations.

Do not critique a claim before reconstructing it.

---

# 4. Distinguish Claim Types

Classify the philosophical function of the passage.

Possible types include:

* descriptive philosophical claim;
* conceptual distinction;
* ontological claim;
* epistemological claim;
* phenomenological claim;
* methodological claim;
* normative claim;
* interpretive claim;
* causal claim;
* modal claim;
* reflexive claim;
* metaphysical claim;
* analogy or heuristic;
* speculative hypothesis.

Different claim types require different standards of support.

Do not demand empirical proof for every philosophical proposition.

Do not treat an empirical assertion as merely philosophical when it requires factual verification.

---

# 5. Conceptual Reconstruction Before Criticism

Before identifying a problem, ask:

> What is the strongest coherent interpretation of this passage?

Then ask:

> Does the surrounding text support that interpretation?

Only afterward test weaker or problematic readings.

This prevents criticism based on isolated wording when the larger argument clearly qualifies the claim.

---

# 6. Philosophical Scope

Audit whether the claim's scope matches the argument.

Look for movement from:

* local → general;
* contextual → universal;
* possible → actual;
* capacity → realization;
* behavior → mentality;
* participation → agency;
* mediation → determination;
* representation → understanding;
* production → knowledge;
* relevance → authority;
* authority → truth;
* technical possibility → historical inevitability.

These transitions are not automatically invalid.

They require examination of the intermediate argument.

---

# 7. Modality

Pay particular attention to modal strength.

Distinguish:

* may;
* can;
* could;
* tends to;
* often;
* appears to;
* participates in;
* enables;
* contributes to;
* produces;
* transforms;
* determines;
* necessarily;
* inevitably.

A philosophical problem may arise when the wording moves from a weaker claim to a stronger one without argumentative justification.

Do not weaken a claim merely because a stronger formulation is philosophically ambitious.

The question is whether the stronger formulation is warranted.

---

# 8. Agency

Audit claims involving agency carefully.

Distinguish:

* causal efficacy;
* operational autonomy;
* functional agency;
* actor status;
* participation;
* intentional agency;
* reflective agency;
* moral agency.

Do not assume that describing AI as an actor automatically attributes human-like agency.

Conversely, do not allow the word "actor" to conceal an unsupported attribution of:

* intention;
* desire;
* belief;
* consciousness;
* understanding;
* will.

Coordinate with:

* `anthropomorphism-audit`;
* `causality-propagation`;
* `conceptual-consistency`.

---

# 9. Understanding

When the text discusses whether AI understands something, distinguish:

* functional performance;
* linguistic competence;
* behavioral success;
* representation;
* information processing;
* semantic responsiveness;
* inferential capacity;
* contextual adaptation;
* understanding as a philosophical mental property.

Ask:

1. What meaning of "understanding" is being used?
2. Is it functional, semantic, phenomenological, intentional, or epistemic?
3. What evidence supports the attribution?
4. Is the argument denying, affirming, suspending, or questioning understanding?
5. Does functional performance get treated as sufficient for understanding?

Do not assume that functional competence proves understanding.

Do not assume that lack of consciousness proves lack of every possible form of understanding.

The argument must be examined on its own terms.

---

# 10. Consciousness

When consciousness appears, distinguish:

* phenomenal consciousness;
* self-awareness;
* access consciousness;
* functional cognition;
* reflective awareness;
* intentionality;
* subjective experience.

Do not collapse these into one concept.

If the text claims that AI is or is not conscious, determine:

* what conception of consciousness is operative;
* whether the claim is empirical, conceptual, philosophical, or speculative;
* what evidence would bear on it;
* whether the conclusion exceeds the premises.

A statement such as "AI is not conscious" requires different analysis from:

> "The text does not establish that AI is conscious."

The latter is an epistemic claim.

The former is an ontological claim.

Do not treat them as equivalent.

---

# 11. Extended Cognition

When the text invokes extended cognition, distinguish:

* cognitive support;
* cognitive scaffolding;
* external representation;
* distributed cognition;
* extended cognition;
* tool use;
* epistemic mediation;
* causal contribution.

Do not infer extended cognition simply because an external technology contributes to thinking.

Ask:

1. What cognitive function is allegedly extended?
2. What role does the external system perform?
3. Is the coupling stable and functionally integrated?
4. Is the system part of the cognitive process or merely an external aid?
5. What philosophical criterion is being used?
6. Does the text acknowledge alternative interpretations?

When AI participates in writing, distinguish:

**AI assistance → cognitive contribution → epistemic mediation → possible cognitive extension**

These are not automatically equivalent.

---

# 12. Knowledge

When philosophical claims concern knowledge, distinguish:

* information;
* representation;
* belief;
* justified belief;
* understanding;
* knowledge;
* production;
* evaluation;
* evidence;
* attribution.

Do not allow the text to move silently from:

**information → knowledge**

or:

**production → knowledge**

without an argument.

Coordinate with:

* `knowledge-evidence-production`;
* `power-mediation-authority`;
* `semantic-precision`.

---

# 13. Truth

Truth may appear in the work without being its central philosophical problem.

Distinguish:

* truth;
* truth claim;
* evidence;
* validity;
* verification;
* authority;
* legitimacy;
* relevance;
* visibility;
* epistemic status.

In the context of AI and power, avoid collapsing:

**visibility → relevance → authority → truth**

These may be connected without being identical.

If the text discusses "truth production," determine whether it means:

* production of claims presented as true;
* institutional production of authoritative knowledge;
* epistemic validation;
* social construction of legitimacy;
* actual production of truth.

Do not assume these meanings are interchangeable.

---

# 14. Emergence

Audit philosophical claims of emergence separately from claims of novelty or complexity.

Ask:

1. What emerges?
2. From which relations?
3. At what scale?
4. Is the phenomenon reducible to a single component?
5. What configuration produces it?
6. Is emergence being used descriptively, ontologically, epistemically, or metaphorically?

Coordinate with:

* `emergence-analysis`.

Do not classify every unexpected AI behavior as philosophical emergence.

---

# 15. Technology and Causality

When technology is said to:

* transform;
* reorganize;
* produce;
* alter;
* constrain;
* enable;
* accelerate;
* replace;

determine whether the text is making:

* a causal claim;
* a structural claim;
* a mediating claim;
* an enabling claim;
* a deterministic claim;
* a metaphorical claim.

Technology may have real causal effects without becoming the sole cause of social or historical outcomes.

Coordinate with:

* `causality-propagation`;
* `technological-determinism`.

---

# 16. Materiality

Philosophical claims about AI should be tested against material conditions when relevant.

Consider:

* computation;
* energy;
* water;
* semiconductors;
* data centers;
* networks;
* devices;
* storage;
* logistics;
* mineral resources;
* infrastructure;
* labor.

Do not treat materiality as merely a background fact.

Ask whether material conditions:

* enable;
* constrain;
* shape;
* slow;
* accelerate;
* redistribute;
* create dependencies;
* generate tensions.

Materiality does not automatically explain every phenomenon.

---

# 17. Power

When philosophical claims concern power, distinguish:

* causal capacity;
* institutional power;
* infrastructural power;
* economic power;
* epistemic power;
* political power;
* authority;
* legitimacy;
* control.

Do not reduce power to intentional domination.

Power may operate through:

* infrastructures;
* standards;
* classifications;
* visibility;
* dependency;
* resource concentration;
* technical architectures;
* institutional arrangements.

Coordinate with:

* `power-mediation-authority`;
* `map-territory`;
* `technological-determinism`.

---

# 18. Observer and Reflexivity

Determine whether the philosophical argument accounts for the observer's position.

Ask:

* Who constructs the map?
* What is selected?
* What is excluded?
* What categories are constructed?
* Which relations are observed rather than created?
* Does the observer become part of the analytical field?
* Does this reflexivity have an actual consequence?

Do not infer that situated observation means that reality is merely subjective.

Coordinate with:

* `observer-implication`;
* `map-territory`.

---

# 19. Productive Uncertainty

A philosophical work may legitimately leave questions open.

Examples:

* whether AI understands;
* whether AI has consciousness;
* whether AI should count as an agent;
* whether cognition is genuinely extended;
* whether a phenomenon is emergent;
* how authority should be attributed.

Do not treat unresolved philosophical questions as defects when the work explicitly treats their unresolvedness as part of the argument.

Distinguish:

* unresolved because insufficiently argued;
* unresolved because genuinely open;
* unresolved because deliberately suspended.

---

# 20. Philosophical Objections

For significant claims, generate the strongest reasonable objection.

Then determine whether:

* the text already answers it;
* the text partially anticipates it;
* the objection exposes a real vulnerability;
* the objection depends on a different philosophical framework;
* the disagreement is legitimate philosophical pluralism.

Do not treat disagreement between philosophical traditions as internal inconsistency.

---

# 21. Argumentative Dependency

Determine whether the claim depends on an unstated premise.

For example:

> AI produces novel outputs → therefore AI understands.

Possible missing premise:

> producing semantically appropriate outputs is sufficient for understanding.

The Skill should expose such inferential bridges rather than automatically rejecting the conclusion.

---

# 22. Counterfactual Testing

Where useful, ask:

* What happens if this claim is false?
* Which later claims depend on it?
* Would the architecture survive?
* Is the claim central or peripheral?
* Could the argument retain its force under a weaker formulation?
* Does the work actually require the strongest version?

Counterfactual analysis is diagnostic, not an automatic invitation to weaken claims.

---

# 23. Cross-Section Consistency

A philosophical claim should be checked against the rest of the work.

Look for cases where:

* agency is defined differently elsewhere;
* understanding changes meaning;
* knowledge shifts between production and evaluation;
* emergence is used inconsistently;
* causality becomes determinism in another section;
* the map becomes the territory implicitly;
* AI is treated as non-intentional in one passage and intentional in another;
* observer reflexivity is acknowledged but later ignored.

Coordinate with `conceptual-consistency`.

---

# 24. Distinguish Philosophical Problem from Semantic Problem

A formulation may be philosophically defensible but semantically imprecise.

For example:

* the argument may distinguish authority from truth;
* but one sentence may linguistically collapse them.

Classify these separately.

Use:

* `philosophical-claims` for the philosophical structure;
* `semantic-precision` for the wording.

Do not use philosophical criticism to solve a linguistic problem.

---

# 25. Distinguish Philosophical Problem from Empirical Problem

A claim may be:

* philosophically coherent;
* empirically unsupported.

Or:

* empirically plausible;
* philosophically underdeveloped.

Do not collapse these.

If external evidence is required, identify the empirical dependency rather than treating it as a purely philosophical defect.

---

# 26. Evidence Proportionality

Evaluate whether the support matches the claim.

Possible relationships:

* strong claim / strong support;
* strong claim / weak support;
* moderate claim / adequate support;
* speculative claim / explicitly speculative framing;
* interpretive claim / interpretive evidence.

The goal is not to eliminate ambitious philosophy.

The goal is to prevent the text from presenting speculation as established fact.

---

# 27. Common False Positives

Do not classify the following automatically as philosophical problems:

* using AI as an actor;
* attributing causal effects to AI;
* discussing AI agency;
* using "understanding" as a question;
* discussing consciousness;
* invoking extended cognition;
* using emergence;
* describing technology as transformative;
* discussing power;
* discussing truth;
* using the rhizome metaphorically;
* leaving questions unresolved.

Each requires contextual analysis.

---

# 28. Common False Negatives

Do not overlook:

* hidden ontological commitments;
* implicit theories of mind;
* hidden determinism;
* unexplained transitions from behavior to mentality;
* production-to-knowledge jumps;
* relevance-to-authority jumps;
* authority-to-truth jumps;
* causal-to-intentional shifts;
* technical-to-historical transitions;
* speculative claims presented as established;
* unstated assumptions.

# 30. Output Protocol

For each significant philosophical claim, produce:

### Claim

State the claim or identify the relevant passage.

### Claim Type

Identify the philosophical function.

### Conceptual Reconstruction

Explain what the claim means in context.

### Scope

Identify its actual scope.

### Modality

Identify how strongly it is stated.

### Argumentative Support

Identify premises, evidence, and inferential bridges.

### Philosophical Tension or Vulnerability

Identify the strongest issue, if any.

### Alternative Interpretation

Provide a plausible alternative reading where relevant.

### Cross-Section Relation

Identify relevant interaction with other parts of the work.

### Classification

Use:

* A — Critical
* B — Important
* C — Local
* D — Stylistic
* E — Non-problematic

### Confidence

* High
* Medium
* Low

### Implication

Explain what the finding means for the larger argument.

### Recommendation

Choose:

* Necessary
* Advisable
* Optional
* Not Recommended
* Leave Unchanged

---

# 31. Intervention Threshold

Recommend intervention only when:

* the philosophical claim is internally incoherent;
* its scope materially exceeds its support;
* an inferential bridge is essential and missing;
* a key philosophical distinction collapses;
* a claim contradicts the work's own conceptual architecture;
* the formulation creates a serious and avoidable philosophical vulnerability.

Do not intervene merely because:

* another philosophical tradition would disagree;
* the claim is unconventional;
* the terminology is unfamiliar;
* the argument could be made more sophisticated;
* additional authors could be cited;
* the claim could be qualified indefinitely.

---

# 32. Final Principle

The purpose of philosophical analysis is not to domesticate the work into an established philosophical vocabulary.

It is to determine whether the work's philosophical claims are:

* intelligible;
* coherent;
* appropriately scoped;
* proportionate to their support;
* internally differentiated;
* consistent with the work's own conceptual operation;
* and open to genuine philosophical challenge.

> **A philosophical claim does not become stronger by sounding more philosophical. It becomes stronger when its conceptual operation, scope, assumptions, and argumentative support are visible.**

> **Do not eliminate philosophical ambition. Make its conditions visible.**
