# Causality and Propagation

## Purpose

Analyze causal claims and causal structures within a text, with particular attention to the relationship between **causality, relational dynamics, transformation, feedback, and propagation**.

The purpose is not to eliminate causal explanations in favor of a generalized language of relations.

Causality can be valid and analytically important.

The purpose is to determine:

* when a causal claim is justified;
* what kind of causality is being asserted;
* whether the causal structure is linear or distributed;
* whether necessary and sufficient conditions are being confused;
* whether correlation, sequence, influence, participation, or relation are being described as causality;
* whether propagation is incorrectly reduced to causality;
* whether causal language introduces technological determinism;
* whether feedback, contingency, mediation, or reciprocal modification are being obscured.

The central question is:

> **“What causal relation is actually being claimed, what evidence supports it, what conditions mediate it, and does the causal formulation adequately represent the dynamics described by the source?”**

---

# Scope

Analyze causal claims involving:

* technologies;
* AI systems;
* scientific practices;
* institutions;
* social practices;
* infrastructures;
* material conditions;
* policies;
* markets;
* standards;
* knowledge production;
* power;
* authority;
* biological processes;
* cognitive processes;
* historical developments;
* transformations;
* propagation.

Analyze both explicit and implicit causal structures.

Pay particular attention to formulations such as:

* causes;
* produces;
* generates;
* leads to;
* results in;
* determines;
* drives;
* makes possible;
* enables;
* constrains;
* transforms;
* reshapes;
* triggers;
* gives rise to;
* results from.

These verbs do not all express the same causal strength.

---

# Core Principles

## 1. Causality Is Not the Enemy of a Rhizomatic Analysis

A rhizomatic perspective does not require abandoning causality.

Some relations genuinely have causal structure.

The analytical problem arises when:

* one cause is treated as sufficient when it is not;
* a contingent relation is treated as necessary;
* a contribution is treated as determination;
* a pathway is treated as the only possible pathway;
* distributed causation is reduced to one agent;
* feedback is represented as a one-way chain;
* historical contingency disappears.

Preserve valid causal claims.

Critique unjustified causal expansion.

---

## 2. Relation Is Not Automatically Causality

A relation may be:

* contextual;
* structural;
* constitutive;
* enabling;
* constraining;
* mediating;
* reciprocal;
* institutional;
* material.

Do not convert every meaningful relation into causal language.

The existence of a connection does not establish causation.

---

## 3. Temporal Sequence Is Not Automatically Causality

If:

> A occurs before B,

this does not by itself demonstrate:

> A causes B.

Look for:

* mechanism;
* intervention;
* dependency;
* counterfactual relevance;
* repeated evidence;
* structural conditions;
* explicit causal reasoning.

A temporal sequence may be important without being causal.

---

## 4. Influence Is Not Necessarily Determination

Expressions such as:

> AI influences practice

are weaker than:

> AI determines practice.

Distinguish:

* influence;
* contribution;
* participation;
* enabling;
* constraining;
* shaping;
* conditioning;
* transformation;
* determination.

The stronger the causal verb, the stronger the evidential burden.

---

## 5. Enablement Is Not Necessity

A condition may make something possible without being necessary for it.

Likewise:

> A enables B

does not mean:

> without A, B could never occur.

Check whether the source establishes:

* possibility;
* increased probability;
* facilitation;
* necessary condition;
* sufficient condition;
* jointly necessary conditions.

Do not infer necessity from enablement.

---

## 6. Necessary Conditions Are Not Sufficient Causes

If A is required for B, this does not mean A alone produces B.

Similarly, if A can produce B under certain conditions, A may not be sufficient in every context.

Distinguish:

* necessary condition;
* sufficient condition;
* contributing condition;
* enabling condition;
* triggering condition;
* maintaining condition.

---

## 7. Distributed Causality Should Remain Distributed

Many transformations arise from combinations of:

* technologies;
* institutions;
* practices;
* infrastructures;
* policies;
* resources;
* people;
* economic incentives;
* material conditions.

Do not attribute the whole transformation to one element simply because that element is highly visible.

AI may participate in a transformation without being its sole cause.

---

## 8. Causal Participation Does Not Imply Intention

An AI system, technology, institution, or infrastructure can have causal effects without possessing intention.

Avoid formulations that move from:

> X participates causally in Y

to:

> X wants, decides, intends, seeks, or chooses Y.

Causal efficacy and intentional agency are distinct analytical questions.

---

## 9. Causality Does Not Require Conscious Agency

A causal relation can involve:

* material systems;
* infrastructures;
* algorithms;
* institutions;
* physical processes;
* biological processes.

Do not assume that only intentional agents can cause effects.

Conversely, do not infer agency merely because a system has causal effects.

---

# Types of Causal Relations

Use descriptive distinctions when useful.

## Direct Causality

A relatively immediate causal relation between two processes.

## Mediated Causality

A causes or contributes to B through an intermediate process.

## Distributed Causality

An outcome depends on several interacting causes or conditions.

## Reciprocal Causality

A affects B while B subsequently affects A.

## Circular Causality

The effects of a process return to modify the conditions that produced them.

## Probabilistic Causality

A changes the likelihood of an outcome without determining it.

## Structural Causality

An arrangement of conditions constrains or systematically shapes possible outcomes.

## Enabling Causality

A condition makes a process possible or more viable.

## Constraining Causality

A condition limits possible trajectories.

## Triggering Causality

A relatively localized event initiates a process whose broader conditions were already present.

These are analytical descriptions, not mutually exclusive categories.

# Procedure

## 1. Identify the Causal Claim

Locate the exact claim.

Do not analyze causal structure from a general impression of the paragraph.

Record the relevant formulation in the source language when wording matters.

---

## 2. Identify Cause and Effect

Determine:

* what is presented as cause;
* what is presented as effect;
* whether the relation is explicit;
* whether intermediate processes are omitted.

If the endpoints are unclear, the causal claim may itself be semantically unstable.

---

## 3. Identify Causal Strength

Classify the formulation approximately as:

### Weak

* associated with;
* contributes to;
* participates in;
* is related to.

### Moderate

* influences;
* shapes;
* conditions;
* facilitates;
* constrains.

### Strong

* produces;
* generates;
* transforms;
* causes.

### Very Strong

* determines;
* necessarily produces;
* inevitably leads to.

This classification is descriptive.

A strong formulation is not automatically wrong.

It requires stronger justification.

---

## 4. Identify the Mechanism

Ask:

> How does A produce or contribute to B?

Possible mechanisms include:

* material interaction;
* institutional decision;
* infrastructural dependency;
* behavioral change;
* technical affordance;
* economic incentive;
* informational mediation;
* policy;
* feedback;
* biological mechanism.

If no mechanism is available, distinguish a demonstrated causal relation from a plausible but underexplained one.

---

## 5. Identify Conditions

Determine what conditions must be present for the causal relation to operate.

These may include:

* infrastructure;
* resources;
* institutional arrangements;
* social practices;
* technical standards;
* energy;
* computing capacity;
* data;
* regulation;
* markets;
* expertise;
* political decisions.

A causal claim without relevant conditions may be overly generalized.

---

## 6. Test Counterfactual Dependence

Where appropriate, ask:

> Would the outcome have occurred, or occurred in the same way, if the alleged cause had been absent?

This is not always empirically answerable.

The purpose is diagnostic.

If the answer appears to be:

> yes, substantially,

the causal claim may be weaker than the wording suggests.

If:

> no, under the relevant conditions,

the causal dependence may be stronger.

Do not manufacture counterfactuals unsupported by the source.

---

## 7. Distinguish Contribution From Determination

Ask whether the alleged cause:

* contributes to the outcome;
* increases its likelihood;
* enables it;
* constrains alternatives;
* substantially shapes it;
* is necessary for it;
* is sufficient for it;
* determines it.

Do not collapse these levels.

---

## 8. Identify Multiple Causal Conditions

Look for:

* coexisting causes;
* enabling conditions;
* background conditions;
* triggering events;
* institutional mediators;
* material constraints;
* feedback effects.

If multiple conditions are necessary, do not represent one as the complete explanation.

---

## 9. Analyze Feedback

Determine whether the effect feeds back into the cause.

For example:

**technology → changed practice → new data → technological development**

This is not adequately represented by a simple:

**technology → practice**

arrow.

Feedback can transform the causal structure itself.

---

## 10. Analyze Reciprocal Causality

Some relations are dynamically reciprocal.

For example:

**A modifies B → B modifies A**

This should not automatically be described as a contradiction.

It may represent:

* reciprocal causation;
* co-adaptation;
* feedback;
* dynamic coupling.

The temporal ordering may matter.

---

## 11. Analyze Delayed Effects

Causal effects may occur after:

* temporal delay;
* institutional mediation;
* infrastructural development;
* adoption;
* accumulation;
* threshold effects.

Do not infer absence of causality merely because the effect is not immediate.

---

## 12. Analyze Nonlinearity

A small change may produce:

* a large effect;
* a delayed effect;
* a threshold effect;
* no immediate effect;
* an effect only under particular conditions.

Likewise, a large intervention may produce a small effect.

Do not assume proportionality unless supported.

---

## 13. Analyze Causal Chains

When the source presents:

**A → B → C → D**

ask:

* Are all links independently supported?
* Are intermediate mechanisms identified?
* Does each transition involve causality?
* Is one link merely temporal or relational?
* Does the chain conceal contingency?
* Could alternative pathways exist?

A long causal chain is only as strong as its weakest unsupported transition.

---

## 14. Analyze Causal Networks

When several causal pathways interact, represent them conceptually without forcing a single chain.

For example:

**A → B**

**C → B**

**B ↔ D**

**E constrains A**

The goal is not mathematical formalization.

The goal is to preserve distributed structure.

---

## 15. Analyze Propagation Separately

A transformation can propagate through relations without every stage being a direct cause of the next.

Likewise, a causal effect may remain local without propagating across the map.

Ask separately:

### Causality

What produces, contributes to, enables, or constrains what?

### Propagation

How does a transformation extend through the relational field?

Do not collapse the two.

---

# Causality and Technological Determinism

Technological determinism occurs when technological development is treated as the primary or inevitable driver of broader historical or social outcomes without adequately accounting for:

* institutions;
* decisions;
* practices;
* economic structures;
* political conditions;
* material constraints;
* contingency;
* resistance;
* alternative trajectories.

Do not label a passage deterministic merely because technology has real causal effects.

The relevant question is whether technological causality is presented as:

* sufficient;
* exclusive;
* autonomous;
* inevitable;
* historically necessary.

A formulation such as:

> technological capacity contributed to this transformation

may be entirely compatible with a rhizomatic perspective.

A formulation such as:

> technological development inevitably produced this social outcome

requires substantially stronger justification.

---

# Causality and Agency

Separate three questions:

1. **Does X have causal effects?**
2. **Does X function as an actor or participant in a relational configuration?**
3. **Does X possess intentional agency?**

These are not equivalent.

An AI system may causally affect practices without intending those effects.

An infrastructure may constrain behavior without deciding anything.

A person or institution may exercise intentional agency while also being constrained by larger assemblages.

Preserve these distinctions.

---

# Causality and the Rhizomatic Perspective

A rhizomatic perspective does not mean:

> everything causes everything else.

Nor does it mean:

> nothing can be explained causally.

Instead, examine whether causal relations coexist with:

* multiplicity;
* contingency;
* reciprocal modification;
* feedback;
* heterogeneous conditions;
* asymmetry;
* distributed agency;
* material constraints;
* alternative pathways;
* temporal variation.

The objective is not to abolish causality.

It is to prevent a **single linear causal chain from becoming the total model of the system** when the source supports a more distributed dynamic.

---

# Causality, Power, and Institutions

Causal analysis should not isolate technical systems from the institutional conditions through which they operate.

For example, a technical capability may contribute to a social transformation only through:

* organizational adoption;
* investment;
* regulation;
* standards;
* infrastructure;
* labor;
* access;
* institutional authority.

Likewise, institutions can constrain or redirect technological effects.

Do not assign causal priority merely because one factor is technically visible.

---

# Causality and Materiality

When a claim concerns technological expansion, include relevant material conditions where supported:

* energy;
* water;
* minerals;
* semiconductor supply;
* compute;
* data centers;
* networks;
* devices;
* logistics;
* storage.

Material conditions can function as:

* enabling conditions;
* constraints;
* causes;
* mediators;
* bottlenecks.

Do not add them merely because they are theoretically relevant.

Use them when they materially affect the causal claim under examination.

---

# Common Analytical Errors

Do not:

* treat correlation as causation;
* treat temporal sequence as causation;
* treat connection as causation;
* treat influence as determination;
* treat enablement as necessity;
* treat contribution as sufficient causation;
* treat causal participation as intentional agency;
* attribute distributed outcomes to one technology;
* assume technology acts autonomously;
* assume technological possibility produces historical inevitability;
* eliminate valid causal relations merely because the analysis is rhizomatic;
* replace every causal verb with weaker relational language;
* invent mechanisms;
* infer counterfactual dependence without evidence;
* treat feedback as contradiction;
* reduce reciprocal dynamics to one direction;
* collapse propagation into causality;
* collapse causality into propagation;
* treat nonlinear causality as absence of causality;
* mistake uncertainty about mechanism for proof that no causal relation exists.

---

# Evidence Standard

Evaluate causal claims according to the evidence available.

## Directly Supported

The source explicitly establishes the causal relation and provides adequate support.

## Strongly Supported

The causal relation is not fully explicit but follows from several converging elements.

## Plausible

The relation is reasonable but important mechanisms or conditions remain uncertain.

## Weak

The causal claim is possible but exceeds what the available evidence establishes.

## Unsupported

The source does not provide sufficient grounds for the causal claim.

## Contradicted

The causal formulation conflicts with evidence elsewhere in the source or with reliable external evidence when external verification is appropriate.

Do not demand empirical proof for every philosophical or conceptual causal claim.

Match the evidential standard to the type of claim.

---

# Output Protocol

For each significant causal finding, report:

### Causal Claim

State the claim being analyzed.

### Cause

Identify the alleged causal factor or condition.

### Effect

Identify the alleged outcome.

### Causal Strength

Weak, moderate, strong, or very strong.

### Mechanism

Explain how the causal relation is supposed to operate.

### Conditions

Identify relevant enabling, constraining, mediating, or background conditions.

### Alternatives

Identify other plausible causal pathways when relevant.

### Feedback

State whether feedback modifies the causal structure.

### Distribution

State whether causation is concentrated, distributed, reciprocal, or mixed.

### Propagation

State separately whether a transformation propagates beyond the initial causal relation.

### Determinism Risk

State whether the formulation risks technological, social, biological, economic, or other determinism.

### Agency

State whether the causal claim improperly attributes intention or agency.

### Evidence

Directly supported, strongly supported, plausible, weak, unsupported, or contradicted.

### Classification

Use:

* **A — Critical**
* **B — Important**
* **C — Local**
* **D — Stylistic**
* **E — Non-Problematic**

### Confidence

Use:

* High
* Medium
* Low

# Diagnostic Questions

Use these questions when needed:

1. What exactly is claimed to cause what?
2. What is the causal strength of the wording?
3. Is the relation actually causal?
4. What mechanism connects cause and effect?
5. What conditions are required?
6. Is the alleged cause necessary?
7. Is it sufficient?
8. Is it merely contributory?
9. Could the effect occur through another pathway?
10. Is the relation direct or mediated?
11. Are multiple causes involved?
12. Is there feedback?
13. Is the relation reciprocal?
14. Is there temporal delay?
15. Is the causal structure nonlinear?
16. Is a long causal chain being assumed without support?
17. Is correlation being converted into causation?
18. Is connection being converted into causation?
19. Is influence being converted into determination?
20. Is participation being converted into intention?
21. Is technology being treated as autonomous?
22. Is technological possibility being treated as historical inevitability?
23. Are institutional and material conditions visible?
24. Does the source support a distributed causal structure?
25. Does propagation occur independently of the causal relation?
26. What would have to change for the causal relation not to occur?
27. Is the causal claim stronger than the argument requires?
28. Is the causal claim weaker than the argument requires?

# Final Principle

> **A rhizomatic analysis does not require abandoning causality. It requires refusing to mistake one causal pathway for the whole field of relations.**

A rigorous causal analysis therefore asks:

**what causes what → through which mechanism → under what conditions → with what degree of necessity → alongside which other causes → with what feedback or alternatives → and whether the resulting transformation propagates beyond the initial relation.**

The objective is not to weaken causal explanations.

It is to make them **proportionate to the evidence and adequate to the complexity of the relational field.**
