# Evidence Calibration for Causal Language

## Purpose

Ensure that the strength of causal language corresponds to the strength and type of evidence available.

The objective is not to weaken causal claims by default. It is to preserve valid causal formulations while preventing the text from presenting association, influence, contribution, or cartographic interpretation as empirically demonstrated causality.

## Core Principle

> **When a causal relation is directly supported by evidence, it may be formulated causally. When the evidence shows association, influence, or contribution, the language must reflect that degree of strength. When the claim is a cartographic interpretation, it should be presented as a relation, condition, transformation, or propagation within the assemblage, not as a demonstrated empirical causal claim.**

## Three Levels of Claim

### 1. Directly Supported Causal Relation

Use causal language when the available evidence adequately establishes the relation and, where relevant, its mechanism.

Examples:

* causes;
* produces;
* generates;
* enables;
* transforms;
* leads to.

A strong causal verb is acceptable when the evidence supports it.

### 2. Association, Influence, or Contribution

When evidence establishes correlation, association, influence, contribution, or probabilistic effect rather than strong determination, calibrate the wording accordingly.

Prefer formulations such as:

* is associated with;
* contributes to;
* influences;
* participates in;
* can affect;
* may modify;
* conditions;
* facilitates;
* constrains;
* helps produce.

Do not upgrade a weaker evidential relation into determination merely because the broader argument suggests a causal pathway.

### 3. Cartographic Interpretation

Some statements in a rhizomatic analysis are not empirical causal claims at all. They interpret how relations, tensions, transformations, or propagations are organized within the map.

Present these as:

* relations;
* conditions;
* transformations;
* reciprocal modifications;
* feedback;
* propagation;
* concentrations of relations and tensions.

Do not present the interpretive mapping itself as if it were an independently demonstrated causal law.

## Calibration Rule

Before accepting a causal formulation, ask:

1. What exactly is being claimed to cause what?
2. What evidence supports the relation?
3. Does the evidence establish causation, or only association, influence, or contribution?
4. Is the mechanism identified or adequately supported?
5. Are there relevant mediating or enabling conditions?
6. Is the statement empirical, inferential, or cartographic?
7. Does the wording express the appropriate causal strength?

## Important Distinction

Do not weaken every causal verb simply because the text is rhizomatic.

The correct procedure is **calibration, not dilution**.

A demonstrated causal relation should remain causal.

A contribution should remain a contribution.

A condition should remain a condition.

A cartographic interpretation should remain an interpretation of relational dynamics.

## Relation to Rhizomatic Analysis

A rhizomatic perspective can contain genuine causal relations without reducing the system to a single linear causal chain.

Therefore distinguish:

**Causality:** what produces, contributes to, enables, or constrains what.

**Cartographic interpretation:** how relations and transformations are organized and represented within the map.

**Propagation:** how a transformation extends through relations and across assemblages.

Do not collapse these three levels.

## Audit Rule

For every significant causal chain:

**claim → evidence → causal strength → mechanism/conditions → formulation**

If the evidence is weaker than the wording, recalibrate the wording.

If the evidence supports the wording, preserve it.

If the statement is interpretive rather than empirical, identify it as such through the language of relations, transformation, condition, feedback, or propagation.
