# Output Protocol

## Purpose

This protocol defines how analytical Skills present their findings.

The output must make a clear distinction between observation, interpretation, diagnosis, confidence, and recommendation.

## General Principle

The purpose of the output is to make the analysis useful and traceable, not to maximize the number of findings.

Prioritize:

* precision over quantity;
* relevance over completeness;
* evidence over speculation;
* diagnosis over rewriting;
* justified intervention over automatic correction.

## Finding Structure

When a Skill identifies a substantive issue, structure the finding around the following elements when applicable:

1. **Finding** — what was identified.
2. **Evidence** — where and how the source supports the finding.
3. **Analysis** — why the finding matters.
4. **Classification** — what type of issue it represents.
5. **Confidence** — how strongly the evidence supports the diagnosis.
6. **Implication** — what the issue affects, if anything.
7. **Recommendation** — whether intervention is warranted.

Not every finding requires every field. Use only what contributes to clarity.

## Problem Classification

Classify substantive findings using the following levels:

### A — Critical

The issue compromises a fundamental conceptual, methodological, structural, or argumentative component of the work.

### B — Important

The issue significantly affects conceptual precision, structural coherence, argumentative strength, or interpretive stability.

### C — Local

The issue is limited in scope and can be addressed without altering the broader architecture.

### D — Stylistic

The issue primarily concerns wording, rhythm, readability, or presentation and has little or no structural consequence.

### E — Non-Problematic

The feature may be unusual, asymmetrical, unconventional, or potentially debatable, but there is no sufficient reason to treat it as a problem.

Use **E** when an apparent problem should explicitly be left alone.

## Issue Type

Where useful, identify the nature of the finding separately from its severity.

Possible types include:

* Conceptual
* Methodological
* Argumentative
* Structural
* Semantic
* Evidential
* Linguistic
* Stylistic
* Scope
* Interpretive

Do not use a severity label as a substitute for explaining what kind of problem has been found.

## Confidence

Calibrate the strength of the diagnosis.

Use:

* **High** — directly established by the source.
* **Medium** — strongly supported but involving interpretation.
* **Low** — plausible but substantially dependent on inference.

Do not use high-confidence language for speculative findings.

## Evidence and Location

Whenever practical, identify where the relevant evidence occurs.

Depending on the available source format, this may include:

* section;
* heading;
* paragraph;
* sentence;
* quotation;
* page;
* line;
* structural position.

Use the most precise location available.

## Recommendations

Separate diagnosis from recommendation.

A recommendation should indicate whether an intervention is:

* **Necessary** — the problem should be addressed.
* **Advisable** — addressing it would materially improve the work, but the architecture can function without it.
* **Optional** — a possible refinement with limited structural importance.
* **Not recommended** — intervention would likely produce more distortion than improvement.
* **Leave unchanged** — the apparent issue is not actually a problem.

Do not automatically recommend rewriting after identifying an issue.

## Before/After Separation

Unless the task explicitly requests rewriting:

* do not replace the source passage;
* do not silently rewrite it;
* do not present an alternative formulation as though it were part of the original;
* keep proposed formulations clearly separate from analytical findings.

Analysis and rewriting are different operations.

## Positive Findings

Report significant strengths, not only problems.

Identify when the source:

* establishes a distinction effectively;
* resolves a potential ambiguity;
* sustains a concept consistently;
* creates a productive tension;
* performs a structurally necessary function;
* preserves an important asymmetry;
* successfully connects otherwise separate territories;
* avoids a foreseeable philosophical or methodological problem.

A rigorous audit must be capable of concluding that something works.

## Negative Findings

Negative findings must be proportionate.

Prefer formulations such as:

* "The text does not currently establish..."
* "The connection remains underdeveloped..."
* "The evidence is insufficient to support..."
* "This distinction is not yet consistently maintained..."

Avoid absolute formulations unless the evidence genuinely warrants them.

## Recommendations Must Be Traceable

Every substantive recommendation should answer:

1. What problem does it address?
2. What evidence establishes the problem?
3. Why does the problem matter?
4. Why is this intervention preferable to leaving the passage unchanged?
5. What conceptual or structural cost could the intervention introduce?

Do not recommend changes merely because they are aesthetically cleaner.

## No False Precision

Do not assign numerical scores merely to create an appearance of rigor.

When an overall evaluation is required, provide a qualitative synthesis supported by the findings.

If a score is explicitly requested, explain what the score represents and do not allow it to replace the underlying analysis.

## Cross-Skill Consistency

When multiple Skills contribute findings:

* preserve distinctions between their analytical functions;
* identify genuine convergence;
* identify conflicting findings when they occur;
* avoid counting the same observation as multiple independent problems;
* prioritize the more specific diagnosis when two Skills identify the same underlying issue.

Repeated detection does not necessarily mean multiple problems.

## Synthesis

When producing a synthesis:

* distinguish local findings from architectural findings;
* distinguish independent problems from manifestations of the same underlying issue;
* identify what should be preserved;
* identify what requires intervention;
* identify what remains uncertain;
* avoid turning every local finding into a global conclusion.

## Core Principle

A useful analytical output does not merely tell the user what could be changed.

It makes visible:

**what the source does, what the evidence supports, what the analyst infers, what genuinely requires attention, and what should be left alone.**
