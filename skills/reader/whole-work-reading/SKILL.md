# Whole-Work Reading

## Purpose

Read and analyze the text as a complete work rather than as a collection of independent sections.

The purpose is to determine whether the work has:

* a recognizable conceptual trajectory;
* an underlying problem or question;
* continuity across its different territories;
* meaningful transformations;
* relationships among its parts;
* internal tensions;
* returns and developments;
* a coherent ending;
* sufficient autonomy as a work.

This Skill does **not** ask whether every section is equally important, equally developed, or explicitly connected to every other section.

It asks whether the parts participate in a larger movement.

The central question is:

> **Does the text function as a work with an identifiable trajectory, or does it remain a collection of adjacent analyses?**

---

# Core Principle

A work can have unity without having a single totalizing thesis.

It can contain:

* multiple perspectives;
* different scales;
* asymmetries;
* unresolved tensions;
* partial arguments;
* heterogeneous sections;
* returns;
* interruptions;
* crossings;
* open questions.

These do not automatically weaken the work.

Therefore:

* unity ≠ uniformity
* coherence ≠ symmetry
* trajectory ≠ linearity
* connection ≠ explicit signposting
* recurrence ≠ redundancy
* multiplicity ≠ fragmentation
* openness ≠ incompleteness
* complexity ≠ incoherence
* heterogeneity ≠ lack of structure
* synthesis ≠ totalization

The work should be understood as a **trajectory through a relational field**, not as a mechanically ordered sequence of arguments.

---

# Scope

Apply this Skill to the complete text.

The primary object is the work as a whole, including:

* opening;
* initial problem;
* methodological framing;
* observer position;
* domains;
* assemblages;
* nodes;
* inter-stem nodes;
* connections;
* propagations;
* returns;
* reflexive passages;
* closing.

Do not treat each section as an autonomous essay unless the work itself clearly does so.

---

# What Makes a Work a Work?

A complete work generally has some combination of:

* a problem;
* a question;
* a trajectory;
* recurring conceptual concerns;
* transformations;
* internal relations;
* tensions;
* development;
* consequences;
* a closing movement.

The work does not need to state these explicitly.

Some may emerge through the architecture itself.

The analysis should therefore distinguish:

> **explicit unity**

from:

> **structural or relational unity.**

---

# Whole-Work Trajectory

For this project, reconstruct the trajectory when supported by the text.

A possible analytical sequence is:

```text
personal encounter / initial question
        ↓
observer position
        ↓
map
        ↓
rhizome
        ↓
domains
        ↓
assemblages
        ↓
nodes
        ↓
inter-stem transformations
        ↓
connections / propagations
        ↓
reflexive implications
        ↓
open closure
```

This is a diagnostic reconstruction.

It is **not** a claim that the work must literally follow this sequence.

Do not impose the project's conceptual architecture on the text if the actual text does not support it.

---

# Procedure

## Step 1 — Read the Work as a Whole

Before evaluating individual sections, identify:

* the initial problem;
* the apparent object of observation;
* the observer's position;
* the principal conceptual operation;
* the trajectory;
* the ending movement.

Do not begin with isolated defects.

---

## Step 2 — Identify the Initial Question

Ask:

> What question initiates the work?

Determine whether the question:

* remains visible;
* evolves;
* becomes more precise;
* is transformed;
* is partially answered;
* remains open;
* disappears without explanation.

A question need not receive a definitive answer.

---

## Step 3 — Identify the Invisible Thread

Search for a conceptual thread connecting the work.

It may involve:

* how AI changes relations;
* how relations produce transformations;
* how technologies participate in knowledge;
* how the observer constructs the map;
* how materiality conditions technological dynamics;
* how power concentrates;
* how categories become unstable;
* how the rhizome is made visible through cartography.

The thread should be reconstructed from the text.

Do not invent one simply because it would improve coherence.

---

# Section-to-Work Relations

## Step 4 — Determine What Each Major Section Contributes

For each major section ask:

> What does this section contribute to the work that cannot be understood solely from the section itself?

Possible contributions:

* establishes a condition;
* introduces a distinction;
* develops a tension;
* opens a new territory;
* transforms an earlier concept;
* connects assemblages;
* demonstrates a consequence;
* complicates the observer's position;
* prepares the ending.

A section can be locally strong while globally unnecessary.

Conversely, a section can be locally modest while structurally indispensable.

---

# Development

## Step 5 — Test Whether the Work Develops

Development does not necessarily mean:

> A → B → C → conclusion.

A rhizomatic work may develop through:

* accumulation;
* branching;
* return;
* displacement;
* reframing;
* crossing;
* recursive movement;
* increasing relational density.

Ask:

* Does something change?
* Does the reader see the problem differently later?
* Do concepts acquire new functions?
* Do relations become more visible?
* Do tensions intensify or transform?
* Does the observer's position change?

If nothing changes across the work, investigate whether it is a collection rather than a trajectory.

---

# Transformation Across the Work

## Step 6 — Identify Major Transformations

Look for transformations such as:

* question → map;
* object → relation;
* relation → tension;
* element → concentration;
* assemblage → crossing;
* representation → intervention;
* technical capacity → strategic capacity;
* information mediation → relevance/authority;
* production → evidence/attribution;
* external analysis → observer implication.

These are examples, not mandatory findings.

The actual transformations must be reconstructed from the source.

---

# Recurrence and Return

## Step 7 — Analyze Recurring Concepts

Track important concepts across the work.

Ask whether recurrence represents:

* persistence;
* development;
* transformation;
* feedback;
* reframing;
* return from another scale;
* return from another assemblage.

A concept that returns can help create unity.

Do not classify recurring concepts as redundant without functional comparison.

Coordinate with `necessity-redundancy`.

---

# Cross-Section Relations

## Step 8 — Identify Relations Between Sections

Determine whether sections are connected through:

* conceptual dependency;
* tension;
* contrast;
* transformation;
* feedback;
* shared conditions;
* shared elements in different assemblages;
* scale transition;
* propagation;
* reflexive return.

Do not require explicit transitional sentences.

A relation may be structural rather than rhetorical.

---

# Necessary Connections

## Step 9 — Identify Missing Connections

Ask:

> Does the work contain a relation that the reader needs in order to understand the trajectory but that remains invisible?

Examples:

* a node appears without preparation;
* an inter-stem transformation appears without the relevant assemblages;
* the ending introduces a conclusion not prepared earlier;
* the observer implication appears without connection to the initial writing situation.

Missing connection does not necessarily mean missing content.

It may require only making an existing relation more visible.

Coordinate with `connection-analysis`.

---

# Fragmentation Test

## Step 10 — Test Whether the Work Has Become a Collection

Signs of fragmentation include:

* each section introduces a new problem with little relation to the larger one;
* nodes function as independent essays;
* conclusions repeatedly restart the argument;
* concepts do not develop across sections;
* connections are merely declared;
* inter-stem nodes are listed rather than demonstrated;
* the ending cannot explain why the sections belong together.

Do not diagnose fragmentation merely because the work contains heterogeneous material.

Heterogeneity can be constitutive of the work.

---

# Totalization Test

## Step 11 — Test the Opposite Problem

A work can also become too unified.

Look for:

* one master concept explaining everything;
* every node subordinated to one thesis;
* every relation forced into one causal chain;
* all differences reduced to one principle;
* every transformation presented as inevitable;
* the ending claiming exhaustive explanation.

This can destroy the multiplicity that the cartographic method is intended to preserve.

The work should have unity without becoming totalizing.

---

# Whole-Work and Rhizome

## Step 12 — Ask Whether the Work Makes the Rhizome Visible

The work need not reproduce a rhizome formally.

Instead, ask whether the reader encounters:

* multiple relations;
* crossings;
* tensions;
* transformations;
* feedback;
* uneven connections;
* contingency;
* material conditions;
* changing configurations;
* limits of representation.

The architecture can be ordered while still making these dynamics visible.

---

# Whole-Work and Map

## Step 13 — Test the Map as a Construction

Ask whether the complete work makes clear that:

* the map is constructed;
* the map is partial;
* categories are provisional;
* stabilization is temporary;
* the observer participates in construction;
* the territory exceeds the map.

The work should not accidentally transform its analytical map into a claim about the complete ontology of the territory.

Coordinate with `map-territory`.

---

# Whole-Work and Observer

## Step 14 — Track the Observer

Follow the position of the observer across the work.

Ask:

* Does the observer begin outside the object?
* Does the work later reveal participation?
* Does the author's use of AI become part of the problem?
* Does the map eventually include its own conditions of construction?
* Does reflexivity alter the analysis?

The observer does not need to become the center of the work.

The relevant question is whether the observer's position is conceptually consequential.

Coordinate with `observer-implication` and `ai-writing-reflexivity`.

---

# Whole-Work and AI

## Step 15 — Test the Role of AI Across the Work

Determine whether AI remains:

* the object of analysis;
* a participant;
* a mediator;
* a technological system;
* an actor in assemblages;
* part of the writing process;
* a reflexive element in the map.

Check whether its role changes appropriately.

Do not assume that AI must occupy the same conceptual role everywhere.

Avoid anthropomorphizing the system merely to create narrative unity.

Coordinate with `anthropomorphism-audit`.

---

# Whole-Work and A–E Nodes

## Step 16 — Test the Current Node Architecture

When the work contains the A–E inter-stem architecture, assess whether:

* each node has a distinct transformation;
* the nodes are not simply a thematic list;
* their relations are meaningful;
* they do not form an unintended causal sequence;
* one node does not absorb the others;
* the sequence is organizational rather than deterministic.

The current transformations may include:

* **A:** observable capacities → attribution of mental capacities;
* **B:** representation → intervention;
* **C:** technical capacity → strategic capacity;
* **D:** information mediation → relevance and authority;
* **E:** production → evidence → attribution.

These should be treated as working architectural distinctions, not as assumptions that override the actual text.

---

# Whole-Work and Propagation

## Step 17 — Test Whether Transformations Extend Beyond Their Origin

Ask whether the work demonstrates transformations that:

* cross assemblages;
* change conditions elsewhere;
* generate new relations;
* produce feedback;
* alter another scale;
* create further tensions.

Do not require every node to propagate.

A work can contain local transformations without every transformation becoming systemic.

Coordinate with `propagation-analysis`.

---

# Scale and Trajectory

## Step 18 — Track Scale Changes

Identify movements among:

* individual experience;
* technical systems;
* institutions;
* infrastructures;
* organizations;
* society;
* geopolitics;
* historical processes.

Ask whether transitions are supported.

A work can move across scales without treating them as a single homogeneous level.

Coordinate with `scale-analysis`.

---

# Materiality Across the Work

## Step 19 — Test Material Continuity

Track whether material conditions remain visible where they matter:

* energy;
* water;
* minerals;
* semiconductors;
* compute;
* data centers;
* networks;
* devices;
* logistics;
* storage;
* infrastructure.

Materiality should not appear as an isolated appendix if it materially conditions other dynamics.

At the same time, do not force materiality into every section where it has no analytical function.

---

# Power Across the Work

## Step 20 — Track Power Relations

Ask whether power appears as a distributed relational dimension rather than as an isolated topic.

Look for:

* concentration;
* dependency;
* asymmetry;
* institutional authority;
* infrastructure;
* ownership;
* access;
* visibility;
* selection;
* standards.

A whole-work reading should detect whether power is structurally connected to other dynamics.

Coordinate with `power-mediation-authority`.

---

# Causality Across the Work

## Step 21 — Test for Hidden Master Causality

A work may begin with relational complexity but gradually reduce everything to:

> technology → society

or:

> AI → transformation.

Check whether the larger trajectory preserves:

* multiple causal pathways;
* conditions;
* mediation;
* feedback;
* contingency;
* distributed causality.

Do not eliminate legitimate causal claims.

The issue is whether one causal model silently becomes the explanation for the entire work.

Coordinate with `causality-propagation`.

---

# Reader Trajectory

## Step 22 — Reconstruct the Reader's Movement

Ask:

* What does the reader initially expect?
* What changes that expectation?
* Where does the reader encounter the map?
* When do relations become visible?
* When do tensions become significant?
* When does the observer become implicated?
* Does the ending transform the reader's understanding of the opening?

This is not a literary-effect exercise.

It is a structural test of whether the work actually produces a trajectory.

---

# Autonomy Test

## Step 23 — Test Whether the Work Can Stand Alone

Assume the reader has **not** participated in the project's previous discussions.

Ask whether the work itself provides enough information to understand:

* the central problem;
* the meaning of the map;
* the role of the rhizome;
* the meaning of assemblages;
* the meaning of nodes;
* the meaning of inter-stem nodes;
* the observer's position;
* the principal transformations;
* the ending.

Do not require the text to reproduce the entire methodology.

The question is whether the work is intelligible without hidden external instructions.

Coordinate with `blind-external-reading`.

---

# Necessity of the Whole

## Step 24 — Perform the Whole-Work Removal Test

Ask counterfactually:

> What would happen if each major section disappeared?

Determine whether its removal would:

* break the trajectory;
* remove a necessary distinction;
* erase a transformation;
* eliminate a connection;
* weaken the ending;
* leave the work substantially unchanged.

This is a whole-work version of structural necessity.

Coordinate with `necessity-redundancy`.

---

# Ending Test

## Step 25 — Test the Final Movement

Ask whether the ending:

* returns to the initial problem;
* integrates the trajectory;
* preserves unresolved questions;
* avoids totalization;
* acknowledges the limits of the map;
* closes the work without closing the rhizome.

A strong ending does not need to summarize every section.

It needs to complete the trajectory.

Coordinate with `ending-openness`.

---

# Whole-Work Stress Test

Perform these counterfactual readings:

### Collection Test

Read each major section independently.

Does the work lose something when the relations among them disappear?

### Thread Test

Remove the presumed central conceptual thread.

Does the architecture still make sense?

If not, determine whether the thread is genuinely structural or artificially imposed.

### Node Test

Remove one major node.

Does another part of the work compensate for it?

### Reordering Test

Could major sections be reordered without conceptual loss?

If yes, the sequence may be organizational rather than developmental.

If no, identify the dependency.

### Ending Test

Read the ending without the preceding trajectory.

Does it depend on developments actually established earlier?

---

# Common Whole-Work Failures

## Collection Without Trajectory

Strong sections but weak relation among them.

## Linearization

A complex field reduced to a single sequence.

## Centralization

One concept becomes the explanation of everything.

## Fragmentation

Each node becomes an independent essay.

## Hidden Taxonomy

The map becomes a classification system rather than a relational construction.

## False Unity

The work appears coherent only because differences are suppressed.

## False Openness

The work claims openness while the conclusion closes every question.

## Reflexive Disconnection

The author's use of AI is mentioned but never becomes conceptually relevant.

## Unprepared Ending

The conclusion introduces claims that the work has not developed.

---

# False Positives

Do **not** classify the work as fragmented merely because:

* sections differ greatly;
* nodes have different lengths;
* connections are not all explicit;
* some questions remain unresolved;
* the work contains multiple scales;
* some sections are more independent;
* the ending is open;
* the architecture is asymmetrical;
* the work does not follow a linear argument.

Do not classify it as incoherent merely because it does not resemble a conventional academic paper.

---

# False Negatives

Look carefully for:

* disconnected thematic essays;
* a hidden master thesis;
* artificial unity;
* unexplained transitions;
* repeated independent conclusions;
* nodes that never interact;
* inter-stem nodes that are only labels;
* a methodological framework that disappears during the actual analysis;
* an ending disconnected from the trajectory;
* concepts that recur without development;
* the observer's position stated but never integrated;
* materiality or contingency mentioned but structurally isolated.

---

# Output Protocol

For each significant finding, report:

### Whole-Work Dimension

Identify what is being evaluated:

* trajectory;
* unity;
* development;
* relation among sections;
* conceptual thread;
* node architecture;
* observer position;
* scale;
* materiality;
* power;
* causality;
* ending;
* autonomy.

### Evidence

Identify the relevant passages and structural relations.

### Whole-Work Analysis

Explain how the part functions within the complete work.

### Diagnosis

Classify the finding as:

* strong;
* partially strong;
* fragmented;
* over-centralized;
* under-connected;
* structurally unresolved;
* productive openness;
* totalizing;
* otherwise justified.

### Classification

Use:

* **A — Critical:** the problem compromises the work as a whole.
* **B — Important:** the problem materially affects trajectory or unity.
* **C — Local:** a limited intervention would improve whole-work coherence.
* **D — Stylistic:** the issue mainly affects presentation or rhetorical continuity.
* **E — Non-problematic:** the apparent problem is justified by the work's conceptual operation.

### Confidence

Use:

* High
* Medium
* Low

### Implication

Explain what the finding means for the work as a whole.

### Recommendation

Choose:

* Necessary
* Advisable
* Optional
* Not recommended
* Leave unchanged

# Intervention Threshold

Do not recommend restructuring merely because the work could be made:

* more linear;
* more symmetrical;
* more explicit;
* more conventional;
* easier to summarize.

Intervene only when:

1. a genuine break in the work's trajectory exists;
2. a necessary relation is missing;
3. a section lacks a meaningful function in the whole;
4. the architecture creates a false causal or hierarchical sequence;
5. the ending is not supported by the preceding trajectory;
6. the work cannot be understood without substantial hidden context.

When the work's heterogeneity is productive:

> **Preserve the heterogeneity.**

When its openness is deliberate:

> **Preserve the openness.**

When asymmetry reflects the mapped field:

> **Preserve the asymmetry.**

# Final Principle

> **A work does not become coherent because every part says the same thing.**

Coherence can arise through:

* relations;
* transformations;
* tensions;
* returns;
* crossings;
* contrasts;
* dependencies;
* changes of scale;
* reflexive movements.

The work should therefore be read neither as:

> a collection of independent sections

nor as:

> a single thesis into which everything must fit.

It is a trajectory through a constructed map.

The map gives the work enough stability for the reader to follow it.

The rhizomatic field exceeds that stability.

The whole-work audit asks whether the text keeps both dimensions alive.

> **The work needs enough unity to be a work, but enough multiplicity to remain faithful to what it is trying to map.**
