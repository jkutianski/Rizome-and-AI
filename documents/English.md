# AI Seen from a Rhizome

## Opening

There is something important I should clarify: I used AI to organize and polish the writing. The ideas, arguments, and responsibility for the content are mine. I did this because Andy Clark and David Chalmers’ notion of extended cognition is particularly relevant here; if I am writing about the impact of AI on society, experimenting with it as a possible extension of my own thinking is part of the very problem this text is trying to explore.

This also raises a question: to what extent does incorporating a tool into the thinking process allow us to consider it part of our cognition? In the case of AI, the question becomes even more difficult because I am not dealing with a passive tool, but with a system that responds, proposes connections, and can lead me to question what I am thinking. The system’s response introduces variations that can alter the development of the process, and it is precisely this participation that makes it more difficult to establish where the tool ends and the cognitive process begins.

By incorporating it into the writing process, I also change the way I am constructing the map. If the map is also a construction of the observer, then I cannot remain completely outside what I am trying to represent. Everything I write or read becomes another connection. This text does not explain the rhizome; it passes through it.

I do not need to determine whether AI possesses understanding in order to observe that its participation modifies the dynamics of the cognitive process in which it intervenes. This does not mean that previous tools were cognitively passive: a book, a pencil, a calculator, or a search engine can also transform what we are able to think and do. What is particular about this coupling lies in the possibility that AI can generate a response that feeds back into the process and may alter its course. It is not, therefore, a matter of abandoning the instrumental dimension, but of recognizing a form of instrumentality capable of intervening in the development of the cognitive circuit without implying that it has intentions of its own.

There is a difficulty that I think is important to acknowledge: this text cannot claim to represent the rhizome neutrally. Constructing the map means selecting some connections and leaving others out, and AI now adds another layer to that process. The map does not merely represent the rhizome; it also modifies our perception of it, and the person constructing it is not outside what they are trying to represent. This journey, which began as a personal reflection, now leads me to construct a map for observing how different relationships and tensions run through assemblages and become concentrated in nodes that allow us to trace the expansion of AI.

In the opening of a talk for the Asociación Técnica de Diarios Latinoamericanos in 2010, I put forward an idea that still seems relevant to me: there are not two separate worlds, one technological and the other social, but a single world in which technology and society transform one another. I also observed how technologies that were once new eventually become incorporated into everyday life until they become part of culture. Today, faced with the expansion of AI, that relationship appears again under new conditions. This idea was developed in the text “[On Technology and Society](https://latintaenlasopa.blogspot.com/2010/10/sobre-la-tecnologia-y-la-sociedad.html).”

That same year, in “[Come to My Cloud and Let Me Tell You](https://latintaenlasopa.blogspot.com/2010/08/veni-mi-nube-que-te-cuento.html),” I was concerned with the growth of information and the need to find relevance within volumes that had already exceeded our capacity to process them directly. What then appeared as a problem of search has now acquired another dimension. Large amounts of information can become part of the data used to train models, which learn patterns from them and construct representations that they later use to generate new responses.

I also wrote “[Technology Seen from a Rhizome](https://latintaenlasopa.blogspot.com/2010/10/la-tecnologia-vista-desde-un-rizoma.html),” where I explored the notion of the rhizome as a dynamic of connections and assemblages. Today I can recognize those three concerns: the relationship between technology and society, the expansion of information, and the rhizomatic perspective. I can now take them up again in a new journey to analyze the expansion of AI.

A node appears when certain connections accumulate enough tension to form a recognizable concentration within the field. That concentration can, in turn, modify other relationships. A node should not be understood merely as a point on the map, but as a process of transition in which those relationships begin to reorganize. The concept of assemblage allows me to capture relatively stable configurations of relationships and tensions within a broader domain, in which particular nodes become concentrated. The substrate refers to the material and technological conditions that enable, limit, and condition the development and expansion of these dynamics. Inter-stem nodes appear when these relationships and tensions cross the boundary between different assemblages or domains and modify their operating conditions, generating transformations that can propagate beyond the point of encounter. When such a configuration produces a dynamic that depends on the relationships among its elements and cannot adequately be attributed to any one of them individually, we can speak of emergence.

Although this approach may be useful, it also has a limit: nodes do not emerge from a vacuum, but from prior relations of knowledge, materiality, and power. There are multiple relationships and possibilities that can acquire consistency through new interactions or through changes in the goals of one or more actors. People, institutions, and states participate in this process, and their decisions and interests can modify the relationships that give rise to new concentrations.

The rhizome is constantly changing, and among the many possible trajectories, some connections become configured while others do not. The map captures only one moment of those connections, and whoever constructs it selects what to represent and what to leave out. Therefore, when I speak of the rhizome, I do not simply mean a network of connections: I am interested in observing how those connections can produce new relationships between different elements and modify those that already exist.

The classification of nodes is a tool of the map that makes certain concentrations of relationships and tensions recognizable, but it is not a property of the territory I am trying to represent. I need to organize the rhizome in order to think about it, while knowing that this organization inevitably simplifies the representation I construct of it.

Based on these ideas, I therefore propose a first cartography of some of the concentrations of relationships and tensions involved in the expansion of AI.

## The Scientific Assemblage

From this framework, the question is no longer which disciplines “invented” AI, but rather tracing the relationships that made its emergence possible. It is not so much a matter of identifying fields that came together as of observing what happens when problems, methods, concepts, and forms of representation originating in different territories begin to operate upon one another. These crossings do not all occur at the same time, nor do they respond to a common plan; some remain without acquiring consistency for decades, while others gain unexpected importance when they encounter new conditions for development.

We habitually say that models “understand” or “handle” language, but what appears first is actually a series of operations through which language could begin to be treated computationally. Large amounts of text were converted into digital corpora, processed through tokenizers and part-of-speech taggers, and subjected to different types of statistical analysis.[^1] Through this movement, the human practice of producing and understanding language began to interact with operations that made it possible to represent and manipulate language inside a machine. Language begins to become entangled with computation through these very operations. Representing it, however, has never been equivalent to understanding it. When discourse analysis attempts to move beyond the sentence in order to trace coherence, relationships, and structures across large volumes of text, this entanglement deepens: new ways of connecting linguistic units, mathematical representations, and processing capacity emerge.

But these representations require operations capable of working with them at a scale that far exceeds manual calculation. Here another decisive relationship appears: representations of language begin to rely on mathematical structures capable of transforming and comparing them on a massive scale. We tend to assume that linear algebra was simply there, available as a passive toolbox that models could use. Yet scale transforms the relationship between those operations and the infrastructure required to execute them. In mechanisms such as attention, for example, the parallelization of operations and the movement of data between different levels of memory modify the material conditions under which those operations can be performed efficiently. Algebra enabled certain operations; the need to execute them at that scale concentrated a new node in the relationship between mathematics, algorithms, and computing architecture.

As these systems attempted to represent a world and a language that are not completely determinate, another tension emerged: if uncertainty was to be incorporated explicitly into the generation process, it was not enough to work with a single possible outcome; it was necessary to represent and use probability distributions. The mathematical tools of statistics and probability made it possible to introduce this dimension. In an autoregressive language model, each new token is produced from a probability distribution conditioned by the context and by learned patterns.[^1] Here, what has been learned becomes entangled with what the system can produce next. If a variable in that context changes, the distribution can shift and lead generation toward an entirely different territory. Uncertainty therefore ceases to appear merely as something to be reduced or resolved and becomes part of the very conditions under which the system produces.

Elsewhere in this assemblage, a different relationship appears. Cognitive science and neuroscience do not function here as models that computation attempts to faithfully copy, but as sources of concepts and intuitions that can be abstracted and transformed. Neural networks incorporate highly simplified ideas inspired, among other things, by the possibility of modifying the strength of a connection through experience, in analogy with synaptic plasticity. What is interesting is not so much that one discipline “contributed” an idea to another, but what happens when that intuition changes territory. Once transferred to the synthetic domain, the idea of plasticity becomes part of architectures whose parameters can be adjusted through algorithms. Biology does not disappear, but neither does it dictate the rules: a conceptual relationship becomes detached from its origin and acquires new possibilities within another assemblage.

Something similar occurs with information theory. The notion of entropy, formulated by Claude Shannon to quantify the uncertainty associated with a probability distribution, makes it possible to relate information and uncertainty. In learning models, these relationships become articulated with probabilistic processes of prediction and adjustment. The probability distribution changes, the error guides new adjustments, and those adjustments modify the conditions for the next prediction. The node is therefore not located in information theory on one side and machine learning on the other, but in the relationship that emerges between them and allows the learning process to be reorganized.

But this ability to find regularities and produce predictions introduces another tension. In scientific research, models can identify complex relationships in large volumes of data and produce predictions or hypotheses that are difficult to interpret. The ability to find regularities can, in some cases, exceed our ability to understand why those regularities work. The problem is not simply the existence of a “black box,” but the possible separation between producing useful knowledge and understanding the mechanism that supports it. AI can therefore expand our ability to anticipate certain phenomena without that predictive capacity necessarily implying an equivalent understanding of why those predictions work.

Seen this way, the scientific assemblage of AI does not appear as a sum of disciplines that each contributed pieces to a machine imagined in advance. It appears as a series of relationships in which concepts, operations, and problems change function when they come into contact with one another. Some connections enable new operations; others transfer intuitions from one territory to another; others generate problems that force existing relationships to be reorganized. It is through these transformations, rather than through the disciplines considered in isolation, that the nodes forming this assemblage begin to become concentrated.

## The Technological Assemblage (Materiality, Formalization, Documentation, and Integration)

Here, infrastructure, silicon, algorithms, and data are not static layers, but components that condition, reorganize, and transform one another in order to sustain computation.

If the scientific dimension provided the formal framework, this movement requires us to look at what that formality rests upon. We tend to think of artificial intelligence as an abstraction floating in the cloud, but when we observe the technological assemblage, we come face to face with a dense, tangible materiality and with a path that could have taken a different direction within the rhizome. It is fascinating to notice that one of the material supports that made it possible to bring AI to scale was not designed for that purpose.

We commonly say that GPUs played a central role in the expansion of deep learning, but what appears first is an encounter between an architecture developed for graphics processing and the computational needs of neural networks. An architecture designed to perform thousands of geometric operations in parallel per second eventually fit the mathematics of neural networks.[^3] The graphics card of entertainment became a formal laboratory, a connection that was not on the original map but that contributed to reshaping the industry. Alongside this convergence, hardware also began to specialize deliberately: accelerators specifically designed for machine-learning workloads appeared in data centers, such as TPUs, as well as specialized units integrated into phones and local devices, such as NPUs.[^19][^20] Part of this computing capacity is thus moving toward the edges of the system.

But matter is not merely specialized; we are also beginning to design it to incorporate some of the dynamics we observe. The idea of plasticity, inspired by biology and developed in software, has also begun to be explored in hardware design. But this is not a single phenomenon: there are architectures that seek to reduce data movement, proposals for training directly on hardware, ways of physically storing parameters, and approaches to analog or specialized computing. Each addresses the relationship between the model and the material substrate differently. Neuromorphic architectures seek to reproduce, in simplified form, some patterns of activation and interaction found in biological networks within silicon. Some of these architectures bring memory and computation closer together and seek to reduce data movement, thereby modifying the relationship between processing, memory, and resource consumption.[^4] In parallel, another line of development takes the integration between the biological and the synthetic into a different territory. Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) cause the boundary between biological tissue and synthetic circuitry to cease being merely a conceptual frontier and become a matter of architectural design. We still do not know what forms this relationship might take when such interfaces are combined with AI systems, but their very possibility shifts the boundary between biological intelligence and artificial systems toward a territory that we can still barely anticipate.

Now, all this computing capacity depends on an enormous infrastructural substrate. Data centers can increasingly be understood less as simple spaces for housing servers and more as distributed systems in which thousands of machines operate as coordinated infrastructure. And beneath them, sustaining the assemblage, there is a material layer that is often absent from abstract discussions about intelligence: energy and water.[^5] Electrical grids and cooling systems are not passive settings in which AI happens; they are part of the material conditions that make computation possible. The operation of these systems requires energy, heat dissipation, infrastructure, and physical resources that must be sustained over time. The capacity to process information therefore depends on a material substrate that does not disappear when intelligence becomes digital.

However, all this material capacity requires logical forms to organize what needs to be computed, and here a relationship of mutual conditioning between infrastructure and algorithmic innovation appears. Backpropagation made it possible to calculate how the parameters of a network should be modified based on error,[^6] allowing learning to adjust those parameters without requiring their values to be specified manually. Later, architectures such as the Transformer reorganized this relationship again by introducing attention mechanisms[^7] that allowed large portions of a sequence to be processed in parallel during training. Every modification in the way computation is organized also transforms the demands placed on the infrastructure that must execute it. The algorithm does not float in a vacuum; it conditions how matter must organize its operations.

This logical architecture still lacked the sediment upon which to build. The massive digitization accumulated over decades transformed the volume and availability of the materials on which these systems could be trained. Large corpora of text, images, and audio reached scales that made it possible to work with modalities and volumes of data that had previously been difficult to process.[^1] Already in 2011, deep-learning architectures appeared that were capable of bringing different modalities together within a common representation.[^14] This documentary archive is not merely an inert data repository; it is the raw material from which models extract regularities, construct representations of language, and reconstruct contexts.

When material infrastructure, logical architecture, and the documentary archive reach an extreme degree of integration, the most suggestive movement of this assemblage occurs: the boundaries between hardware, software, and data begin to become less distinct. In approaches such as In-Memory Computing, the system seeks to reduce the movement of data toward the processor by performing certain operations where the data are stored.[^15] In On-Chip Training, this integration advances another step when certain architectures allow the learning process to modify the physical states of the device while the system operates. In other approaches to specialized computing, model parameters can become physically incorporated into the device, reducing the distance between the model and the material support that executes it.[^16] The plasticity of the model thus begins to encounter the physical limits of matter. Not because movement stops, but because it becomes increasingly difficult to separate what the model is, what it does, and what it is made of.

This integration also makes visible a tension between the openness of architectures and the material conditions that allow them to be used. The availability of models with open weights, open-source software, or tools that can be modified and redistributed can expand the possibilities for experimentation and deployment, but that openness requires infrastructure capable of sustaining it. Running, adapting, or training a model requires memory, accelerators, energy, devices, and technical knowledge. Even when part of the processing is moved to local devices, the substrate does not disappear: it redistributes its demands among the device, the network, and the data center. The possibility of accessing a model does not guarantee the ability to run it; the ability to run it does not guarantee the ability to train it; and the ability to modify it does not necessarily imply having the material conditions to sustain its development. Opening one layer does not therefore mean opening the entire assemblage.

The technological assemblage thus reveals a paradox: the expansion of capabilities that appear increasingly abstract depends on increasingly deep material integration. Openness can expand the entry points into the system, but the effective possibilities of accessing, running, modifying, or developing these capabilities continue to depend on how their material and formal conditions are organized.

The technological assemblage is therefore not simply a support for the other territories. Its material and formal configurations condition which forms of computation can exist, where they can be deployed, and what relationships can be established among those who produce, execute, modify, and use the systems. Technology does not merely transport relationships coming from other assemblages: by organizing the material and formal conditions of computation, it actively participates in their transformation.

## The Sociocultural Assemblage

To look at the sociocultural assemblage is to address the moment when this material and formal web unfolds within our everyday ways of inhabiting the world. Social practices, institutions, and legal frameworks are not passive settings into which technology falls; they actively condition which systems are developed, how they are adopted, and what place they eventually occupy. AI does not merely introduce new technical capabilities: as it becomes incorporated into existing practices, it modifies the conditions under which we work, learn, produce, and circulate information, while those same practices can generate new demands, data, restrictions, and directions that intervene in the next cycle of technical development. The sociocultural assemblage thus appears as a feedback circuit in which technology transforms practices and practices feed back into technology.

A first concentration appears in the reorganization of work and learning practices. AI is also entering activities that we previously associated primarily with intellectual, creative, and administrative work.[^8] The change does not lie merely in the replacement of isolated tasks, but in the reconfiguration of how production is organized, which capabilities become valuable, and how responsibility is redistributed between people and systems. By redefining what forms of organization are possible, work practices themselves can generate new data, new demands, and new directions that intervene in the next cycle of technical development.

A similar transformation appears in learning practices. Interaction with systems capable of adapting explanations, examples, and exercises can facilitate learning processes, but it can also reduce some of the cognitive effort that students must invest in particular tasks, modifying the conditions under which they formulate problems, make mistakes, and construct their own answers.[^18] The same tool that expands the possibilities for learning can modify the conditions under which learning takes place. The question is not simply whether AI facilitates or hinders learning, but how its intervention redistributes cognitive effort and transforms the practices through which learning occurs.

A second concentration appears when the expansion of these systems begins to modify the conditions under which we produce, verify, and regulate what circulates socially. Faced with synthetic media, a difficulty emerges that is not limited to determining whether the information contained in a message is true or false, but to establishing whether an image, voice, or video whose authenticity may be difficult to determine can still function as evidence that something actually happened. The difficulty of establishing authenticity does not simply destroy prior trust; it may drive the development of new authentication and verification mechanisms that, in turn, transform how we produce and circulate information.[^13]

This reproduction of the world carries traces of its own origin. If models are fed by massive volumes of data produced by society, the historical patterns of inequality, bias, and discrimination embedded in those records do not necessarily disappear; they may be reproduced, transformed, or, under certain conditions, amplified. The ethical dimension ceases to be an external addition or a list of normative principles and becomes a structural problem of design. Deciding which data are selected, how they are weighted, and which uses are authorized are political and social decisions that can directly shape the architecture of the system that comes next. Social relationships are therefore not only inscribed in systems; they also feed back into them through the decisions that determine which data can be used, which objectives are prioritized, and which uses are considered acceptable.

And the language that, within the scientific assemblage, began to be translated, tokenized, and processed computationally now returns to the sociocultural plane in the form of a conflict over authorship. If training feeds on the accumulated sediment of intellectual and cultural production, technology puts pressure on the rules through which we have historically regulated culture. Training on copyrighted works creates legal crossroads that put pressure on the traditional categories through which we regulate the use of works. Intellectual property does not appear here as a fixed set of laws to be applied, but as a category in mutation, attempting to adapt to the new conditions created by the scale of computation and model training.[^9][^10]

The sociocultural assemblage therefore does not appear as a collection of social effects produced by an already constituted technology. It appears as a configuration in which practices, institutions, norms, and technical systems modify one another. AI can reorganize forms of work and learning, and it can also modify the conditions of trust, authorship, regulation, and legitimacy. It is in this reciprocal transformation that the sociocultural dynamics of AI become concentrated.

## Inter-Stem Nodes

Inter-stem nodes appear when the relationships and tensions of AI cross into other assemblages and generate transformations that modify the relationships between them. They are not simply topics that combine two territories, but concentrations in which the crossing begins to modify the operating conditions of the assemblages involved.

### AI + Philosophy of Mind: Consciousness, Understanding, and Agency

This node is concentrated at the intersection between the technological assemblage and the conceptual and epistemological configurations through which we attempt to interpret mental capacities. On the one hand, AI systems produce behaviors that may appear associated with capacities such as understanding, reasoning, or acting; on the other, those behaviors feed back into the criteria through which we attempt to decide what counts as understanding, consciousness, or agency. The relationship is not simply a matter of applying philosophical concepts to an already constituted technology. The emergence of these behaviors puts pressure on the conditions under which these categories can be discussed, while the categories and criteria we use to interpret them also modify which questions we ask about the systems and what kind of evidence we consider relevant. It is in this feedback that the intersection acquires its own concentration.

The problem, then, is not to decide in advance whether an AI thinks or understands, but to observe what happens when a technological system begins to produce behaviors in response to which our categories of mental attribution no longer function as stably as they once did. The central transformation of this node is not to demonstrate that AI possesses a mind, but to shift the conditions under which we attribute, or refuse to attribute, mental capacities to a system.

* **Extended cognition:** Interaction with generative AI makes the idea of extended cognition more complex. If a tool can recurrently modify the development of a cognitive process, the question is no longer only what the tool does, but under what conditions an operation can be considered part of a cognitive system. The difficulty is that AI introduces responses that are not simply stored or consulted, but can be incorporated into the process and modify the operations that follow. This shifts the question from the location of cognition toward the boundaries of the cognitive system and the conditions under which a relationship with a tool can be considered constitutive of that process.

* **The tension between syntax and semantics:** Models can manipulate linguistic structures and produce responses that appear appropriate to context without this demonstrating that they understand their meaning in the same way we do. The tension between syntax and semantics remains open. But the difficulty arises precisely because the behavior can be functionally convincing. If a machine can use concepts, relate them, reformulate them, and respond contextually, then the criteria we use to recognize understanding become less stable. The question is not to conclude that AI understands, but to ask what we mean by understanding when behavior associated with it can appear without our being able to determine what kind of understanding is involved, or whether any such understanding exists.

* **Complex behavior and consciousness:** AI allows us to observe systems capable of producing increasingly complex behaviors, but that complexity does not necessarily imply phenomenal experience. A tension therefore emerges between functional capability and subjective experience: we can observe what a system does without being able to determine what, if anything, it experiences. The problem is not to decide whether AI is conscious, but to reconsider what evidence we consider sufficient to attribute consciousness to a system whose behavior can be complex without giving us direct access to its experience. AI thus introduces a situation that modifies the relationship between observable behavior, mental capacity, and experience.

* **Agency and autonomy:** Something similar occurs with agency. An AI can intervene causally in a decision, modify an action, or produce a recommendation that someone later adopts, without this implying that it has intentions of its own or that responsibility can be attributed to it in the same sense as to a person. The tension appears when intervening in an action, determining its course, and assuming responsibility for it cease to coincide.

This shifts the question from who has agency toward how an action is configured when people, models, data, interfaces, institutions, and rules intervene. Autonomy ceases to be simply a property of the system and becomes related to the degree to which its intervention can modify the course of an action without being reduced to the execution of a previously defined operation. The question is not to attribute agency to every component, but to observe how the incorporation of AI modifies the conditions under which we attribute action, intention, and responsibility.

These tensions do not remain within the philosophy of mind. The question of understanding extends into territories where AI participates in the production and recognition of knowledge; the reconfiguration of action and responsibility relates to forms of power and decision that run through other assemblages. The question is not to decide from the outside whether AI thinks, but to observe what happens when behaviors associated with mental capacities make unstable the categories through which we attempt to interpret them. The tool with which I think is also part of the assemblage I am attempting to describe: when I interact with it, the problem is not to decide whether it has a mind, but to observe how a system that produces variations enters human circuits of thought, writing, and decision-making. The boundary between mind and tool does not necessarily disappear, but it ceases to function as a simple and stable frontier.

### AI + Biology: From Describing to Designing

The space where this intersection becomes most radical is the intersection between AI and biology. If, within the scientific assemblage, biology appeared mainly as a source of intuitions for algorithmic architecture, as in the abstraction of synaptic plasticity, once it becomes an inter-stem node the relationship changes in nature: biology ceases to be merely an object that AI can analyze and begins also to become a space for simulation, prediction, and design. At the same time, problems specific to biology begin to demand new forms of processing and modeling. The relationship therefore moves from a logic of application toward a logic of feedback, in which AI modifies the operating conditions of biological practice while, at the same time, the problems emerging from that practice feed back into forms of processing and modeling.

This transformation can be observed at different levels of biology. In DNA and RNA sequence design, AI is beginning to become incorporated into design processes that already existed, expanding the possibility of exploring and proposing sequences within spaces of possibilities that are difficult to traverse manually. In the case of proteins, the prediction of three-dimensional structures modifies the relationship between sequence, structure, and experimentation, allowing laboratory work to be directed toward the validation and refinement of hypotheses generated computationally. The combination of genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data extends this logic toward models of cellular processes, where relationships between multiple levels can be explored as a dynamic system.[^17] Finally, the simulation of large numbers of variants shifts part of biological exploration into a computational space in which possibilities can be traversed before being taken to the laboratory. Taken together, these shifts show that AI not only makes it possible to represent what exists with greater scale and speed, but also to explore in advance what might come to exist.[^11]

Several tensions emerge from this shift across the relationship between AI and biology:

* **Representation and design:** The representation of biological systems ceases to function solely as a description or interpretation of what exists and also begins to participate in the generation of possibilities that do not yet exist. The model no longer merely represents a biological object; it can intervene in defining what might eventually be designed.

* **Computational exploration and experimental validation:** The ability to traverse large spaces of possibilities through computational models modifies the function of experimentation. The laboratory does not disappear, but it may move from directly exploring a broad space of possibilities toward validating and refining hypotheses selected or generated computationally. A feedback circuit emerges among model, hypothesis, experimentation, and new data.

* **Biological complexity and modeling capacity:** The integration of genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic information makes it possible to explore relationships across different levels of biological organization, but it also makes visible the distance between modeling capacity and the complexity of the phenomenon being represented. The ability to integrate more variables does not imply that the model can fully capture the dynamics of the biological system.

* **Computational possibility and biological possibility:** The expansion of the space of possibilities that can be generated or explored through AI does not imply that all of them are biologically realizable. Between what can be proposed computationally and what can be materialized lie experimental, material, energetic, and biological constraints that bring the laboratory and the substrate back into the process.

When these variations move beyond the internal procedures of science, the inter-stem node begins to propagate into other assemblages and generates new tensions:

* **Geopolitics and bioeconomy:** AI-assisted design of microorganisms and biological systems capable of producing fuels, degrading materials, or synthesizing compounds may modify the strategic value of resources and shift part of industrial sovereignty toward those who control design platforms, data, and production capacity. The ability to computationally intervene in the design of biological systems also begins to become a strategic resource.

* **Health and precision medicine:** The integration of biomedical models with individual data may move part of medicine toward a more predictive logic, generating new tensions concerning privacy, access, insurance, medical responsibility, and the limits of what we consider modifiable in the body.

* **Security and biosecurity:** The same ability to explore and design biological systems that opens therapeutic and industrial possibilities also introduces new risks. As models increase their capacity to work with biological information, traditional biosecurity mechanisms must adapt to a scenario in which some forms of exploration that previously required experimental infrastructure may begin with computational tools.[^11] The problem is no longer only what can be done in a laboratory, but also what capabilities can be enabled through design and prediction systems.

The node appears here in the transformation of the relationship between representation and intervention: AI moves from functioning primarily as an instrument for representing and analyzing biological systems to intervening in the space of possibilities of what can be designed within them. Representation therefore ceases to function solely as description and begins to become part of a circuit in which modeling, prediction, proposal, and experimentation feed back into one another. From this shift, the distinction between analyzing and producing begins to lose its stability.

The transformation does not remain contained within scientific practice. When the ability to design living matter connects with industrial production, medicine, or security, computationally generated possibilities begin to modify other assemblages. It is in this propagation, rather than simply in the application of AI to biology, that the intersection acquires its character as an inter-stem node.

### AI + Geopolitics and Technological Sovereignty

The expansion of AI begins to modify geopolitical relationships when the ability to produce and use these systems ceases to be merely a technological advantage and becomes a strategic capability. Access to computing, semiconductors, data centers, specialized talent, and energy then acquires a value that extends beyond the technical domain. Technology enters the logic of sovereignty and, by doing so, also transforms what states consider necessary to control.

The production of semiconductors required to develop and deploy AI systems is not distributed evenly. This production depends on a highly fragmented global value chain, with strong specialization in certain economies, concentration of critical inputs, and dependencies among different segments of production.[^12]

But this strategic capability does not exist in an abstract space. A model depends on material infrastructure; that infrastructure depends on supply chains, energy, capital, territory, and knowledge; and these dependencies are distributed unevenly among different actors and territories.[^12] AI may operate as an apparently delocalized service, but its conditions of possibility remain anchored in a concrete geography. The cloud does not eliminate territory: it redistributes it and, in some cases, makes its importance less visible.

This dynamic concentrates several tensions:

* **Technological sovereignty versus dependency:** A state’s ability to develop AI does not necessarily imply technological autonomy. It may depend on processors produced in other territories, external computing platforms, supply chains it does not control, or energy and connectivity infrastructures that limit its possibilities for action. Technological sovereignty therefore does not appear as an absolute condition of independence, but as the capacity to negotiate, reduce, or redistribute certain dependencies. At the same time, the concentration of computing capabilities can reinforce those dependencies, while interdependence among actors means that even those possessing strategic resources depend on other components of the system.

* **Technological openness versus material concentration:** The open availability of models, knowledge, or code can formally expand access to certain capabilities, but this openness does not eliminate the material conditions required to use them. A publicly available model may still require large amounts of memory, computing capacity, specialized accelerators, energy, or infrastructure to be trained, adapted, or run at a given scale. The openness of a technology can therefore coexist with a concentration of the material conditions that make it effective. Openness and accessibility cease to be equivalent.

* **Technology policy versus technical trajectory:** When AI acquires strategic value, decisions concerning research, manufacturing, infrastructure, technology transfer, and talent development cease to be merely technical decisions and become part of national strategies. Policy begins to intervene in technological trajectories, while available technical capabilities in turn delimit political options. Investments, trade restrictions, standards, and industrial policies can favor particular trajectories, but those trajectories also generate inertia and dependencies that condition later decisions.

* **Territory versus transnational infrastructure:** Data centers, networks, semiconductor factories, and energy and cooling systems are located in concrete territories, but the infrastructures supporting AI operate through relationships that cross borders. This produces a tension between the material localization of resources and the transnational scale of the networks that connect them. Territory concentrates costs, resources, energy, and environmental effects, while the benefits and capabilities that depend on these infrastructures may be distributed far beyond the places where they are installed.

These tensions form a feedback circuit: technological capability modifies power relationships; power relationships guide investments, restrictions, and industrial policies; those decisions reorganize infrastructures and supply chains; and the new infrastructures once again modify the technological capabilities available. Sovereignty, openness, and political decisions therefore do not appear as conditions external to technological development, but as relationships that participate in shaping its trajectories.

Geopolitics thus ceases to function merely as the context in which AI unfolds and begins also to participate in shaping the conditions of its development. Material infrastructure ceases to function as a simple technological support and becomes a component of the power relationships that condition who can develop particular capabilities, under what conditions, and with what degree of dependency.

These transformations propagate into other assemblages:

* **Reorganization of supply chains:** The search for technological autonomy can encourage the relocation, diversification, and protection of particular segments of production chains. AI infrastructure can therefore contribute to reorganizing industrial territories and trade relationships that were previously not connected to this technology in the same way.

* **Transformation of industrial policy:** Computing, semiconductors, and other capabilities considered critical may become increasingly integrated into national strategies for investment, subsidies, research, and development. Technology policy may cease to operate merely as support for innovation and begin to intervene in the deliberate construction of strategic capabilities.

* **Reconfiguration of state-business relationships:** When a significant portion of AI capability is concentrated in companies that control models, platforms, infrastructure, and specialized knowledge, relationships between public authorities and private actors acquire new forms. The technological capacity of particular companies also begins to become a component of states’ strategic capacity, generating relationships of cooperation, dependency, and negotiation that cross traditional institutional boundaries.

* **Territorial displacement of material costs:** The concentration of computing in particular territories can shift demands for energy, water, land, and infrastructure onto those territories, while others remain connected to AI expansion through mineral extraction, energy production, or labor associated with its supply chains. The spatial distribution of capabilities and their costs thus begins to acquire a new geopolitical dimension.

The node therefore connects different territories of the map. Its material dependencies relate to the technological assemblage and to the substrate sustaining computation; its effects on production chains and industrial policy extend into the relationships between technology, economy, and power; and the struggle over technological capabilities establishes a connection with **AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority**, where the issue appears from the standpoint of the capacity to intervene in the mediations through which knowledge is organized and acquires authority.

The inter-stem node is therefore not reduced to competition among states. Its dynamics emerge when a technological capability becomes a strategic capability and, in doing so, simultaneously reorganizes the relationships of sovereignty, dependency, infrastructure, and territory that condition its own expansion.

### AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority

At the intersection between knowledge and power, a particularly significant concentration of relationships and tensions emerges. AI participates in the mechanisms through which information is organized, ranked, made visible, and presented as valid knowledge. Systems incorporating AI do not merely process information: they can also intervene in its selection, synthesis, and circulation, modifying the conditions under which something can acquire relevance, authority, and legitimacy. AI does not determine by itself what is valid, but it can intervene in the conditions through which particular pieces of information, interpretations, or responses come to occupy a relevant position and acquire authority.

The issue is not merely whether a response is true or false. Before reaching that point, there is a series of operations that condition what information appears, how it is related to other information, what is left out of the response, and from what position it can be interpreted. AI thus begins to participate in a dimension of power that does not consist merely in controlling information, but in intervening in the conditions under which that information can acquire relevance and authority.

Authority is not equivalent to truth. A claim may acquire authority within a community, institution, or circulation system and nevertheless be false or subsequently challenged. Authority here refers to the position from which a claim can guide the judgment of others, not to a guarantee that the claim is true. Legitimacy introduces another dimension: it refers not merely to the ability to guide judgment, but to recognition of the position, procedure, or conditions from which that authority is exercised.

This issue differs from the evaluation of knowledge because relevance and authority do not by themselves determine that a production constitutes evidence of knowledge. **AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority** concerns the conditions through which information, interpretation, or a response acquires visibility, relevance, and authority; **AI + Evaluation of Knowledge**, by contrast, concerns the conditions under which a production can function as evidence and allow knowledge to be attributed. A production may acquire relevance and authority without thereby constituting sufficient evidence to attribute knowledge.

Relevance and authority are not equivalent either: something can acquire a relevant position within a circulation without thereby acquiring authority, just as a source can retain authority without necessarily occupying a position of maximum visibility.

The central tension of the node therefore lies between the capacity of AI systems to organize, select, and mediate information and the distribution of power to determine what acquires visibility, relevance, and authority within a field of knowledge. The concentration lies not only in the information produced, but in the conditions that allow particular pieces of information, interpretations, or responses to be found, related, synthesized, legitimized, and taken into consideration.

This transformation can be observed through several tensions:

* **Centralization of relevance:** AI systems can concentrate the determination of what information acquires visibility and relevance in the systems, institutions, and actors that design, train, implement, or control them. The ability to select and order can cease to be distributed solely among multiple mediators and shift toward new forms of intermediation.

* **Opacity of mediation:** AI-generated synthesis can make it difficult to reconstruct why certain sources, relationships, or interpretations received greater weight than others. The authority of a response can appear detached from the operations through which it was constructed, making the process supporting its legitimacy less visible.

* **Appearance of neutrality:** Algorithmic mediation can present itself as a technical or neutral operation, even though its results depend on data, design criteria, objectives, constraints, and human and institutional decisions. What appears to be automatic selection can contain decisions distributed throughout the entire assemblage.

* **Synthesis and reduction of complexity:** Condensing large amounts of information facilitates access, but it can also displace differences, contradictions, and contexts that were part of the original problem. The clarity of a response can simultaneously produce a loss of complexity that modifies what can be considered relevant.

* **Openness and concentration of epistemic power:** Access to, modification of, or openness of models can expand the ability to intervene in the forms of knowledge mediation, but it does not eliminate existing concentrations of computing, data, infrastructure, or specialized knowledge.

* **Auditability and complexity:** As systems, training processes, and operating conditions become more complex, it can become more difficult to reconstruct the operations that produce particular results. The ability to audit a system therefore becomes part of the dispute over its authority.

* **Reconfiguration of knowledge production and circulation:** The incorporation of AI into search, synthesis, interpretation, and distribution tasks modifies not only the practices through which knowledge is produced, but also the paths through which that knowledge circulates and acquires visibility.

These tensions produce a transformation that can propagate into other spaces. AI can modify the conditions under which information acquires relevance and authority, but those conditions do not remain static as AI expands. Institutions, users, knowledge producers, and other actors begin adapting their practices to an increasingly present form of mediation, generating new forms of search, validation, circulation, and control. A circuit is thus configured in which AI transforms the conditions of mediation, while transformations in those practices in turn modify the conditions under which AI participates in them.

This dynamic can propagate in different directions:

* **Transformation of epistemic authority:** When AI responses become privileged points of access to information, the relationship between sources, experts, institutions, and users may change, partially displacing traditional forms through which authority is recognized.

* **Transformation of mediation:** Search, selection, synthesis, and presentation operations can become concentrated in a single interface, modifying the role of mediators that were previously distributed among different institutions, tools, and actors.

* **Reconfiguration of knowledge circulation:** Information begins to travel through circuits in which algorithmic mediation actively participates in its selection, presentation, and distribution, modifying the conditions under which particular content reaches different audiences.

* **Reconfiguration of the space of knowledge:** By making certain relationships between pieces of information visible and leaving others in the background, AI can modify the structure through which a field of knowledge is explored, making some connections more accessible than others.

* **Dispute over cognitive sovereignty:** The ability to decide what information can be found, which systems are used, and which criteria operate introduces a dimension of sovereignty that extends beyond material infrastructure and reaches the conditions of access to and mediation of knowledge.

* **Redistribution of mediation capacity:** The openness, modification, and auditability of systems can expand who has the capacity to intervene in these conditions, although that possibility remains limited by the material, economic, and technical concentrations that run through the assemblage.

This node connects directly with **AI + Evaluation of Knowledge**, but from a different position. There, the central question is what allows knowledge to be attributed to a subject when their production may be mediated by AI. Here, the question is how information, interpretation, or a response acquires relevance and authority within a field of knowledge, and who can intervene in those conditions. It also connects with **AI + Geopolitics and Technological Sovereignty**, although again from a different level: there, sovereignty concerns strategic control over material capabilities, infrastructure, and resources; here, a cognitive dimension of sovereignty appears, linked to the capacity to intervene in the conditions of access, mediation, and legitimation of knowledge. The dispute over open, auditable, or modifiable models introduces an additional dimension: whoever can intervene in the system can, at least partially, contest the conditions of that mediation, although that possibility remains shaped by the material, economic, and technical concentrations of the assemblage. AI therefore intervenes not only in what circulates, but also in the conditions under which something can acquire relevance and authority.

### AI + Evaluation of Knowledge

The evaluation of knowledge begins with an operation that often remains implicit: inferring something we cannot directly observe—the knowledge of a subject—from what we can observe, such as their answers, productions, explanations, or solutions. The incorporation of generative AI introduces an additional layer of mediation into this relationship. An answer may be produced through interaction between a person and a system, and its correctness alone cannot determine which operations belong to the subject, which were performed with assistance, or which capabilities can effectively be attributed to the person presenting the result.[^21]

The tension is not merely about determining whether a production was created with AI, but about the fact that the relationship between production, evidence, and attribution of knowledge begins to become less stable. Evaluation seeks to recognize a capability from an observable manifestation, but when that manifestation emerges from an interaction between a subject and a system, the result may no longer provide such direct evidence of what is being evaluated. AI therefore modifies the conditions under which a production can function as evidence that someone knows something.

This transformation can be observed through several tensions:

* **Result and understanding:** A correct result can coexist with different degrees of understanding on the part of the person presenting it. AI makes the relationship between the quality of a production and the cognitive capacity being evaluated less direct. The result retains its value, but its ability to function as evidence changes when some of the operations leading to it may have been performed through interaction with AI.

* **Product and process:** When the final production is not sufficient by itself to reconstruct how it was reached, evaluation may shift toward the process: what decisions the subject made, what they can explain, defend, modify, or reconstruct, and how they intervened in the result. Evaluation thus begins to seek new ways of making visible what the final product no longer allows us to infer by itself. The process ceases to be merely the path toward the result and also becomes part of the evidence.

* **Knowing and demonstrating that one knows:** AI makes more visible a separation that evaluation often keeps relatively unified: possessing a capability and producing a demonstration of that capability. Using a tool does not necessarily imply an absence of knowledge, but neither does it allow us to assume that the knowledge demonstrated in the result exists independently of it. The question becomes what relationship with the tool allows a capability to be attributed to the subject, and when that relationship transforms what the evaluation was intended to recognize.

* **Evidence and traceability:** When a production can result from an interaction between a subject and AI, evaluation may attempt to reconstruct which operations belong to each participant. Explaining, defending, modifying, or reconstructing an answer then acquires value as evidence. But this search for traceability is not neutral: in attempting to recover attribution of knowledge, it also modifies the conditions under which AI can participate in the production and demonstration being evaluated.

These tensions produce a transformation that can propagate into other spaces. AI modifies what can function as evidence of knowledge, but evaluation practices do not simply wait for that modification. By changing the ways in which capabilities are observed, demonstrated, and attributed, they begin to establish new conditions for AI participation. A feedback circuit is thus configured in which AI transforms the conditions of evaluation, while transformations in evaluation in turn modify the conditions under which AI can participate in the production and demonstration of knowledge.

This dynamic can propagate in different directions:

* **Reorganization of evaluation practices:** The ways of observing, demonstrating, and reconstructing knowledge change when a production may be mediated by AI.

* **Transformation of the criteria of knowledge:** When the result is no longer sufficient by itself, greater weight may be placed on understanding, explanation, decision-making, modification, and the ability to reconstruct the process.

* **Redistribution of evaluative authority:** As the conditions of evidence change, who can intervene in the attribution of knowledge and according to which criteria also changes.

* **Transformation of valued capabilities:** When the conditions under which we recognize that someone knows something change, what capabilities come to be considered relevant for learning, working, and acting with AI also changes.

The node connects with **AI + Philosophy of Mind**, but through a different problem: there, the question is what understanding means when a system produces behaviors associated with understanding; here, the question is what allows us to recognize that a subject understands when their production may be mediated by AI. It also connects with **AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority**, because every evaluation presupposes criteria that determine what can function as evidence and who has the authority to establish them.

AI does not eliminate the need to evaluate knowledge; it modifies the conditions under which a production can function as evidence that someone knows something. When the relationship between result and capability ceases to be relatively direct, evaluation involves determining what can be attributed from that production and under what conditions.
