# AI Viewed from a Rhizome

## Opening

Something important that I should clarify is that I used AI to organize and polish the wording. The ideas, arguments, and responsibility for the content are mine. I did this because Andy Clark and David Chalmers’s notion of extended cognition is especially relevant here;[^2] if I write about the impact of AI on society, experimenting with it as a possible extension of my own thinking is part of the problem that this text is trying to explore.

This also raises a question: to what extent does incorporating a tool into the thinking process allow us to consider it part of our cognition? In the case of AI, the question becomes even more difficult because I am not dealing with a passive tool, but with a system that responds, proposes connections, and can lead me to question what I am thinking. The system's response introduces variations that can modify the development of the process, and it is precisely this participation that makes it more difficult to establish where the tool ends and the cognitive process begins.

By incorporating it into the writing process, the way in which I am constructing the map also changes. If the map is also a construction of the observer, then I cannot remain completely outside what I am trying to represent either. Everything I write or read becomes one more connection. This text does not explain the rhizome; it moves through it.

AI does not need to contribute a cognitive capacity of its own in order to modify the dynamics of the cognitive process in which it participates. This does not mean that earlier tools were cognitively passive: a book, a pencil, a calculator, or a search engine can also transform what we are able to think and do. What is particular about this coupling is the possibility that AI can generate a response that feeds back into the process and can modify its course. It is not, then, a matter of abandoning the instrumental dimension, but of recognizing a form of instrumentality capable of intervening in the development of the cognitive circuit without implying that it has an intention of its own.

There is a difficulty that I think is important to acknowledge; this text cannot claim to represent the rhizome neutrally. Constructing the map means selecting some connections and leaving others out, and AI now adds a new layer to that process. The map does not merely represent the rhizome; it also modifies our perception of it, and the person constructing it is not outside what they are trying to represent either. This journey, which began as a personal reflection, now leads me to construct a map in order to observe how different relationships and tensions run through assemblages and become concentrated in nodes that allow us to follow the expansion of AI.

In the opening of a talk for the Asociación Técnica de Diarios Latinoamericanos in 2010, I put forward an idea that still seems relevant to me: there are not two separate worlds, one technological and the other social, but a single one, in which technology and society transform each other. I also observed how technologies that were once new eventually become incorporated into everyday life until they become part of culture. Today, faced with the expansion of AI, that relationship appears again under new conditions. This idea was expressed in the text “[On Technology and Society](https://latintaenlasopa.blogspot.com/2010/10/sobre-la-tecnologia-y-la-sociedad.html).”

That same year, in “[Come to My Cloud and I’ll Tell You About It](https://latintaenlasopa.blogspot.com/2010/08/veni-mi-nube-que-te-cuento.html),” I was concerned with the growth of information and with the need to find relevance within volumes that had already exceeded our capacity to process them directly. What then appeared as a search problem now acquires another dimension. Large amounts of information can become part of the data used to train models, which learn patterns from them and construct representations that they later use to generate new responses.

I also wrote “[Technology Viewed from a Rhizome](https://latintaenlasopa.blogspot.com/2010/10/la-tecnologia-vista-desde-un-rizoma.html),” where I explored the notion of the rhizome as a dynamic of connections and assemblages. Today I can recognize those three concerns: the relationship between technology and society, the expansion of information, and the rhizomatic perspective. I can now take them up again in a new journey to analyze the expansion of AI.

A node appears when certain connections accumulate enough tension to form a recognizable concentration within the field. That concentration can, in turn, modify other relationships. The node should not be thought of merely as a point within the map, but as a process of transition in which those relationships begin to reorganize themselves. The concept of assemblage allows me to capture relatively stable configurations of relationships and tensions within a general domain, in which certain nodes become concentrated. The substrate names the material and technological conditions that enable, limit, and condition the development and expansion of these dynamics. Inter-stem nodes appear when these relationships and tensions cross the boundary between different assemblages or domains and modify their operating conditions, generating transformations that can propagate beyond the point of encounter. When such a configuration produces a dynamic that depends on the relationships between the elements and cannot be adequately attributed to any one of them separately, we can speak of emergence.

Although this approach can be useful, it also has a limit; nodes do not emerge from a vacuum, but arise from prior relations of knowledge, materiality, and power. There are multiple relationships and possibilities that can acquire consistency through new interactions or transformations in the goals of one or more actors. People, institutions, and states intervene in this process, and their decisions and interests can modify the relationships that give rise to new concentrations.

The rhizome is changing, and among the multiple possible trajectories, some connections become configured and others do not. The map captures only one moment of those connections, and whoever constructs it selects what to represent and what to leave out. Therefore, when I speak of the rhizome, I do not simply mean a network of connections: I am interested in observing how those connections can produce new relationships between different elements and modify those that already exist.

The classification of nodes is a tool of the map that makes certain concentrations of relationships and tensions recognizable, but it is not a property of the territory I am trying to represent. I need to organize the rhizome in order to think about it, knowing that this organization inevitably simplifies the representation I construct of it.

Based on these ideas, I therefore propose a first cartography of some of the concentrations of relationships and tensions involved in the expansion of AI.

## The Scientific Assemblage

From this framework, the question is no longer which disciplines “invented” AI, but rather tracing the relationships that made its emergence possible. It is less a matter of identifying fields that meet than of observing what happens when problems, methods, concepts, and forms of representation originating in different territories begin to operate upon one another. These crossings do not all occur at the same time or respond to a common plan; some remain without acquiring consistency for decades, while others gain unexpected importance when they encounter new conditions for development.

We habitually say that models “understand” or “handle” language, but what appears there first is a series of operations through which language could begin to be treated computationally. Large quantities of text were converted into digital corpora, processed through tokenizers and part-of-speech taggers, and subjected to different types of statistical analysis.[^1] In this movement, the human practice of producing and understanding language began to become connected with operations that made it possible to represent and manipulate it within a machine. Language begins to knot itself with computation through these operations. Representing it, however, did not by itself amount to understanding it. When discourse analysis attempts to go beyond the sentence in order to trace coherence, relationships, and structures across large volumes of text, this knotting deepens: new ways of connecting linguistic units, mathematical representations, and processing capacity appear.

But these representations require operations capable of working with them at a scale that vastly exceeds manual calculation. Another decisive relationship appears here: representations of language begin to rely on mathematical structures capable of transforming and comparing them on a massive scale. We tend to assume that linear algebra was simply there, available as a passive toolbox that models could use. Yet scale introduces a transformation in the relationship between those operations and the infrastructure required to execute them. In mechanisms such as attention, for example, the parallelization of operations and the movement of data between different levels of memory modify the material conditions under which those operations can be performed efficiently. Algebra enabled certain operations; the need to execute them at that scale concentrated a new node in the relationship between mathematics, algorithms, and computing architecture.

As these systems attempted to represent a world and a language that are not completely determined, another tension emerged: if uncertainty was to be explicitly incorporated into the generation process, it was not enough to work with a single possible outcome; it was necessary to represent and use probability distributions. The mathematical tools of statistics and probability made it possible to introduce this dimension. In an autoregressive language model, each new token is produced from a probability distribution conditioned by the context and by learned patterns.[^1] This is where what has been learned and what the system may produce next become knotted together. If a variable in that context changes, the distribution can shift and lead generation toward a completely different territory. Uncertainty then ceases to appear merely as something that must be reduced or resolved and becomes part of the very conditions under which the system produces.

At another point in this assemblage, a different relationship appears. Cognitive science and neuroscience do not function here as models that computation attempts to faithfully copy, but as sources of concepts and intuitions that can be abstracted and transformed. Neural networks incorporate highly simplified ideas inspired, among other things, by the possibility of modifying the strength of a connection through experience, in analogy with synaptic plasticity. What is interesting is not so much that one discipline “contributed” an idea to another, but what happens when that intuition changes territory. When transferred to the synthetic domain, the idea of plasticity becomes part of architectures whose parameters can be adjusted through algorithms. Biology does not disappear, but neither does it dictate the rules: a conceptual relationship detaches itself from its origin and acquires new possibilities within another assemblage.

Something similar happens with information theory. The notion of entropy, formulated by Claude Shannon to quantify the uncertainty associated with a probability distribution, makes it possible to relate information and uncertainty. In learning models, these relationships are articulated with probabilistic processes of prediction and adjustment. The probability distribution changes, the error guides new adjustments, and those adjustments modify the conditions for the next prediction. The node is therefore not information theory on one side and machine learning on the other, but the relationship produced between them that makes it possible to reorganize the learning process.

But this capacity to find regularities and produce predictions introduces another tension. In scientific research, models can identify complex relationships in large volumes of data and produce predictions or hypotheses that are difficult to interpret. The ability to find regularities can, in some cases, exceed our ability to understand why those regularities work. The problem is not simply the existence of a “black box,” but the possible separation between producing useful knowledge and understanding the mechanism that sustains it. AI can thus expand our ability to anticipate certain phenomena without that predictive capacity necessarily implying an equivalent understanding of why those predictions work.

Seen in this way, the scientific assemblage of AI does not appear as a sum of disciplines that contributed pieces to a machine imagined beforehand. It appears as a series of relationships in which concepts, operations, and problems change function when they come into contact with one another. Some connections enable new operations; others transfer intuitions from one territory to another; others generate problems that force existing relationships to be reorganized. It is in these transformations, rather than in the disciplines considered in isolation, that the nodes forming this assemblage begin to concentrate.

## The Technological Assemblage (Materiality, Formalization, Documentation, and Integration)

Here, infrastructure, silicon, algorithms, and data are not static layers, but components that condition, reorganize, and transform one another in order to sustain computation.

If the scientific dimension provided the formal framework, this movement requires us to look at where that formality rests. We tend to think of artificial intelligence as an abstraction floating in the cloud, but when we observe the technological assemblage, we come face to face with a dense, tangible materiality and with a path that could have been different within the rhizome. And it is fascinating to notice how one of the material supports that made it possible to bring AI to scale was not designed for this purpose.

We habitually say that GPUs played a central role in the expansion of deep learning, but what appears there first is an encounter between an architecture developed for graphics processing and the computational needs of neural networks. An architecture designed to solve thousands of geometric operations in parallel per second ended up fitting the mathematics of neural networks.[^3] The entertainment graphics card became a formal laboratory, a connection that was not on the original map but that helped reconfigure the industry. Alongside this convergence, hardware also began to specialize deliberately: accelerators specifically designed for machine-learning workloads in data centers appeared, such as TPUs, as well as specialized units integrated into phones and local devices, such as NPUs.[^19][^20] Part of this computing capacity thus moves toward the edges of the system.

But matter does not only become specialized; we are also beginning to design it to incorporate some of the dynamics we observe. The idea of plasticity, inspired by biology and developed within software, also began to be explored in hardware design. But this is not a single phenomenon: there are architectures that seek to reduce data movement, proposals for training directly on hardware, ways of physically storing parameters, and approaches to analog or specialized computing. Each addresses the relationship between the model and the material substrate differently. Neuromorphic architectures seek to reproduce, in simplified form, certain patterns of activation and interaction present in biological networks within silicon. Some of these architectures bring memory and computation closer together and seek to reduce data movement, thereby modifying the relationship between processing, memory, and resource consumption.[^4] In parallel, another line of development takes the integration between the biological and the synthetic into a different territory. Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) make the boundary between biological tissue and synthetic circuitry cease to be a conceptual frontier and turn it into a matter of architectural design. We still do not know what forms that relationship might take when these interfaces become articulated with AI systems, but the mere possibility shifts the boundary between biological intelligence and artificial systems toward a territory that we can still barely anticipate.

Now, all this computational capacity depends on an enormous infrastructural substrate. Data centers can increasingly be understood less as simple spaces for housing servers and more as distributed systems in which thousands of machines operate as coordinated infrastructure. And beneath them, sustaining the assemblage, there is a material layer that is often left out of abstract discussions about intelligence: energy and water.[^5] Electrical grids and cooling systems are not passive scenery in which AI takes place; they are part of the material conditions that make computation possible. The operation of these systems requires energy, thermal dissipation, infrastructure, and physical resources that must be sustained over time. The ability to process information therefore depends on a material substrate that does not disappear when intelligence becomes digital.

However, all this material capacity needs logical forms to organize what must be computed, and here a relationship of mutual conditioning between infrastructure and algorithmic innovation appears. Backpropagation made it possible to calculate how to modify the parameters of a network based on error[^6], allowing learning to adjust those parameters without having to specify their values manually. Later, architectures such as the Transformer[^7] reorganized this relationship again by making attention central to an architecture that could process large portions of a sequence in parallel during training. Each modification in the way computation is organized also transforms the demands placed on the infrastructure required to execute it. The algorithm does not float in a vacuum; it conditions how matter must organize its operations.

This logical architecture still lacked the sediment upon which to build. The massive digitization accumulated over decades transformed the volume and availability of the materials on which these systems could be trained. Large corpora of text, images, and audio reached scales that made it possible to work with modalities and volumes of data that had previously been difficult to process.[^1] Already in 2011, deep-learning architectures appeared that could articulate different modalities within a single representation.[^14] This documentary archive is not merely an inert data repository; it is the raw material from which models extract regularities, construct representations of language, and reconstruct contexts.

When material infrastructure, logical architecture, and the documentary archive reach an extreme degree of integration, the most suggestive movement of this assemblage occurs; the boundaries between hardware, software, and data begin to become less distinct. In schemes such as In-Memory Computing, the system seeks to reduce the movement of data toward the processor by performing certain operations where the data are stored.[^15] In On-Chip Training, this integration advances one step further when certain architectures allow the learning process to modify physical states of the device while the system operates. In other approaches to specialized computing, a model's parameters may become physically incorporated into the device, reducing the distance between the model and the material support that executes it.[^16] The model's plasticity thus begins to encounter the physical limits of matter. Not because movement stops, but because it becomes increasingly difficult to separate what the model is, what it does, and what it is made of.

This integration also makes visible a tension between the openness of architectures and the material conditions that make their use possible. The availability of models with open weights, open-source software, or tools that can be modified and redistributed can expand the possibilities for experimentation and deployment, but that openness requires infrastructure capable of sustaining it. Running, adapting, or training a model requires memory, accelerators, energy, devices, and technical knowledge. Even when part of the processing moves toward local devices, the substrate does not disappear: it redistributes its demands among the device, the network, and the data center. The possibility of accessing a model does not guarantee being able to run it; being able to run it does not guarantee being able to train it; and being able to modify it does not necessarily imply having the material conditions to sustain its development. Opening one layer therefore does not amount to opening the entire assemblage.

The technological assemblage thus reveals a paradox: the expansion of capabilities that appear increasingly abstract depends on increasingly deep material integration. Openness can expand the points of entry into the system, but the effective possibilities of accessing, running, modifying, or developing those capabilities continue to depend on how their material and formal conditions are organized.

The technological assemblage is therefore not simply a support for the other territories. Its material and formal configurations condition what forms of computation can exist, where they can be deployed, and what relationships can be established among those who produce, run, modify, and use the systems. Technology does not merely transport relationships that come from other assemblages: by organizing the material and formal conditions of computation, it actively participates in their transformation.

## The Sociocultural Assemblage

Looking at the sociocultural assemblage means addressing the moment when this material and formal web unfolds within our everyday ways of inhabiting the world. Social practices, institutions, and legal frameworks are not the passive setting in which technology falls; they actively condition which systems are developed, how they are adopted, and what place they eventually occupy. AI does not merely introduce new technical capabilities: as it becomes incorporated into existing practices, it modifies the conditions under which we work, learn, produce, and circulate information, while those same practices can generate new demands, data, restrictions, and orientations that intervene in the next cycle of technical development. The sociocultural assemblage thus appears as a feedback circuit in which technology transforms practices and practices feed back into technology.

A first concentration appears in the reorganization of work and learning practices. AI also enters activities that we previously associated primarily with intellectual, creative, and administrative work.[^8] The change does not lie merely in the replacement of isolated tasks, but in the reconfiguration of how production is organized, which capabilities become valuable, and how responsibility is redistributed between people and systems. By redefining what forms of organization are possible, work practices themselves can produce new data, new demands, and new orientations that intervene in the next cycle of technical development.

A similar transformation appears in learning practices. Interaction with systems capable of adapting explanations, examples, and exercises can facilitate learning processes, but it can also reduce some of the cognitive effort that students must invest in certain tasks, modifying the conditions under which they formulate problems, make mistakes, and construct their own answers.[^18] The same tool that expands the possibilities of learning can modify the conditions under which that learning takes place. The question is not simply whether AI facilitates or hinders learning, but how its intervention redistributes cognitive effort and transforms the practices through which learning occurs.

A second concentration appears when the expansion of these systems begins to modify the conditions under which we produce, verify, and regulate what circulates socially. Faced with synthetic media, a difficulty emerges that is not limited to determining whether the information contained in a message is true or false, but to establishing whether an image, voice, or video whose authenticity may be difficult to determine can continue to function as evidence that something actually happened. The difficulty of establishing authenticity does not simply destroy previous trust; it can drive the development of new authentication and verification mechanisms that, in turn, can transform the way we produce and circulate information.[^13]

This reproduction of the world carries the traces of its own origin. If models feed on massive volumes of data produced by society, the historical patterns of inequality, bias, and discrimination inscribed in those records do not necessarily disappear; they can be reproduced, transformed, or, under certain conditions, amplified. The ethical dimension ceases to be an external addition or a list of normative principles and becomes a structural problem of design. Deciding which data are selected, how they are weighted, and which uses are authorized are political and social decisions that can directly shape the architecture of the system to come. In this way, social relationships are not only inscribed in systems: they also feed back into them through the decisions that determine which data can be used, which objectives are prioritized, and which uses are considered acceptable.

And that language which, in the scientific assemblage, began to be translated, tokenized, and processed computationally now returns to the sociocultural plane in the form of a conflict over authorship. If training feeds on the accumulated sediment of intellectual and cultural production, technology puts pressure on the rules through which we have historically regulated culture. Training on copyrighted works opens legal crossroads that put pressure on the traditional categories through which we regulate the use of works. Intellectual property does not appear here as a fixed set of laws to be applied, but as a category in mutation that attempts to adapt to the new conditions created by the scale of computation and model training.[^9][^10]

The sociocultural assemblage therefore does not appear as a collection of social effects produced by an already constituted technology. It appears as a configuration in which practices, institutions, norms, and technical systems modify one another. AI can reorganize forms of work and learning, and it can also modify the conditions of trust, authorship, regulation, and legitimation. It is in this reciprocal transformation that the sociocultural dynamics of AI become concentrated.

## Inter-Stem Nodes

Inter-stem nodes appear when the relationships and tensions of AI cross into other assemblages and generate transformations that modify the relationships between them. These are not simply topics that combine two territories, but concentrations in which the crossing begins to modify the operating conditions of the assemblages involved.

### AI + Philosophy of Mind: Consciousness, Understanding, and Agency

This node is concentrated at the intersection between the technological assemblage and the conceptual and epistemological configurations through which we attempt to interpret mental capacities. On the one hand, AI systems produce behaviors that may appear associated with capacities such as understanding, reasoning, or acting; on the other, those behaviors feed back into the criteria through which we attempt to decide what counts as understanding, consciousness, or agency. The relationship does not simply consist in applying philosophical concepts to an already constituted technology. The emergence of these behaviors puts pressure on the conditions under which these categories can be discussed, while the categories and criteria we use to interpret them also modify which questions we ask about the systems and what kind of evidence we consider relevant. It is in this feedback that the crossing acquires its own concentration.

The problem, then, is not to decide in advance whether an AI thinks or understands, but to observe what happens when a technological system begins to produce behaviors in response to which our categories of mental attribution cease to function as stably as before. The central transformation of this node does not consist in demonstrating that AI has a mind, but in shifting the conditions under which we attribute, or refuse to attribute, mental capacities to a system.

* **Extended cognition:** Interaction with generative AI makes the idea of extended cognition more complex. If a tool can recurrently modify the development of a cognitive process, the question is no longer simply what the tool does, but under what conditions an operation can be considered part of a cognitive system. The difficulty is that AI introduces responses that are not simply stored or consulted, but can be incorporated into the process and modify the operations that follow. This shifts the question from the location of cognition toward the boundaries of the cognitive system and the conditions under which a relationship with a tool can be considered constitutive of that process.

* **The tension between syntax and semantics:** Models can manipulate linguistic structures and produce responses that appear appropriate to the context without this demonstrating that they understand their meaning in the same way we do. The tension between syntax and semantics remains open. But the difficulty arises precisely because the behavior can be functionally convincing. If a machine can use concepts, relate them, reformulate them, and respond contextually, then the criteria we use to recognize understanding cease to be as stable. The question is not to conclude that AI understands, but to ask what we mean by understanding when a behavior that we associate with it can appear without our being able to determine what kind of understanding, if any, is involved.

* **Complex behavior and consciousness:** AI allows us to observe systems capable of producing increasingly complex behaviors, but that complexity does not necessarily imply phenomenal experience. A tension therefore emerges between functional capacity and subjective experience: we can observe what a system does without this allowing us to determine what, if anything, it experiences. The problem is not to decide whether AI is conscious, but to reconsider what evidence we consider sufficient to attribute consciousness to a system whose behavior can be complex without our having direct access to its experience. AI thus introduces a situation that modifies the relationship between observable behavior, mental capacity, and experience.

* **Agency and autonomy:** Something similar occurs with agency. An AI can causally intervene in a decision, modify an action, or produce a recommendation that someone later adopts, without this implying that it has an intention of its own or that responsibility can be attributed to it in the same sense as to a person. The tension appears when intervening in an action, determining its course, and assuming responsibility for it cease to coincide.

This shifts the question from who has agency toward how an action is configured when people, models, data, interfaces, institutions, and rules intervene. Autonomy ceases to be simply a property of the system and becomes related to the degree to which its intervention can modify the course of an action without being reduced to the execution of a previously defined operation. The question is not to attribute agency to each component, but to observe how the incorporation of AI modifies the conditions under which we attribute action, intention, and responsibility.

These tensions do not remain within the philosophy of mind. The question of understanding extends into territories where AI participates in the production and recognition of knowledge; the reconfiguration of action and responsibility relates to the forms of power and decision that run through other assemblages. The question is not to decide from outside whether AI thinks, but to observe what happens when behaviors we associate with mental capacities make the categories through which we attempt to interpret them unstable. The tool with which I think is also part of the assemblage I am trying to describe: when I interact with it, the problem is not to decide whether it has a mind, but to observe how a system that produces variations enters human circuits of thought, writing, and decision-making. The boundary between mind and tool does not necessarily disappear, but it ceases to function as a simple and stable frontier.

### AI + Biology: From Describing to Designing

The space where this crossing becomes most radical is the intersection between AI and biology. If, in the scientific assemblage, biology appeared primarily as a source of intuitions for algorithmic architecture, as in the abstraction of synaptic plasticity, when it becomes an inter-stem node the relationship changes in nature: biology ceases to be merely an object that AI can analyze and begins also to become a space of simulation, prediction, and design. At the same time, problems specific to biology begin to demand new forms of processing and modeling. The relationship thus moves from a logic of application to a logic of feedback, in which AI modifies the operating conditions of biological practice while, at the same time, the problems arising within that practice feed back into forms of processing and modeling.

This transformation can be observed at different levels of biology. In the design of DNA and RNA sequences, AI is beginning to become incorporated into design processes that already existed, expanding the possibility of exploring and proposing sequences within spaces of possibilities that are difficult to traverse manually. In the case of proteins, the prediction of three-dimensional structures modifies the relationship between sequence, structure, and experimentation, making it possible to orient laboratory work toward the validation and refinement of hypotheses generated computationally. The combination of genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data extends this logic toward models of cellular processes, where relationships among multiple levels can be explored as a dynamic system.[^17] Finally, the simulation of large numbers of variants shifts part of biological exploration toward a computational space in which possibilities can be traversed before they are taken to the laboratory. Taken together, these shifts show that AI not only makes it possible to represent what exists at greater scale and speed, but also to explore in advance what might come to exist.[^11]

Several tensions emerge from this shift and run through the relationship between AI and biology:

* **Representation and design:** The representation of the biological ceases to function solely as a description or interpretation of what exists and begins also to participate in the generation of possibilities that do not yet exist. The model is no longer limited to representing a biological object, but can intervene in defining what might eventually be designed.

* **Computational exploration and experimental validation:** The ability to traverse large spaces of possibilities through computational models modifies the function of experimentation. The laboratory does not disappear, but it may move from directly exploring a broad space of possibilities toward validating and refining hypotheses selected or generated computationally. A feedback circuit is thus configured among model, hypothesis, experimentation, and new data.

* **Biological complexity and modeling capacity:** The integration of genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic information makes it possible to explore relationships among different levels of biological organization, but it also makes visible the distance between modeling capacity and the complexity of the phenomenon being represented. The possibility of integrating more variables does not imply that the model can fully capture the dynamics of the biological system.

* **Computational possibility and biological possibility:** The expansion of the space of possibilities that can be generated or explored through AI does not imply that all of them are biologically realizable. Between what can be proposed computationally and what can be materialized, there are experimental, material, energetic, and biological constraints that bring the laboratory and the substrate back into the process.

When these variations move beyond the internal procedures of science, the inter-stem node begins to propagate into other assemblages and generates new tensions:

* **Geopolitics and bioeconomics:** AI-assisted design of microorganisms and biological systems capable of producing fuels, degrading materials, or synthesizing compounds can modify the strategic value of resources and shift part of industrial sovereignty toward those who control design platforms, data, and production capacity. The ability to computationally intervene in the design of biological systems also begins to become a strategic resource.

* **Health and precision medicine:** The integration of biomedical models and individual data can shift part of medicine toward a more predictive logic, generating new tensions around privacy, access, insurance, medical responsibility, and the limits of what we consider modifiable in the body.

* **Security and biosecurity:** The same ability to explore and design biological systems that opens therapeutic and industrial possibilities also introduces new risks. As models increase their ability to work with biological information, traditional biosecurity mechanisms must adapt to a scenario in which some forms of exploration that previously required experimental infrastructure can begin with computational tools.[^11] The problem is no longer only what can be done in a laboratory, but also what capabilities can be enabled through design and prediction systems.

The node appears here in the transformation of the relationship between representation and intervention: AI moves from functioning primarily as an instrument for representing and analyzing biological systems to intervening in the space of possibilities of what can be designed within them. Representation thus ceases to function solely as description and begins to form part of a circuit in which modeling, prediction, proposal, and experimentation feed back into one another. From this shift, the distinction between analyzing and producing begins to lose its stability.

The transformation does not remain contained within scientific practice. When the capacity to design living matter becomes connected with industrial production, medicine, or security, the possibilities generated computationally begin to modify other assemblages. It is in this propagation, and not simply in the application of AI to biology, that the crossing acquires its character as an inter-stem node.

### AI + Geopolitics and Technological Sovereignty

The expansion of AI begins to modify geopolitical relationships when the capacity to produce and use these systems ceases to be merely a technological advantage and becomes a strategic capability. Access to computing, semiconductors, data centers, specialized talent, and energy then acquires a value that exceeds the technical sphere. Technology enters the logic of sovereignty and, in doing so, also transforms what states consider necessary to control.

The production of semiconductors required to develop and deploy AI systems is not distributed homogeneously. This production depends on a highly fragmented global value chain, with strong specialization among certain economies, concentration of critical inputs, and dependencies among different segments of production.[^12]

But this strategic capability does not exist in an abstract space. A model depends on material infrastructure; that infrastructure depends on supply chains, energy, capital, territory, and knowledge; and these dependencies are distributed unequally among different actors and territories.[^12] AI can operate as an apparently delocalized service, but its conditions of possibility remain anchored in a concrete geography. The cloud does not eliminate territory: it redistributes it and, in some cases, makes its importance less visible.

This dynamic concentrates several tensions:

* **Technological sovereignty versus dependency:** A state's capacity to develop AI does not necessarily imply technological autonomy. It may depend on processors produced in other territories, external computing platforms, supply chains it does not control, or energy and connectivity infrastructures that limit its possibilities for action. Technological sovereignty therefore does not appear as an absolute condition of independence, but as the capacity to negotiate, reduce, or redistribute certain dependencies. At the same time, the concentration of computing capabilities can reinforce those dependencies, while interdependence among actors means that even those possessing strategic resources depend on other components of the system.

* **Technological openness versus material concentration:** The open availability of models, knowledge, or code can formally expand access to certain capabilities, but this openness does not eliminate the material conditions necessary to use them. A publicly available model may still require large amounts of memory, computing capacity, specialized accelerators, energy, or infrastructure to be trained, adapted, or run at a given scale. The openness of a technology can therefore coexist with a concentration of the material conditions that make it effective. Open and accessible cease to be equivalent.

* **Technology policy versus technical trajectory:** When AI acquires strategic value, decisions concerning research, manufacturing, infrastructure, technology transfer, and talent development cease to be merely technical decisions and become part of national strategies. Politics begins to intervene in the technological trajectory, while available technical capabilities once again delimit political options. Investments, trade restrictions, standards, and industrial policies can favor certain trajectories, but those trajectories also generate inertia and dependencies that condition subsequent decisions.

* **Territory versus transnational infrastructure:** Data centers, networks, semiconductor factories, and energy and cooling systems are located in concrete territories, but the infrastructures that sustain AI operate through relationships that cross borders. This produces a tension between the material localization of resources and the transnational scale of the networks that articulate them. Territory concentrates costs, resources, energy, and environmental effects, while the benefits and capabilities that depend on those infrastructures can be distributed far beyond the place where they are installed.

These tensions form a feedback circuit: technological capability modifies power relationships; power relationships orient investments, restrictions, and industrial policies; those decisions reorganize infrastructures and supply chains; and the new infrastructures once again modify the technological capabilities available. Sovereignty, openness, and political decisions therefore do not appear as conditions external to technological development, but as relationships that participate in shaping its trajectories.

Geopolitics thus ceases to function merely as the context in which AI unfolds and begins also to participate in shaping the conditions of its development. Material infrastructure ceases to function as a simple support for technology and becomes a component of the power relationships that condition who can develop certain capabilities, under what conditions, and with what degree of dependency.

These transformations propagate into other assemblages:

* **Reorganization of supply chains:** The search for technological autonomy can drive the relocation, diversification, and protection of certain segments of production chains. AI infrastructure can thus contribute to reorganizing industrial territories and commercial relationships that were previously not connected to this technology in the same way.

* **Transformation of industrial policy:** Computing, semiconductors, and other capabilities considered critical can increasingly become integrated into national strategies for investment, subsidies, research, and development. Technology policy can cease to operate merely as support for innovation and begin to intervene in the deliberate construction of strategic capabilities.

* **Reconfiguration of relationships between states and companies:** When a significant part of AI capability is concentrated in companies that control models, platforms, infrastructure, and specialized knowledge, relationships between public power and private actors acquire new forms. The technological capability of certain companies also begins to become a component of the strategic capability of states, generating relationships of cooperation, dependency, and negotiation that cross traditional institutional boundaries.

* **Territorial displacement of material costs:** The concentration of computing in certain territories can shift demands for energy, water, land, and infrastructure toward them, while other territories remain connected to the expansion of AI through mineral extraction, energy production, or labor associated with its supply chains. The spatial distribution of capabilities and their costs thus begins to acquire a new geopolitical dimension.

The node thus connects different territories of the map. Its material dependencies are related to the technological assemblage and to the substrate that sustains computation; its effects on production chains and industrial policy extend toward relationships among technology, economy, and power; and the struggle over technological capabilities establishes a connection with **AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority**, where the issue appears from the capacity to intervene in the mediations through which knowledge is organized and acquires authority.

The inter-stem node is therefore not reduced to competition among states. Its dynamics appear when a technological capability becomes a strategic capability and, in doing so, simultaneously reorganizes the relationships of sovereignty, dependency, infrastructure, and territory that condition its own expansion.

### AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority

At the articulation between knowledge and power, a particularly significant concentration of relationships and tensions appears. AI participates in the mechanisms through which information is organized, ranked, made visible, and presented as valid knowledge. Systems incorporating AI do not merely process information: they can also intervene in its selection, synthesis, and circulation, modifying the conditions under which something can acquire relevance, authority, and legitimacy. In this way, AI does not determine by itself what is valid, but it can intervene in the conditions through which certain pieces of information, interpretations, or responses come to occupy a relevant position and acquire authority.

The issue is not only whether a response is true or false. Before reaching that point, there is a series of operations that condition what information appears, how it is related to other information, what is left out of the response, and from what position it can be interpreted. AI thus begins to participate in a dimension of power that does not consist solely in controlling information, but in intervening in the conditions under which that information can acquire relevance and authority.

Authority is not equivalent to truth either. A statement can acquire authority within a community, institution, or circulation system and nevertheless be false or subsequently challenged. Authority here refers to the position from which a statement can guide the judgment of others, not to a guarantee that the statement is true. Legitimacy introduces another dimension: it refers not only to the capacity to guide judgment, but to the recognition of the position, procedure, or conditions from which that authority is exercised.

This issue differs from the evaluation of knowledge because relevance and authority do not by themselves determine that an output constitutes evidence of knowledge. D concerns the conditions through which information, an interpretation, or a response acquires visibility, relevance, and authority; E, by contrast, concerns the conditions under which an output can function as evidence and allow knowledge to be attributed. An output can acquire relevance and authority without thereby constituting sufficient evidence to attribute knowledge.

Relevance and authority are not equivalent either: something can acquire a relevant position within a circulation without thereby acquiring authority, just as a source can retain authority without necessarily occupying a position of maximum visibility.

The central tension of the node therefore appears between the capacity of AI systems to organize, select, and mediate information and the distribution of power to determine what acquires visibility, relevance, and authority within a field of knowledge. The concentration lies not only in the information produced, but in the conditions that allow certain pieces of information, interpretations, or responses to be found, related, synthesized, legitimized, and taken into consideration.

This transformation can be observed through different tensions:

* **Centralization of relevance:** AI systems can concentrate the determination of what information acquires visibility and relevance in the systems, institutions, and actors that design, train, implement, or control them. The capacity to select and order can cease to be distributed solely among multiple mediators and can shift toward new forms of intermediation.

* **Opacity of mediation:** AI-generated synthesis can make it difficult to reconstruct why certain sources, relationships, or interpretations acquired greater weight than others. The authority of a response can appear separated from the operations through which it was constructed, making the process that sustains its legitimacy less visible.

* **Appearance of neutrality:** Algorithmic mediation can present itself as a technical or neutral operation, even though its results depend on data, design criteria, objectives, constraints, and human and institutional decisions. What appears as automatic selection may contain decisions distributed throughout the entire assemblage.

* **Synthesis and reduction of complexity:** Condensing large amounts of information facilitates access, but it can also displace differences, contradictions, and contexts that were part of the original problem. The clarity of a response can simultaneously produce a loss of complexity that modifies what can be considered relevant.

* **Openness and concentration of epistemic power:** Access to, modification of, or openness of models can expand the ability to intervene in the forms through which knowledge is mediated, but they do not eliminate existing concentrations of computing, data, infrastructure, or specialized knowledge.

* **Auditability and complexity:** As systems, their training processes, and the conditions of their operation become more complex, it may become more difficult to reconstruct the operations that produce particular results. The possibility of auditing the system then becomes part of the dispute over its authority.

* **Reconfiguration of the production and circulation of knowledge:** The incorporation of AI into tasks of search, synthesis, interpretation, and distribution modifies not only the practices through which knowledge is produced, but also the paths through which that knowledge circulates and acquires visibility.

These tensions produce a transformation that can propagate into other spaces. AI can modify the conditions under which information acquires relevance and authority, but those conditions do not remain static in the face of its expansion. Institutions, users, knowledge producers, and other actors begin to adapt their practices to an increasingly present form of mediation, generating new forms of search, validation, circulation, and control. A circuit is thus configured in which AI transforms the conditions of mediation and transformations in those practices feed back into the conditions under which AI participates in them.

This dynamic can propagate in different directions:

* **Transformation of epistemic authority:** When AI responses become privileged points of access to information, the relationship among sources, experts, institutions, and users can change, partially displacing traditional forms through which authority is recognized.

* **Transformation of mediation:** Operations of search, selection, synthesis, and presentation can become concentrated in a single interface, modifying the role of mediators that were previously distributed among different institutions, tools, and actors.

* **Reconfiguration of the circulation of knowledge:** Information begins to travel through circuits in which algorithmic mediation actively participates in its selection, presentation, and distribution, modifying the conditions under which certain contents reach different audiences.

* **Reconfiguration of the space of knowledge:** By making certain relationships between pieces of information visible and leaving others in the background, AI can modify the structure from which a field of knowledge is explored, making some connections more accessible than others.

* **Dispute over cognitive sovereignty:** The capacity to decide what information can be found, which systems are used, and which criteria operate introduces a dimension of sovereignty that exceeds material infrastructure and reaches the conditions of access to and mediation of knowledge.

* **Redistribution of mediation capacity:** The openness, modification, and auditability of systems can expand who has the ability to intervene in those conditions, although that possibility remains limited by the material, economic, and technical concentrations that run through the assemblage.

This node connects directly with **AI + Evaluation of Knowledge**, but from a different position. There, the central question is what allows knowledge to be attributed to a subject when their output may be mediated by AI. Here, the question is how information, an interpretation, or a response acquires relevance and authority within a field of knowledge, and who can intervene in those conditions. It also connects with **AI + Geopolitics and Technological Sovereignty**, although again from another level: there sovereignty relates to the strategic control of material capabilities, infrastructure, and resources; here a cognitive dimension of that sovereignty appears, linked to the capacity to intervene in the conditions of access to, mediation of, and legitimation of knowledge. The dispute over open, auditable, or modifiable models introduces an additional dimension: whoever can intervene in the system can dispute, at least partially, the conditions of that mediation, although that possibility remains shaped by the material, economic, and technical concentrations of the assemblage. AI thus intervenes not only in what circulates, but also in the conditions under which something can acquire relevance and authority.

### AI + Evaluation of Knowledge

The evaluation of knowledge begins with an operation that often remains implicit: inferring something that we cannot observe directly—the knowledge of a subject—from what we can observe, such as their answers, outputs, explanations, or solutions. The incorporation of generative AI introduces an additional mediation into this relationship. An answer can be produced through the interaction between a person and a system, and its correctness does not by itself make it possible to determine which operations belong to the subject, which were performed with assistance, and which capabilities can effectively be attributed to the person presenting the result.

The tension does not consist merely in determining whether an output was produced with AI, but in the fact that the relationship between output, evidence, and attribution of knowledge begins to become less stable. Evaluation seeks to recognize a capacity from an observable manifestation, but when that manifestation emerges from an interaction between subject and system, the result may cease to provide such direct evidence of what is being evaluated. AI thus modifies the conditions under which an output can function as evidence that someone knows something.

This transformation can be observed through different tensions:

* **Result and understanding:** A correct result can coexist with different degrees of understanding on the part of the person presenting it. AI makes the relationship between the quality of an output and the cognitive capacity being evaluated less direct. The result retains its value, but its ability to function as evidence changes when some of the operations that led to it may have been performed through an interaction with AI.

* **Product and process:** When the final output is not sufficient, by itself, to reconstruct how it was reached, evaluation may shift toward the process: what decisions the subject made, what they can explain, defend, modify, or reconstruct, and how they intervened in the result. Evaluation thus begins to seek new ways of making visible what the final product no longer allows us to infer by itself. The process ceases to be merely the path toward the result and also begins to become part of the evidence.

* **Knowing and demonstrating that one knows:** AI makes more visible a separation that evaluation tends to keep relatively unified: possessing a capability and producing a demonstration of that capability. Using a tool does not necessarily imply an absence of knowledge, but neither does it allow us to assume that the knowledge demonstrated in the result exists independently of it. The question becomes what relationship with the tool allows a capability to be attributed to the subject and when that relationship transforms what evaluation intended to recognize.

* **Evidence and traceability:** When an output may result from an interaction between a subject and AI, evaluation may attempt to reconstruct which operations correspond to each participant. Explaining, defending, modifying, or reconstructing an answer then acquires value as evidence. But this search for traceability is not neutral: by attempting to recover the attribution of knowledge, it also modifies the conditions under which AI can intervene in the production and demonstration being evaluated.

These tensions produce a transformation that can propagate into other spaces. AI modifies what can function as evidence of knowledge, but evaluation practices do not simply wait for that modification. By changing their ways of observing, demonstrating, and attributing capabilities, they begin to establish new conditions for AI participation. A feedback circuit is thus configured in which AI transforms the conditions of evaluation and transformations in evaluation feed back into the conditions under which AI can participate in the production and demonstration of knowledge.

This dynamic can propagate in different directions:

* **Reorganization of evaluation practices:** The ways of observing, demonstrating, and reconstructing knowledge change when an output may be mediated by AI.

* **Transformation of the criteria of knowledge:** When the result is no longer sufficient by itself, greater weight may be given to understanding, explanation, decision-making, modification, and the ability to reconstruct the process.

* **Redistribution of evaluative authority:** As the conditions of evidence change, so does who can intervene in the attribution of knowledge and according to which criteria.

* **Transformation of valued capabilities:** When the conditions under which someone is recognized as knowing something change, what capabilities are considered relevant for learning, working, and acting with AI also changes.

The node connects with **AI + Philosophy of Mind**, but through a different problem: there, the question is what it means to understand when a system produces behaviors associated with understanding; here, what allows us to recognize that a subject understands when their output may be mediated by AI. It also connects with **AI + Power, Relevance, and Authority**, because every evaluation presupposes criteria that determine what can function as evidence and who has the authority to establish them.

AI does not eliminate the need to evaluate knowledge; it modifies the conditions under which an output can function as evidence that someone knows something. When the relationship between result and capacity ceases to be relatively direct, evaluation means determining what can be attributed from that output and under what conditions.
