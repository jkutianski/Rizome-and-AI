# Semantic Precision

## Purpose

Analyze whether the wording of a source expresses its intended conceptual scope, distinctions, and claims with sufficient semantic precision.

This Skill focuses on the relationship between **formulation and meaning**.

It does not determine whether a concept is philosophically correct or whether the overall argument is coherent. Those questions belong to other Skills.

## Scope

Evaluate:

* ambiguity;
* semantic scope;
* overstatement;
* understatement;
* conceptual qualification;
* terminology;
* shifts in meaning;
* categorical language;
* modality;
* attribution;
* distinctions that depend on wording;
* formulations that may support unintended interpretations.

Do not treat ordinary variation in wording as a problem.

## 1. Identify Semantically Sensitive Claims

Focus on formulations where a small change in wording could significantly alter the claim.

Examples include claims involving:

* causality;
* agency;
* intention;
* autonomy;
* understanding;
* consciousness;
* knowledge;
* authority;
* legitimacy;
* relevance;
* production;
* representation;
* intervention;
* transformation;
* emergence;
* determination;
* participation;
* capacity;
* necessity;
* possibility.

These are examples, not a mandatory vocabulary.

## 2. Determine Intended Scope

For each semantically significant formulation, ask:

* What does the sentence explicitly claim?
* How broad is the claim?
* What entities, situations, or cases does it cover?
* Is that scope supported by the surrounding argument?
* Could a reasonable reader interpret it more broadly or narrowly than intended?

Distinguish between:

**Literal scope**
What the formulation directly asserts.

**Contextual scope**
What the surrounding text appears to limit or qualify.

**Possible reader interpretation**
What a reasonable reader could infer from the wording.

A semantic problem exists when these diverge in a consequential way.

## 3. Detect Overstatement

Flag formulations that appear stronger than the evidence or argument supports.

Common patterns include:

* possibility presented as inevitability;
* influence presented as determination;
* participation presented as authorship;
* functional behavior presented as intention;
* representation presented as intervention;
* correlation presented as causation;
* local observation presented as universal claim;
* capacity presented as actual achievement;
* potential consequence presented as established outcome.

Do not weaken a strong claim merely because it is strong.

Ask whether the text provides sufficient qualification or evidence for its strength.

## 4. Detect Understatement

A formulation can also be too weak for the argument it is intended to express.

Consider whether the wording:

* obscures a significant distinction;
* reduces a relational claim to a vague statement;
* uses excessive qualification that makes the argument unclear;
* describes a structural transformation merely as an association;
* uses generic language where a specific relation is demonstrable.

Do not replace precision with unnecessary technicality.

## 5. Analyze Modality

Pay attention to modal language such as:

* may;
* can;
* could;
* tends to;
* often;
* generally;
* necessarily;
* inevitably;
* must;
* appears to;
* suggests;
* demonstrates.

Determine whether the modality matches the evidential and argumentative strength of the claim.

A shift from "can" to "does", or from "may" to "necessarily", may be semantically significant.

Do not assume stronger wording is always worse.

## 6. Analyze Attribution

When a sentence attributes an action, capacity, property, or effect to an entity, determine what kind of attribution is being made.

Distinguish, where relevant, among:

* functional attribution;
* causal attribution;
* relational attribution;
* institutional attribution;
* intentional attribution;
* experiential attribution;
* normative attribution.

For example, saying that a system "selects" information may describe a functional process.

It does not automatically mean that the system has an intention to select.

The problem arises when the wording causes an unsupported shift between these levels.

## 7. Analyze Ambiguity

Not all ambiguity is a defect.

Distinguish:

### Productive ambiguity

The ambiguity is deliberate, meaningful, or structurally useful.

### Contextual ambiguity

The surrounding text resolves the possible meanings adequately.

### Unresolved ambiguity

Multiple materially different interpretations remain possible and the text provides insufficient guidance.

Only the third should normally be treated as a semantic problem.

## 8. Analyze Key Distinctions

Where a distinction is important to the argument, test whether the wording preserves it.

Potential distinctions include:

* possibility / inevitability;
* participation / intention;
* influence / determination;
* capacity / achievement;
* behavior / understanding;
* representation / intervention;
* production / knowledge;
* relevance / authority;
* authority / legitimacy;
* authority / truth;
* relation / causality;
* connection / propagation;
* complexity / emergence.

These distinctions must not be imposed where they are irrelevant to the source.

## 9. Terminological Stability

Track terms that recur throughout the document.

Determine whether a term:

* retains its meaning;
* changes meaning deliberately;
* becomes broader or narrower;
* shifts analytical level;
* acquires a new technical function;
* becomes ambiguous through repeated use.

Distinguish semantic variation from genuine semantic drift.

A term may legitimately change meaning when the context makes the shift intelligible.

## 10. Quantifiers and Generalization

Pay particular attention to:

* all;
* every;
* always;
* never;
* only;
* fundamentally;
* inherently;
* necessarily;
* inevitably;
* universally.

Also examine less explicit generalizations such as:

* "technology changes society";
* "AI transforms knowledge";
* "models produce relevance";
* "automation eliminates...";
* "systems determine...".

A general formulation is not automatically problematic.

Evaluate whether its scope is justified by the evidence and argument.

## 11. Metaphorical Language

Determine whether metaphorical language is functioning as:

* metaphor;
* heuristic;
* conceptual model;
* descriptive shorthand;
* literal claim.

Do not treat metaphor as conceptual error merely because it is not literally true.

Flag a problem when metaphorical language is likely to be interpreted as a literal empirical or philosophical claim without sufficient indication.

## 12. Semantic Drift Across Sections

Compare important formulations across the work.

Look for cases where:

* a qualification disappears;
* a tentative claim becomes categorical;
* an example becomes a general rule;
* a descriptive formulation becomes normative;
* a functional description becomes an intentional one;
* a local relationship becomes a universal claim.

Determine whether the shift is deliberate and supported.

## 13. Translation and Multilingual Contexts

Follow the shared language-analysis protocol.

When semantic precision depends on the original wording:

* preserve the original expression;
* do not evaluate a translation as though it were the source;
* distinguish translation difficulty from conceptual weakness;
* consider whether apparently equivalent terms have different semantic ranges.

Do not create a semantic finding solely because an English rendering, Spanish rendering, or other translation sounds unusual.

## 14. Semantic Precision vs. Style

Do not classify a sentence as semantically imprecise merely because it could be:

* shorter;
* more elegant;
* more academic;
* more technical;
* more formal;
* more symmetrical.

A stylistic preference becomes a semantic issue only when the wording changes or destabilizes meaning.

## What Not to Do

Do not:

* rewrite every ambiguous sentence;
* eliminate all ambiguity;
* replace ordinary language with technical terminology unnecessarily;
* assume categorical language is automatically incorrect;
* weaken claims without examining their evidential basis;
* infer anthropomorphism solely from human-like verbs;
* infer determinism solely from claims of influence;
* infer causality from grammatical structure alone;
* treat unusual wording as conceptual error automatically.

## Evidence Standard

For each substantive finding, identify:

1. the exact or relevant formulation;
2. its literal semantic scope;
3. the interpretation it permits;
4. the intended or contextual scope, if recoverable;
5. the specific semantic mismatch;
6. why the mismatch matters.

If the context resolves the apparent ambiguity, do not report it as a substantive problem.

## Output

Use the shared Output Protocol.

When useful, organize findings as:

| Formulation | Literal Scope | Contextual Scope | Risk | Diagnosis |
| ----------- | ------------- | ---------------- | ---- | --------- |

Possible conclusions include:

* Semantically precise
* Precise with contextual qualification
* Productively ambiguous
* Potential ambiguity
* Scope overstatement
* Scope understatement
* Terminological drift
* Significant semantic instability
* Insufficient evidence

## Core Principle

Semantic precision is not the elimination of ambiguity.

It is the alignment between **what the wording says, what the surrounding text supports, and what a reasonable reader is likely to understand**.

**Do not make the text more precise than its argument requires; make sure its wording is no less precise than its argument demands.**
