# Knowledge, Evidence, Production, and Attribution

## Purpose

Analyze how a text relates:

* production;
* information;
* knowledge;
* evidence;
* understanding;
* attribution;
* validation;
* authority;
* verification.

This Skill is designed primarily for the conceptual territory represented by **Node E: AI + Knowledge Evaluation**.

Its central transformation is:

**production → evidence → attribution**

The purpose is not to decide whether AI "produces knowledge" in the abstract.

The purpose is to reconstruct the conditions under which produced outputs become:

* candidates for knowledge;
* objects of evaluation;
* evidentially supported or unsupported;
* attributed to sources or actors;
* accepted, rejected, or qualified.

---

# 1. Governing Principle

Do not collapse:

**production = knowledge**

**production = evidence**

**evidence = knowledge**

**authority = truth**

**fluency = understanding**

**functional performance = understanding**

The central analytical problem is the transition from something being **produced** to something being **treated as evidentially relevant and attributable**.

---

# 2. Central Chain

A useful heuristic is:

**production → evaluation → evidence → attribution → possible knowledge status**

This is not a rigid causal sequence.

Different contexts may:

* omit stages;
* reverse the order;
* combine stages;
* introduce feedback;
* contest attribution;
* reject evidence;
* treat outputs provisionally.

The specific text determines which relations actually exist.

---

# 3. Production

Production concerns the generation or transformation of:

* text;
* images;
* hypotheses;
* classifications;
* predictions;
* models;
* summaries;
* recommendations;
* datasets;
* interpretations;
* experimental designs;
* technical outputs.

Ask:

> **What is actually being produced?**

Do not use "production" as a synonym for knowledge.

---

# 4. Knowledge

Knowledge is a stronger epistemic category.

If the text claims that something constitutes knowledge, ask:

* What criteria are being used?
* What process validates it?
* What evidence supports it?
* Who evaluates it?
* What standards apply?
* Is the claim provisional?
* Is knowledge being used descriptively or normatively?

Do not impose a single external theory of knowledge unless the task explicitly requires it.

Analyze the epistemic standard actually operating in the text.

---

# 5. Evidence

Evidence is not simply information.

Evidence functions as support for a claim under some epistemic or methodological standard.

Ask:

* Evidence for what?
* According to which standard?
* How is the evidence obtained?
* How is it evaluated?
* Can it be independently checked?
* What would count against the claim?

A generated output can contain information without thereby constituting evidence.

---

# 6. Information

Information is broader than evidence.

Something can be:

* informative;
* relevant;
* novel;
* useful;

without being evidentially sufficient.

Preserve:

**information ≠ evidence**

and:

**relevance ≠ evidence.**

---

# 7. Verification

Verification concerns processes through which claims or outputs are checked.

Possible mechanisms include:

* observation;
* experiment;
* replication;
* comparison;
* source checking;
* formal proof;
* expert review;
* independent testing;
* cross-validation;
* documentary evidence.

The appropriate mechanism depends on the claim type.

Do not require scientific verification for every philosophical proposition.

---

# 8. Attribution

Attribution concerns who or what receives epistemic, intellectual, causal, or practical credit for an output.

Ask:

* Who generated the output?
* Who supplied the underlying data?
* Who formulated the question?
* Who selected the method?
* Who interpreted the result?
* Who verified it?
* Who is credited?
* Who is responsible?

Attribution can be distributed.

Do not assume that the entity producing an output automatically owns the epistemic achievement.

---

# 9. Attribution and Responsibility

Responsibility and causal contribution must remain distinct.

An AI system may causally contribute to:

* a text;
* an analysis;
* a recommendation;
* a classification.

That does not by itself establish:

* authorship;
* responsibility;
* intentional agency;
* understanding.

Coordinate with:

`ai-writing-reflexivity`

and:

`anthropomorphism-audit`.

---

# 10. AI-Generated Output

When evaluating AI output, distinguish:

1. generation;
2. retrieval or transformation;
3. evidential support;
4. verification;
5. interpretation;
6. attribution;
7. institutional uptake.

An output may be:

* useful but unsupported;
* correct but insufficiently verified;
* evidentially supported but misattributed;
* authoritative but poorly evidenced;
* wrong despite appearing coherent.

---

# 11. Fluency

Fluency can influence perception.

A fluent output may appear:

* knowledgeable;
* coherent;
* confident;
* authoritative.

But:

**fluency ≠ knowledge**

**fluency ≠ evidence**

**fluency ≠ understanding**

Analyze whether the text mistakes stylistic or functional properties for epistemic status.

---

# 12. Functional Success

An AI system may perform a task successfully without establishing that it:

* understands the task;
* possesses the relevant concepts;
* has conscious knowledge;
* has intentional beliefs.

Therefore distinguish:

**functional performance**

from:

**epistemic possession**

and:

**mental understanding.**

Coordinate with:

`anthropomorphism-audit`

and:

`philosophical-claims`.

---

# 13. Prediction

Prediction deserves special treatment.

A successful prediction can provide evidence under certain conditions.

But:

**prediction ≠ explanation**

and:

**prediction ≠ knowledge automatically.**

Ask:

* What was predicted?
* Under what conditions?
* How was success measured?
* Is the result reproducible?
* What mechanism, if any, is claimed?
* What epistemic status is attributed to the prediction?

---

# 14. Classification

AI classifications can produce useful outputs without necessarily revealing natural categories.

Analyze:

* classification criteria;
* training data;
* labels;
* thresholds;
* evaluation procedures;
* error rates;
* institutional use.

Do not assume:

**successful classification = discovery of an ontologically natural category.**

Coordinate with:

`map-territory`.

---

# 15. Generation and Evidence

A generated statement is not evidence merely because a model produced it.

Ask:

> **What makes the output evidentially relevant, if anything?**

Possible answers may include:

* independently verified data;
* cited sources;
* reproducible computation;
* experimental confirmation;
* external observation;
* methodological validation.

The source of generation and the basis of evidence are distinct questions.

---

# 16. Data and Evidence

Data are not automatically evidence.

Ask:

* What was measured?
* How?
* Under what conditions?
* What preprocessing occurred?
* What selection occurred?
* What assumptions structure the dataset?
* What inference connects data to claim?

Preserve:

**data → interpretation → evidential function**

rather than:

**data = truth.**

---

# 17. Training Data

Training data may influence model behavior.

But the presence of information in training data does not automatically mean the model:

* knows it;
* understands it;
* preserves it accurately;
* can justify it;
* can identify its source.

Analyze the epistemic role of training data separately from the epistemic status of generated output.

---

# 18. Source Attribution

Generated outputs may obscure or complicate source attribution.

Analyze:

* provenance;
* citation;
* transformation;
* synthesis;
* omission;
* traceability;
* authorship.

Ask:

> **Can the origin of an important claim be reconstructed?**

Do not assume that lack of immediate citation means the output is false.

It may instead create an attribution or verification problem.

---

# 19. Knowledge Evaluation

The key question is not:

> "Does AI produce knowledge?"

It is:

> **"What happens to the conditions under which outputs are evaluated as knowledge when AI participates in their production?"**

This shift keeps the analysis relational.

It allows examination of:

* changed evaluation standards;
* increased output volume;
* altered verification burdens;
* new attribution problems;
* new forms of epistemic dependence;
* changes in expert roles;
* altered thresholds of trust.

---

# 20. Evaluation Burden

Generative AI can alter the relationship between production and evaluation.

If production becomes easier or faster, evaluation may become comparatively more important.

Analyze whether the text identifies:

* verification bottlenecks;
* increased review burden;
* changed expertise requirements;
* new filtering requirements;
* attribution challenges.

Do not assume increased production automatically causes epistemic decline.

---

# 21. Verification Bottlenecks

A transformation in production may create a transformation in evaluation.

For example:

**lower production cost → more outputs → greater evaluation burden → stronger need for filtering or verification**

This is a possible relational pattern, not a universal law.

Look for evidence before asserting it.

---

# 22. Epistemic Dependence

AI systems can create or alter dependencies on:

* models;
* platforms;
* datasets;
* benchmarks;
* APIs;
* infrastructures;
* experts;
* proprietary systems.

Ask:

> **What epistemic capacity becomes dependent on which infrastructure or actor?**

This may connect Node E with Node C or D.

Do not collapse the nodes.

---

# 23. Node E and Node D

Node E:

**production → evidence → attribution**

Node D:

**mediation → selection → visibility → relevance → authority → power**

They interact but perform different analytical operations.

E asks:

> What supports the claim, and how is it evaluated and attributed?

D asks:

> How does information become visible, relevant, authoritative, and consequential?

A claim can therefore:

* have strong evidence but low visibility;
* have high visibility but weak evidence;
* have strong evidence but weak authority;
* acquire authority despite weak evidence.

These differences are analytically important.

---

# 24. Node E and Node A

Node A concerns:

**observable capacities → attribution of mental capacities**

Node E concerns:

**production → evidence → attribution**

Do not infer mental understanding from epistemic usefulness.

An AI can produce an output that is epistemically useful without thereby possessing understanding.

---

# 25. Node E and Node B

Node B concerns:

**representation → intervention**

AI may generate predictions or models used in biological intervention.

Node E asks how those outputs become:

* evidentially supported;
* validated;
* attributed;
* incorporated into knowledge practices.

Do not collapse intervention with epistemic validation.

---

# 26. Node E and Node C

Node C concerns:

**technical capacity → strategic capacity**

Node E may intersect with C where knowledge infrastructures become strategic resources.

But epistemic dependence should not automatically be interpreted as geopolitical power.

Identify the additional relations.

---

# 27. Authority and Evidence

Authority and evidence must remain distinct.

A source can be authoritative because of:

* institutional status;
* professional role;
* expertise;
* procedural recognition.

Evidence provides support for a claim.

Therefore:

**authority ≠ evidence**

and:

**evidence ≠ authority.**

Coordinate with:

`power-mediation-authority`.

---

# 28. Truth and Evidence

Evidence supports claims under particular standards.

Evidence is not identical to truth.

A claim may have:

* insufficient evidence;
* conflicting evidence;
* strong evidence;
* provisional evidence.

Do not convert evidential support into absolute certainty unless the relevant methodology justifies it.

---

# 29. Epistemic Status

When the text uses terms such as:

* knowledge;
* fact;
* truth;
* evidence;
* certainty;
* reliability;
* validity;
* credibility;

identify the exact epistemic function.

Ask:

* Is the term descriptive?
* Normative?
* Methodological?
* Rhetorical?
* Absolute?
* Provisional?

Coordinate with:

`semantic-precision`.

---

# 30. Epistemic Standards

Different claims require different evaluation standards.

Possible standards include:

* empirical;
* experimental;
* logical;
* mathematical;
* historical;
* documentary;
* interpretive;
* institutional;
* procedural.

Do not apply one standard indiscriminately.

The question is whether the standard fits the claim.

---

# 31. Counterfactual Tests

### Remove Generation

Would the epistemic issue remain?

If yes, the issue may concern general knowledge evaluation rather than AI specifically.

### Remove Verification

Would the output still count as knowledge under the text's own standards?

If no, verification is constitutive.

### Remove Attribution

Would the epistemic status change?

If yes, attribution is part of the transformation.

### Replace AI with Human Production

Does the epistemic problem disappear?

If no, the issue may be a general epistemic problem.

If yes, identify what AI changes specifically.

### Remove Institutional Uptake

Would the output still acquire epistemic authority?

If no, institutional embedding is relevant.

---

# 32. Common Conceptual Errors

Flag when the text assumes:

* production = knowledge;
* generation = evidence;
* data = truth;
* prediction = explanation;
* prediction = knowledge;
* fluency = understanding;
* coherence = truth;
* citation = truth;
* authority = evidence;
* relevance = evidence;
* visibility = truth;
* classification = natural ontology;
* functional success = mental understanding;
* causal contribution = authorship;
* output generation = responsibility.

Each requires separate analysis.

---

# 33. Evidence and Uncertainty

Epistemic uncertainty is not necessarily weakness.

A strong analysis may explicitly distinguish:

* established;
* well-supported;
* plausible;
* uncertain;
* contested;
* unsupported.

Do not eliminate uncertainty merely to produce a cleaner argument.

---

# 34. Production–Evaluation Feedback

Look for feedback loops.

Possible pattern:

**more efficient production → more outputs → altered evaluation burden → new evaluation tools → changed production practices**

This can become recursive.

The relevant question is:

> **Does the evaluation system feed back into subsequent production conditions?**

If yes, analyze it as a relational feedback process.

---

# 35. Attribution Feedback

Attribution can also become recursive.

For example:

**AI-assisted production → attribution uncertainty → new attribution norms → altered AI use → changed production practices**

Again, this is a possible pattern, not a universal sequence.

---

# 36. Institutional Embedding

Knowledge evaluation often occurs within institutions.

Analyze:

* scientific communities;
* universities;
* laboratories;
* journals;
* professional organizations;
* governments;
* companies;
* regulatory bodies.

Ask:

> **Who decides whether an AI-mediated output counts as acceptable evidence or knowledge?**

The answer may be distributed across institutions rather than located in the AI system.

---

# 37. Materiality

Knowledge production and evaluation depend on infrastructures.

Relevant conditions may include:

* compute;
* storage;
* networks;
* data;
* laboratories;
* instruments;
* energy;
* human labor;
* platforms.

Do not add materiality automatically.

Include it when it changes the conditions of production or evaluation.

---

# 38. Power

Epistemic evaluation can involve power.

Analyze:

* who sets standards;
* who controls access;
* who controls infrastructure;
* whose evidence is recognized;
* who can contest evaluations;
* whose outputs become visible;
* who bears verification costs.

Do not reduce every epistemic disagreement to power.

Power is one relational dimension among others.

---

# 39. Contingency

Knowledge standards and attribution practices can change.

Consider:

* institutional reform;
* technological change;
* new verification practices;
* regulation;
* professional norms;
* competing infrastructures;
* changes in access.

Avoid treating current epistemic arrangements as inevitable.

---

# 40. Inter-Stem Potential

Node E may become an inter-stem node when changes in AI production alter the conditions of another assemblage's knowledge evaluation.

For example:

**AI-generated output → increased production volume → altered verification burden → changed evaluation practices → new attribution norms**

The decisive test is whether this transformation crosses assemblage boundaries and modifies conditions of functioning.

Use:

`inter-stem-validation`.

---

# 41. Cartographic Implications

Knowledge categories are themselves potentially cartographic.

Ask whether the text treats:

* knowledge;
* evidence;
* authority;
* relevance;

as fixed natural categories or as categories whose boundaries are partly produced by practices and institutions.

Do not conclude that they are arbitrary.

The aim is to make the conditions of categorization visible.

Coordinate with:

`map-territory`.

---

# 42. Philosophical Claims

Claims such as:

* "AI understands";
* "AI knows";
* "AI produces knowledge";
* "AI discovers";
* "AI reasons";

require philosophical analysis beyond functional description.

Ask:

* What does the predicate mean?
* What evidence supports it?
* Is it functional or mental?
* Does the claim require consciousness?
* Is attribution metaphorical or literal?

Coordinate with:

`philosophical-claims`.

---

# 44. Final Validation Test

Before making a significant claim about AI and knowledge evaluation, ask:

1. What is being produced?
2. What makes it informative?
3. What makes it evidential, if anything?
4. What standards evaluate it?
5. How is it verified?
6. Who or what is attributed with the result?
7. Who accepts or rejects it?
8. Is authority involved?
9. Is truth being claimed?
10. What institutions participate?
11. What infrastructures support the process?
12. What power relations affect evaluation?
13. Is there feedback between production and evaluation?
14. What makes the arrangement contingent?
15. Does AI change the conditions of knowledge evaluation specifically?
16. Is the issue actually about mental understanding rather than epistemic evaluation?
17. Is the issue actually about authority and relevance rather than evidence?
18. Is the issue actually about biological intervention, geopolitics, or another node?

If these distinctions cannot be maintained, the analysis risks collapsing production, knowledge, evidence, attribution, authority, and truth into one category.

---

# 45. Final Principle

> **Production is not knowledge. Generation is not evidence. Evidence is not authority. Authority is not truth.**

> **The important question is not simply whether AI produces knowledge, but what happens to the conditions under which produced outputs are evaluated, verified, attributed, and accepted as knowledge.**

> **An AI-generated output can be useful without being evidence, correct without being verified, authoritative without being well supported, or fluent without being understood.**

> **The epistemic problem begins where production encounters evaluation.**

> **The strongest analysis therefore follows the transformation from production to evidence to attribution without collapsing the distinctions that make those transitions analytically meaningful.**
